# The Architect's Pocket Guide to Portfolio Design

**Zuzana Kubišová, 2025** (Routledge, Abingdon/New York; the CIP record is from 2024). The author teaches at the College of Architecture & Environmental Design, Kent State University, and works at J. KURTZ Architects (Cleveland). The pages cited are the book's printed pages (in the PDF file, page = printed page + 7).

## At a glance

- **What it is about:** a visual guide, in ten steps, to the architecture portfolio: purpose and audience, curation and pacing, medium and format (print, PDF, web), layout (grid, margins, white space), visual elements (proximity, alignment, scale, color, hierarchy, balance, similarity), typography, content (cover, resume, design statement, table of contents, project pages, closing), annotated case studies and the final review (feedback, cover letter, sharing). Each step has "DO / DON'T" recommendations and a section of common mistakes with examples.
- **Central thesis:** the portfolio is itself a design project (a statement of your identity), and its quality comes from curation, structure and layout principles applied with restraint, so that the work speaks, not the decoration.
- **For whom and when:** art direction and design students, in the portfolio course; anyone preparing a printed or PDF portfolio with many images.
- **Relevance for ADC:** ★★★☆☆: written for architects, but the principles of curation (YES / NO / MAYBE), pacing (four ordering methods), portfolio types (sample, full, leave-behind), the project page, the resume and the cover letter transfer directly. The chapters on layout and typography are solid but generic (also covered by the ART skill), and those on printing (DPI, CMYK, binding) are useful only for a physical portfolio.

## What is missing / what to read critically

- Centered on architecture (models, technical drawings, the academic studio); the portfolio examples are almost exclusively by architects.
- The numerical rules (5–15 pages for a sample, up to 50 for a full portfolio, 6–8 projects, 300 DPI, 200–500 KB per web image) are a practitioner's recommendations, not research findings.
- Little about story and results: the emphasis is on visual presentation and process, not on the business impact of the projects (unlike Fenn, SRC-048).
- Some claims about visual perception ("the right page is typically more dominant", "warmer colors are heavier") are design heuristics, presented without sources.
- The images (half the book) are missing from the extracted text; the comments on the case studies can be understood only in part.
