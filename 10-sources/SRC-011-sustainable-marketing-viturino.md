# Sustainable Marketing: Responsible Practices for the Future of Marketing

**Maria Cesarini Viturino, 2024** (the year comes from the catalog; the bibliography has references up to 2024). No publisher is named; probably self-published. The text looks machine-translated from Portuguese: "Campanha", "Stakeholders Internos", "Real Beleza", "virgin cousins" instead of "virgin raw materials", "Where:" instead of "Dove:". About 23,000 words.

## At a glance

- **What it is about:** an introductory handbook in five chapters: the fundamentals of sustainable marketing and its link with CSR; integrating sustainability into marketing strategy; green and eco-efficient marketing (product design, production, packaging, communication, circular economy); CSR and cause marketing; impact measurement (carbon footprint, resources, satisfaction, reputation, audits, KPIs).
- **Central thesis:** sustainable marketing balances economic growth with social and environmental responsibility and brings reputation, loyalty, innovation and competitive advantage, provided there is transparency and measurement.
- **For whom and when:** first-year students, as vocabulary and a map of the notions (CSR, cause marketing, eco-efficiency, scope 1-2-3, materiality). For strategists it is too uniformly positive.
- **Relevance for ADC:** ★★☆☆☆. Clear structure, definitions with academic references (Belz & Peattie, Kotler & Lee, Ottman) and useful checklists (circular design and packaging, indicators, materiality). The cases (Patagonia, Unilever, Tesla, IKEA, Interface, Ben & Jerry's, TOMS), however, are presented as praise, with no critical perspective at all.

## What is missing / what to read critically

- **Greenwashing is not discussed at all in the text**, although the bibliography cites *The Drivers of Greenwashing* (Delmas & Burbano, 2011) and the TerraChoice report *The Sins of Greenwashing* (2010). This is the biggest gap for an advertising school.
- **Uncritical cases:** the "by 2020" goals (Interface's Mission Zero, an energy-independent IKEA) are presented without saying whether they were met. The TOMS "One for One" model is praised without the well-known criticism. Tesla and Coca-Cola appear only as positive examples.
- **A worrying claim:** solid CSR would help the company "resist negative criticism". This treats responsibility as reputational armor.
- **Errors of detail:** GRI is listed among the reputation rankings, although it is a reporting standard; there are translation artifacts.
- **Repetition:** the same benefits (reputation, loyalty, advantage) come back in every chapter. A discussion of the gap between consumers' stated intentions and their actual behavior is missing.
- There is nothing on creative execution: how to communicate sustainability without visual clichés (leaves, green) or a moralizing tone.
