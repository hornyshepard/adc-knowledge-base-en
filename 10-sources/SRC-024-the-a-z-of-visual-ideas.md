# The A–Z of Visual Ideas: How to Solve Any Creative Brief

**John Ingledew, 2011.** Laurence King Publishing, London (ISBN 978-1-85669-714-9). The book was written with the support of a research grant from the University of Gloucestershire; many of its photographs are the author's.

## At a glance

- **What it is about:** an illustrated dictionary of sources and techniques for visual ideas, from "Art" to "Zeitgeist" (about 70 entries). Each entry has examples (posters, ads, covers, art), a "DIY" section (how to use the technique) and a "Brainjack" section (why it works in the viewer's mind). Before the dictionary: an introduction about ideas, the 16-stage process for solving a brief and the chapter "Working on ideas".
- **Central thesis:** successful communication "brainjacks" the viewer; an idea is the transformation of an inspiration into something new, and the sources and mechanisms of this transformation can be learned and practiced.
- **For whom and when:** art direction and copywriting students, in the first months; as a set of exercises (pick an entry at random and solve the brief with it).
- **Relevance for ADC:** ★★★★☆ (4). Very useful as a repertoire of techniques and for "visual culture" (Abram Games, Fukuda, johnson banks, BBH, Saatchi). Limits: the book depends on images (only the captions remain in the extracted text), it is centered on the UK, and it predates social media.

## What is missing / what to read critically

- Many quotes come from little-known people ("Schway Whar, designer", "Rose Tang, artist"), thanked at the end among the contributors, probably the author's students and former students; "Alan Smithee", however, is a famous pseudonym from film. Treat these quotes as illustrations, not as authorities.
- A few claims are unsupported or pseudo-scientific: "the collective telepathic intelligence", ideas that "solidify" more readily when you lie down.
- Nothing on measured effectiveness: the techniques are judged by their visual impact, not by results. For the link with strategy, see [[SRC-022-the-creative-brief-blueprint|SRC-022]].
- The examples date from 1960–2010; social media, mobile and AI are missing. Some of the shock or humor examples would fail a responsibility check today (see [[STO-040-the-responsibility-checklist-for-representation-stereotypes|STO-040]]).
