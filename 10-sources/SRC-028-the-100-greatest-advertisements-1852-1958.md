# The 100 Greatest Advertisements 1852–1958: Who Wrote Them and What They Did

**Julian Lewis Watkins, 1959** (second edition, revised and enlarged, Dover; first edition: Moore Publishing, 1949; reprinted by Dover in 1993 under the current title). Foreword by Raymond Rubicam. Watkins worked for 15 years at N. W. Ayer, Young & Rubicam and J. Walter Thompson, then for 16 years as a partner at the H. B. Humphrey Company (Boston). In 1959 he was working at Leo Burnett.

> EPUB with no pagination: the derived notes cite the **entry** (the name of the ad / advertiser, as in the table of contents). The text does not contain the images of the ads, only part of the copy and the author's comments.

## At a glance

- **What it is about:** an anthology of about 113 American ads and campaigns (1852–1958), each with a short commentary and, often, the behind-the-scenes story told by the writer himself: Caples, Rubicam, James Webb Young, Ogilvy, Burnett, Getchell, Sackheim, Eichler and others. It covers long and short copy, mail order with a coupon, institutional ads, brand characters, slogans, launches and wartime advertising.
- **Central thesis:** great ads differ enormously in style, but they have in common an idea that cannot be buried and sincerity; they are proven by the public and by time, not by a rule. Good ideas are everywhere, but they have to be recognized.
- **For whom and when:** copywriters and art directors at the start (years 1–2), as a "library of examples", exactly as Rubicam recommends it: writers learn by absorbing the work of others. Useful in seminars analyzing classic ads and in discussions about the creative process.
- **Relevance for ADC:** ★★★★☆ (4/5). A rare primary source: the writers of great ads describe their own process (Young and his method, Rubicam and Squibb, Getchell and Plymouth, Ogilvy and the Rolls-Royce brief, Burnett and Marlboro). Limits: enthusiastic, uncritical, centered on the US, with no comparable effectiveness data; attitudes of the period (racial stereotypes treated as folklore, pleas for "free enterprise"); the images are missing from the text.

## What is missing / what to read critically

- An openly subjective selection; "greatness" is proven anecdotally (letters, requests for reprints, citations), rarely with comparable sales data. Many figures come from the advertisers or agencies themselves.
- An exclusively American, trade perspective: ads are presented as the last strong line of free enterprise; the tone is celebratory.
- Uncriticized attitudes of the period: the Cream of Wheat chef and the "negro dialect" are treated as charming folklore; cigarette ads are praised without reservation; fear appeals (Listerine, Odorono) are admired for their sales.
- The EPUB text does not reproduce the images, and many entries are only a paragraph of commentary; for visual analysis you need to consult the original reproductions.
- It stops in 1958, right at the start of the creative revolution; for what followed, see [[SRC-027-a-history-of-advertising|SRC-027]].
