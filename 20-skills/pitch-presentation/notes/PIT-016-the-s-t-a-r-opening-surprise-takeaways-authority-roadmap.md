# The S.T.A.R. opening: Surprise, Takeaways, Authority, Roadmap

> **The idea in one sentence:** the audience has to know you have begun; a good opening surprises, promises the benefit, establishes credibility and only at the end presents the agenda.

## Explanation

Colenso cites research from the University of York: people judge our trustworthiness, status and attractiveness in as little as 33 milliseconds. Most presentations lose the start: apologies, checking whether the sound works, the fire exits, the agenda slide read aloud. If the audience does not notice that you have begun, the fault lies with the speaker. The S.T.A.R. order is deliberate:

| Step | What you do | Details |
|---|---|---|
| **S**urprise | you work against expectations | a short story (~60 sec) clearly linked to the topic, a rhetorical question, a short quote, a figure, a fact ("Did you know…?"), an image or a video |
| **T**akeaways | you answer "what's in it for me?" | for example, that over the next 25 minutes you will show them how to cut their recruitment costs in half; the audience can check early whether they are in the right room |
| **A**uthority | only now do you introduce yourself | why you are the right person, humbly but without selling yourself short; steer clear of job titles and say how your experience is relevant to them; you can "borrow" credibility |
| **R**oadmap | the agenda, the timings, "the rules of the game" | how long each part lasts, when questions are taken; in pitches involving procurement, you confirm they have budgeted the same amount of time as you |

The opening takes about 20% of the time and scales: 12 minutes for an hour-long speech, or a couple of sentences per element for a ten-minute update. The author also uses it as an anchor against nerves: the first minutes are the most tense, and a well-rehearsed opening keeps you from tripping over your words or speeding up.

## Application

- Sketch the S.T.A.R. in bullet points and time it aloud; adjust until it fits into ~20%.
- Rapport-building small talk is fine *before* you begin, not as the beginning.

## Example

An agency can open with a figure from its research ("7 in 10 of your customers don't recognize your logo on the shelf"), promise "an idea that changes this in 6 months", briefly introduce its team through a relevant project and then present the agenda.

## Questions

- What do you do when the client interrupts you after ten seconds with "get to the idea"?
- Is a surprise risky with a very conservative client?
