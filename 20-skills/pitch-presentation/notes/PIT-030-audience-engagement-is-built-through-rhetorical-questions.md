# Audience engagement is built through rhetorical questions, frames for interaction and names

> **The idea in one sentence:** even in a keynote, the audience has to be part of the "conversation"; a few simple techniques (the rhetorical question with a pause, the raised hand, "Imagine…", asking what questions they have, people's names) prevent the awkward silence (*tumbleweed*).

## Explanation

Without engagement, you talk *at* people, not *with* them, and you lose them. Colenso separates the techniques for large audiences from those for discussions.

**Large audiences:**
- *The rhetorical question*: the brain finds it very difficult not to answer; you ask the question, leave a couple of seconds of silence, then continue.
- *The raised hand*: ask by a show of hands who has experienced X, and raise your own hand too; the visual cue makes others follow, and once one person responds, the rest come.
- *"Imagine…"*: the audience joins the dots themselves; if you make them imagine the outcome you want, you are halfway there.

**Discussions and pitches:**
- *Set the frame* (from the book *IMPACT*): the **open frame** (questions at any time, so stop regularly and ask for them) or the **structure frame** (questions at an announced point, and honour it). End with "is that ok?"; if you get yeses, you have a contract you can come back to.
- *Signpost in advance*: do not ask "Any questions?" after ten minutes of monologue; say you have one final slide and then would love to hear their questions.
- *Change the wording*: "What questions do you have?" presupposes that there are questions; then stay silent. The pause that feels enormous to you is short for the audience.
- *Use names*: mention that a topic is close to Sara's heart; or ask directly and gently for Alisha's thoughts. This is essential for the silent decision-makers in a pitch.

## Application

- Mark 2–3 engagement moments in the pitch structure, and the decision-makers you want to bring into the discussion by name.
- When presenting a creative idea, after the reveal, ask "What do you like most?" before "What don't you like?".

## Example

The author jokes that he knows no one (except a comedian friend) who has walked out of a meeting wishing they had had less engagement.
