# You pick a pitch team by ABC and it is judged by its transitions

> **The idea in one sentence:** a small pitch team, with a clear leader and distinct roles, chosen by approach, background and credentials, often wins or loses in the handovers between speakers.

## Explanation

**The line-up.** Co-presenting shares the pressure and brings expertise, but it demands coherence. Rules: the team smaller than the audience (a maximum of four is a good rule of thumb); every person has a speaking part, because someone lurking silently in the background is a distraction and lacks credibility; a hierarchical structure, not a flat one, with a leader who conducts the flow, fields the questions and delivers the ask. The **ABC** criteria:

| Criterion | Question |
|---|---|
| **A**pproach | Do we have a mix of styles and energies (head, heart, gut, feet; introverts and extroverts)? |
| **B**ackground | Do we have diversity (age, gender, ethnicity, thinking) and similarities with the audience? Similarity reduces the sense of threat. |
| **C**redentials | What expertise relevant to *this* client does each person bring? |

**Team rehearsals.** Everyone together for "the car crash", then "divide and conquer": scenes, then acts, then a full run-through (as in theatre). A "runner" who does not present can coordinate. Everyone speaks early, in the authority section; anyone who appears only at the end has diminished status.

**Transitions** are the moments where trust and dynamics show: "poor team dynamic" is a common reason for losing a pitch. As in a 4 x 100m relay, the handover is what counts: your co-presenter's name and "over to you" is the foolproof option. Avoid "next slide please"; at a table, one person controls the deck. Whoever is not speaking looks at the person who is, to direct the audience's attention.

## Application

- In an agency pitch: a strategist (head), a creative (gut), an account person (heart), a senior who closes (feet), and a designated leader.
- Rehearse the transitions specifically and ask for feedback on them in the public rehearsal.

## Example

A team of four in which each person states, in one sentence and within the first three minutes, their experience relevant to the client looks like "a team of experts"; one in which the director talks for 25 minutes and the junior only answers one question looks like a hierarchy.

## Questions

- Is it appropriate to build the team on similarity with the client? Where does that become tokenism?
- What do you do when the team leader is the weakest presenter?
