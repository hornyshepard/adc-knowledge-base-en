# A pitch is organized like a tree: one headline, three key messages and the evidence underneath

> **The idea in one sentence:** what you want the audience to know comes down to a tabloid headline and three key messages, under which all the figures, cases and stories sit: a "family tree" structure.

## Explanation

**The headline.** Colenso says that a newspaper headline is the ultimate pitch: broadsheets tend to use seven to ten words, tabloids three to five. The titles of YouTubers, working alongside the thumbnail, do the same. The usual presentation titles ("Company-wide Q2 Update") are sleep-inducing. The headline answers "what do I want my audience to know?" in a single sentence: what someone walking out of the room would tell a colleague who was not there. The author recommends the tabloid version (emotion, memorability) over the cautious one. Sometimes the headline never appears explicitly in the presentation, but it serves as a reference point: all content either reinforces it or dilutes it.

**Three key messages.** Like the subheadings of an article, they carry the weight. The expert has the curse of knowing too much and crams everything in; selecting is the expert's job, not the audience's. Starting from Miller (7 ± 2) and from the fact that the audience already has a few things in short-term memory, Colenso proposes three as the magic number. Rhetoric confirms it (the speechwriter Simon Lancaster: four sounds over the top, two too little, three sounds complete). Four is acceptable, five is suspect, more than five is a disservice.

**The tree.** The headline at the top, the three messages underneath, then the evidence under each message. Busy brains crave logic and order.

**Convergence and nuance.** Nishad reaches a similar conclusion from another angle: when he asks people how many ideas they remember from the best speaker they have ever seen, the answer is one, two, rarely three, never more than five. Hence his rule: at most five messages, each in ten words or less, although in a later chapter he goes down to "no more than three", ideally one. Both authors say the same essential thing: the judgment of selection matters more than gestures or voice. Milavsky reaches the same limit for ads: one good reason, backed by at most three arguments, because the fourth becomes the target of counterarguments ([[CPY-005-one-good-reason-beats-a-list-of-37-at-most-three-support|CPY-005]]).

## Application

- Write the headline in under ten words and 3 (at most 5) key messages; use them as section titles.
- Test: if someone reads only the headline and the three messages, do they understand the pitch?

## Example

Steve Jobs launched the iPhone with a triad: "An iPod, a phone and an internet communicator", the product explained in eight words. The author's example for a VP of Sales: headline "Revenue targets exceeded by 11%", messages: average order size up 6%, 16 new clients won, a healthy pipeline for Q3.

## Questions

- Is a pitch headline the same thing as the single-minded proposition in a brief?
- When is the "tabloid" tone dangerous (conservative clients, sensitive topics)?
