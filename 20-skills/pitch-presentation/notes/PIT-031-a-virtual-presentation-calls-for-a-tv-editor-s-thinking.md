# A virtual presentation calls for a TV editor's thinking: eyes on the lens, a tight frame, changes of “shot”

> **The idea in one sentence:** online, a slide screen left up the whole time hides you and gives the audience permission to drift off; you have to look down the lens for the key messages and change the "shot" often, like a film editor.

## Explanation

For Colenso, virtual presentations "aren't better or worse. They're just different." News presenters look down the lens, not at the faces on the screen; you have to do the same when you land an important idea (a sticky note with an arrow next to the camera helps). Sharing your screen from beginning to end is a recipe for disaster: your image becomes tiny, and people can check their email undisturbed. The solution is a "pattern interrupt": you share, then stop sharing for the discussion, use the *spotlight* function, bring your gestures into frame; the brain pays attention to movement.

The author's checklist:

- camera at eye level; a *medium close-up* frame (a little space above your head, shoulders and upper arms in shot, as across the boardroom table);
- an external microphone if necessary; additional lighting;
- notes and slides close to the camera on the screen;
- energy at "level 8.5", standing if you tend to have low energy;
- logging on ten minutes early (non-negotiable), checking screen-sharing, chat and polls; microphone on when you speak, muted when you don't;
- recording at least one rehearsal and reviewing it.

**The host metaphor.** As at a dinner party: you welcome the guests, introduce them to each other, tell them what comes next. **Hybrid:** be more assertive, use names, engage the remote participants early and check back in with them explicitly at Q&A, since they interrupt less. **In a team:** separate roles for the waiting room, the slides, the chat, and someone who watches the faces' reactions and flags confusion.

## Application

- Build your online pitch set-up (camera, light, background) before your first real pitch, not on the day.
- For a team pitch on Zoom, decide in advance who controls what and how you signal to each other.

## Example

A 30-minute online pitch alternates: 3 minutes of face in frame (S.T.A.R.), 5 minutes of slides, stopping the share for a question, then another visual block; the rhythm of an edit, not of a continuous projection.

- Is looking down the lens more important than reading the reactions on the audience's faces?
