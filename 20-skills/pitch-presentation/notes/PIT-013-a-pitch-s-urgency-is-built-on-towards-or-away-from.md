# A pitch's urgency is built on "towards" or "away from" motivation

> **The idea in one sentence:** people act either to get something (*towards*) or to avoid a pain (*away from*); the answer to "why now?" has to be built on the audience's type of motivation or, if you do not know it, on both.

## Explanation

Everyone's to-do list holds things that are important but not urgent and keep getting bumped. Rapid action happens where important meets urgent. That is why, once you know what you want the audience to do, you have to explain why now; otherwise homeostasis wins.

| | "Towards" motivation | "Away from" motivation |
|---|---|---|
| Focus | the goal, the outcome | the pain to avoid |
| Typical language | "win", "obtain", "achieve", objectives, timelines | "problems", "obstacles", "issues", situations to avoid |
| How you create urgency | show why taking steps today is necessary for the desired outcome | dial up the pain of staying still and all the bad stuff your proposal avoids |

An important threshold for "away from": these people act only when the danger of inaction is greater than the risk of doing something. When you do not know the profile (large audiences, pitches with many decision-makers), you paint both pictures: the benefit of taking action and the pain of staying still.

## Application

- The author's exercise: a sheet in two columns, "Towards" (all the positive reasons to act quickly) and "Away From" (all the pain to avoid); pick the most compelling ones and amplify them.
- Listen to the client's language in the briefing meeting: do they talk about "targets" or about "problems"? That tells you how to frame the proposal.

## Example

The same relaunch campaign can be sold to a "towards" client as "the first brand in the category to…" and to an "away from" client as "stop losing share before the new competitor settles in".
