# You plan the audience's emotional journey with active verbs

> **The idea in one sentence:** instead of asking how you feel, choose an active verb for what you want to *make* the audience feel (reassure them, challenge them, excite them) and change it along the way like a gear change.

## Explanation

Buying decisions are not exclusively logical: even the most analytical audience reacts emotionally, however hard procurement procedures try to remove this. Colenso borrows an acting technique: actors annotate every line with what they want to make the other character feel. The difference between a bad actor and a good one: the first *shows* how they feel, the second *makes* others feel something. The same goes for a presentation: your excitement is irrelevant; what matters is theirs.

Practical rules:

- Use active verbs ("intrigue", "reassure", "provoke", "uplift"). The author's test: "Can my audience feel (verb)ed?" "Impressed" works; "confident" is a state, not a verb.
- Think in broad brush strokes: one intention per key message or, for presentations of five minutes or less, a single one. As a rule of thumb, a shift every five minutes or so, as in a soundtrack or a symphony, so that the experience does not flatline.
- A journey that moves between extremes is more powerful than a repetitive one (e.g. challenge → educate → uplift; or reassure → intrigue → surprise).
- The final verb matters most: the emotional resonance lives on after the detail has been forgotten.

The effect is double: the intention changes not only the tone but also the words, images and case studies you choose. Table 2 in the book lists about 70 verbs (*alarm, align, dare, delight, intrigue, provoke, reassure, uplift, wow*, etc.).

## Application

- Note the verb for each section in the margin of your structure; in rehearsals, use the "speed run": for each section, say out loud what you want the audience to know and to feel.
- When presenting a creative concept: intrigue (the insight) → delight (the idea) → reassure (the plan, the budget).

## Example

An update saying "Project X is seriously delayed" can start by alarming, continue by reassuring (we have a plan) and end by mobilizing.

## Questions

- Does the technique risk turning the presentation into emotional manipulation?
- How do you find out, after the presentation, whether the audience felt what you intended?
