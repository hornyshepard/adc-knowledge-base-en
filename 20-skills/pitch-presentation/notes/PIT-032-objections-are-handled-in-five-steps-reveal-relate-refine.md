# Objections are handled in five steps: Reveal, Relate, Refine, Reduce, Redirect

> **The idea in one sentence:** an objection that surfaces in the room is an opportunity, not a threat; you bring it into the open, listen to it, dig down to its core, shrink it by changing the perspective and then take back control.

## Explanation

Sometimes questions turn into opposition: someone takes exception to an idea, or you sense unspoken resistance. Colenso says it is much better for negativity to surface during the presentation than to find out after the client has left unconvinced. The aim: the objection should strengthen the pitch.

| Step | What you do | Wording |
|---|---|---|
| 1 Reveal | you acknowledge the "elephant in the room"; you draw out hidden objections, you say what you see | "what would stop you saying yes?"; say what you notice (that someone looks concerned) or what you feel (that there may be differing opinions in the room) |
| 2 Relate | you show that you are listening; you put money into the "emotional bank account" of the relationship | say it is interesting and ask them to walk you through their concerns; say you can see why they might think that; ask them to tell you more |
| 3 Refine | you dig three layers deep: surface, detail, core; you listen to understand, not to respond; then you play it back in your own words | ask whether there is anything else (twice) |
| 4 Reduce | you change level: out of the weeds and up to the big picture, you show where you already agree; objections seem binary, but both perspectives can coexist | *yes, and…* |
| 5 Redirect | you take back control; if it is a single person, you offer a one-to-one conversation; you reconnect with Know–Feel–Do–Now and course correct | "I suggest we discuss the details afterwards; coming back to…" |

Step 2 also finds support in Milavsky: a belief attacked head-on triggers defensiveness, so you start from what the other person believes and help it evolve ([[INS-038-do-not-contradict-the-target-s-belief-start-from-it-and|INS-038]]). Step 3 is counterintuitive: instead of countering at once, you ask for more information, because the first objection is usually only the surface layer. Step 4 rests on the idea attributed to Einstein that a problem cannot be solved at the level at which it was created.

## Application

- When a creative idea is rejected ("it's too risky"), do not defend the idea at once: ask what exactly seems risky, then what else; often the core is an internal fear (the boss's reaction, legal).
- Practise the wording in the public rehearsal, with a "difficult client".

## Example

The surface layer: "The budget is too high." Detail: "We can't justify the video production." Core: "The finance director hasn't approved video since last year's campaign." Reduce: "We agree that we have to prove efficiency; we can start with a measurable pilot."

## Questions

- Is "Reduce" a legitimate persuasion technique or a way of minimizing real concerns?
- What do you do when the objection is right and your idea does have a problem?
