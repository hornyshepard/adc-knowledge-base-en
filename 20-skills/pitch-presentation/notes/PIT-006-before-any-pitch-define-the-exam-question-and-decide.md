# Before any pitch, define the "exam question" and decide whether it is worth taking part

> **The idea in one sentence:** every pitch answers a single question from the audience; if you do not understand it, or cannot get at least a "B grade" on it, it is wiser to change the format or walk away.

## Explanation

Colenso opens the Discover step with a move few people make: asking whether it is worth going in. A good pitch takes time and effort; every "yes" means "no" to other things (the opportunity cost). Not all prospects are a good fit for what you offer, and not every topic is within your area of expertise.

The central tool is the **"exam question"**: a clear statement of the question the presentation answers, for example "How does your product solve problem Y for the prospect?" or "Why are you best suited to job role Z?". As in an exam, there is an A* outcome, an F outcome and many shades in between. To find it, you ask questions instead of assuming what the audience wants, and you check whether different stakeholders have different expectations.

The author's rule: if you are not confident of achieving at least a B grade, do not proceed in the current form. Alternatives: a co-presenter, a different format (a video, a document, an informal conversation) or, especially in sales, walking away. The question comes back in the Design step: every word, image or prop has to serve the answer; the author recommends writing it in bold letters, in your field of vision. If the client (a procurement department, for example) sets questions in a specific order, you follow them.

## Application

- Restate the brief or the RFP (Request for Proposal) as a single question and validate it with the client before you start work.
- Set up a qualification filter: the chance of winning, the fit, the resources. Pitching "for anything" wears the team out.
- While you work, cut any slide that does not answer the question.

## Example

For an interview, the implicit question is "Why are you best suited to the role?"; for a project update, "What's the best approach for the team to take?". Each calls for different material.

## Questions

- Can a junior afford to turn down a presentation the boss asked for? What alternatives do they have?
- What does the "exam question" hidden in a vague creative brief look like?
