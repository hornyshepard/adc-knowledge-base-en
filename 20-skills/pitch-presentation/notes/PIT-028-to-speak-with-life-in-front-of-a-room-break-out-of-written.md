# To speak with life in front of a room, break out of "written punctuation": firm attacks, deliberate silences, enjambment, scansion, contrasting rhythm

> **The idea in one sentence:** a person who talks with life among friends becomes monotonous on stage because they "recite" by the rules of writing learned at school; the voice becomes expressive again when you place silences, stresses and rhythm by meaning, not by punctuation.

## Explanation

*La bible de la communication* (in a chapter inspired by Jean-Claude Martin's *Le guide de la communication*) explains speakers' monotony as a "cultural handicap" (*handicap culturel*): school asked us to recite the lesson, not to say what we know. Through dictation we learned to pause only at commas and full stops and to lower the voice at the end of a sentence. The result: a continuous tune, filled with "um"s out of fear of silence, which is perceived as a sign of not knowing.

The techniques it proposes:

| Technique | What you do |
|---|---|
| **The attack** (*attaques*) | the first words, spoken frankly, slightly louder than normal, followed at once by a silence; they give an impression of clarity and determination |
| **Enjambment** (*enjambement*) | you pass over the full stop and link the end of one sentence to the start of the next; you breathe somewhere else. On the text, actors mark a small "bridge" above the full stop |
| **Silence** (*silence*) | a breath, not a blank: it lets the room understand; it seems longer to the person making it than to those listening; it gives an impression of mastery |
| **Scansion** (*scansion*) | a tonic stress placed on the word that matters: "c'est MON entreprise, ce sont MES règles !" [it's MY company, these are MY rules!] |
| **Rate and rhythm** (*débit et rythme*) | there is no perfect rate (the figure of 140 words a minute makes speech uniform); you alternate slow and fast passages |

A bonus for debates: a silence placed **in the middle** of a sentence protects you from interruptions, because the other person waits for the end of the idea. The paraverbal dimension (volume, rhythm, tone, articulation) and the nonverbal one (gaze, gestures, movement) complete the meaning of the words.

**Convergence.** Colenso makes the same diagnosis from his experience as an actor: we speak differently from how we write, so a memorized text sounds recited ([[PIT-021-present-from-frameworks-and-key-words-not-from-a-memorized|PIT-021]]). The two sources complement each other: Muller works on phrasing (attack, silence, scansion), Colenso on energy and on warming up the body and the voice ([[PIT-029-the-speaker-leads-the-room-s-energy-at-level-8-and-warms-up|PIT-029]]).

## Application

- Before a pitch: rehearse with a timer, film yourself, check whether your gestures support or distract. On the slides, as little text as possible.
- Mark 3–4 deliberate silences and the words to stress in your notes. Do not leave them to chance.
- Start directly with the first strong idea, not with "so, good morning, we're going to present…". The first sentence is an attack.

## Example

The sentence "We tested three directions. Only one worked.", said with a silence after "three directions" and a stress on "only one", creates suspense. The same sentence recited in an even tone goes unnoticed.

## Questions

- The techniques come from theatre. Where does "performed" diction become a problem of authenticity in front of the client?
- How do these rules adapt to an online presentation, where silence can be mistaken for a technical problem?
