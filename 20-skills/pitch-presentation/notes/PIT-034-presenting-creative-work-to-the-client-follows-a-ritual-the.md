# Presenting creative work to the client follows a ritual: the strategy reread, the idea revealed, questions instead of solutions, the next step asked for

> **The idea in one sentence:** a successful creative meeting does not sell a finished object; it leads the client from the shared strategy to the idea and from there to a decision, asking them for questions, not solutions.

## Explanation

Four sources look at the same meeting from different angles: Milavsky (an agency man writing for the client-side manager), McTigue and Rucker (the client), Allan Peters (the designer) and Greg Hahn (the creative director). Put together, they give five moves:

| Move | What you do | Who supports it |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Preparation | you bring the client into the process early, so that the final solution seems obvious, "almost like math"; the client's team also decides in advance who speaks and how it gives feedback | Hahn; McTigue & Rucker |
| 2. Re-anchoring | the strategy or the brief is read aloud before any creative work; you state the goal of the meeting (for example, picking three directions) | Milavsky; McTigue & Rucker (the account person does it); Peters |
| 3. The reveal | you present live, not through a PDF sent by email; you show all the options before the discussion and come with a reasoned recommendation | Peters; Milavsky |
| 4. Feedback | you first ask what they like; comments come as questions, not as new copy; in large rooms, a silent vote on paper | Peters; Milavsky; McTigue & Rucker |
| 5. The close | you recap the priorities and ask for the next step; you propose a campaign, not an isolated execution, because its variations (*pool-outs*) are easier to approve | McTigue & Rucker; Milavsky |

Two warnings complete the ritual. Milavsky notes that presenters set up the idea with a preamble the public will never get; the test is whether the idea can be explained in 30 seconds and stands on its own without an introduction. What is shown in the meeting is usually a rough version (a storyboard, a mock-up), and the client has to be helped to imagine the final execution (McTigue & Rucker).

**Tension.** Peters says the secret to great work is your ability to sell it; Hahn says his agency does not sell its ideas but talks the client through the reasoning. The difference is one of tone, not of substance: both make the strategy the arbiter and refuse to let the client become the creative director.

## Application

- Put the strategy in one sentence on the first slide and read it before the first image.
- Come with the agency's recommendation and the reason for it; when asked which option you like, "all of them" is the wrong answer.
- After the reveal, ask "What do you like most?" and note the reactions by category (love, like, questions, red flags).

## Example

An agency presents three routes for a local juice brand: it reads the insight from the brief, shows the routes without commentary, recommends route B because it is the only one that extends naturally into social, asks for the positive reactions first and closes with "we propose testing B and C in two cities over the next four weeks".

## Questions

- What do you do if the main decision-maker walks in halfway through the meeting?
- When is it better to send the idea in advance, as a pre-read, even though Peters insists on presenting live?
