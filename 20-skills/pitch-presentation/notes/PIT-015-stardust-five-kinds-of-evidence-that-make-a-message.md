# Stardust: five kinds of evidence that make a message memorable

> **The idea in one sentence:** once you have the messages, you choose the *stardust* that gives them credibility and memorability: figures, cases, visuals, social proof and metaphors.

## Explanation

Colenso compares this stage to a film director who decides on all the shots before the editing suite, or to a chef who gathers the ingredients before the recipe. Of everything you gathered in Discover, only what supports Know–Feel–Do stays. Five categories to check:

| Type | What to look for | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| Facts and figures | the big numbers, the key evidence for the headline and the messages (they appeal to "head" people) | make them relatable for the audience; the detail comes later |
| Case-studies and stories | examples of the proposal working elsewhere; personal anecdotes or stories about the organization | choose the ones that reinforce a key message |
| Visuals | specific images, even abstract ones; short video; punchy demos | avoid clip art and generic stock; long video = audience lost |
| Testimonials and social proof | what clients say, results, quotes from authorities; Cialdini: we determine what is correct by finding out what others think is correct | what others say about you counts for more than what you say |
| Metaphor and simile | a unifying idea, "it's like…" something the audience already understands | the metaphor has to simplify, not complicate |

Nishad makes the same point for beginners: "for example" is a magical phrase, because it turns an abstract statement into a picture; nobody has ever complained about too many *relevant* examples, only about too many data points. A story is "the ultimate example": a setting, a character, a problem and a solution, told in a minute, stay in memory much better than a principle stated flatly. His rule: at least one example or story for each key message.

## Application

- Take an inventory per key message: does each message have at least one figure, one case and one image?
- Look for the metaphor that sums up the proposal in a comparison with something familiar; it is often the most memorable thing in a pitch.

## Example

Film producer Charlie Fink is credited with pitching *The Lion King* to Disney in three words: "Bambi in Africa". The comparison makes a new concept instantly understandable.

## Questions

- When does a metaphor become a trap (the client remembers the comparison, not your idea)?
- What social proof does a student without clients have? (testimonials from teachers, awards, project results)
