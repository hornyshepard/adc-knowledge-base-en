# Rehearsal goes through five stages, from "the car crash" to the dress rehearsal

> **The idea in one sentence:** you have to fail in private to succeed in public; rehearsal has five distinct stages, and if time is short, you invest in the beginning and the end.

## Explanation

In theatre, a two-hour show takes four to six weeks of rehearsal, that is, 140–210 hours. To the excuse "it makes me too wooden", the author replies: you just haven't rehearsed enough. The stages:

| Stage | Purpose |
|---|---|
| 1 "The car crash" | the first time you speak it aloud, inevitably a mess; get it out of the way as early as possible |
| 2 Private rehearsal | familiarity, flow, tweaks; in sections, with no external feedback |
| 3 Public rehearsal | colleagues, friends or a coach, with a precise brief on the feedback you want; questions are introduced (at least two sessions if there will be Q&A) |
| 4 Technical rehearsal | the space, seated or standing, the screen, who does what, sharing the slides; 1–2 days before |
| 5 Dress rehearsal | a complete run-through without stopping, as if it were the real thing; only high-level feedback, to build confidence; ideally the day before |

**MVR** (*minimal viable rehearsal*): if you have no time, rehearse the beginning (where nerves peak) and the end (peak-end). Thirty minutes of focused preparation is worth hours of semi-distracted time. Rehearse at "level 8" energy, standing if you will stand, and ideally on film.

**Plans B and C** cover "the two Ts": *timings* (someone arrives late, ten minutes are cut from your slot: you know in advance what to drop and what to add) and *technology* (can you deliver without the laptop?). Plus the "parachute": water, bullet points on note cards, spare cables, questions to ask the audience if you lose your thread.

## Application

- Schedule the rehearsals in your calendar, working backwards from the pitch date, as soon as you hear about it, and treat them as non-negotiable.
- For each section, note a "short" and an "extended" version.

## Example

The author rehearses walking down the street with his headphones in: "People just think you're on the phone!"
