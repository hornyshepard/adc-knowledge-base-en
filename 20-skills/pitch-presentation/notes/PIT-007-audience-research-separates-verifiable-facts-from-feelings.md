# Audience research separates verifiable facts from feelings

> **The idea in one sentence:** the presenter is the least important person in the room, so they have to research their audience like a detective, telling apart the facts, which you build on, from the feelings, which you test.

## Explanation

The starting point: people do not care about you or your product, only about what you and your product can do for them; the biggest question in everyone's mind is "what's in it for me?". It follows that you cannot roll out the same material for every audience, and with an audience of several people you have to give each of them something.

Colenso lists the sources of research: LinkedIn profiles, but above all information "between the lines" (charities they support, hobbies, public comments); videos of the decision makers at conferences or on podcasts (tone of voice, way of thinking); your network and colleagues; and finally, direct conversation: a pre-call with the key decision makers, or two or three conference delegates contacted in advance. Direct contact also shows who decides, who influences, and who is an advocate or a detractor.

The information falls into two categories:

| Facts | Feelings |
|---|---|
| verifiable: where the person has worked, where they went to school, what good causes they support | interpretations: "they're not bought into the project", "they've already made up their mind", "they don't like me" |
| used to speak the audience's language and build rapport | useful for a 360-degree view, but they have to be tested and challenged |

People change, so meet your audience in the present moment and be prepared to be surprised.

For school presentations, Nishad adds a useful observation: the audience is threefold: you (what you would want to listen to), your classmates and the teacher (who grades against a rubric). Imagine a Venn diagram and aim for its center; use the grading rubric as a checklist of what the assessor is looking for.

## Application

- The author's exercise: write down the name of each participant and five to ten things about them, then highlight the facts and the feelings in two colors. What needs adapting? Which feeling has to be addressed explicitly?
- For unknown audiences, create "avatars" for each segment (business owners, accountants, etc.).

## Example

Colenso directed *Hamlet* for the cruise ship QM2 (a traditional audience) at the same time as Michael Sheen was playing an edgy *Hamlet* at the Young Vic. The same text, both got standing ovations, but swapped they would have failed: the packaging is chosen according to the audience.

## Questions

- Where does legitimate research end and "cyber-stalking" begin?
- What do you do when a negative feeling ("they have already chosen another agency") turns out to be true?
