# The agency industry has polarized into global holding companies and boutiques, and account conflicts limit who can work for whom

> **The idea in one sentence:** after the wave of mergers from the 1970s to the 1990s, a few holding companies control most of the world's advertising, boutiques live at the other end, and the rule that you do not work for two competitors decides which accounts an agency can take.

## Explanation

Richards traces the industry's "merger mania". In 1960, Marion Harper turned McCann Erickson into a subsidiary of a holding company, Interpublic, which began to acquire other agencies. The Saatchi brothers, from 1972 on, swallowed about thirty-five companies. In 1982, Keith Reinhard (then at Needham Harper Steers) predicted a two-tiered industry (boutiques full of vitality at the bottom, six or seven giants at the top and no middle) and built Omnicom (1986) out of BBDO, DDB and Needham. Martin Sorrell made WPP the biggest group, acquiring J. Walter Thompson, Ogilvy & Mather and Young & Rubicam. Of the hundred biggest agencies of the 1980s, two-thirds had been taken over by the 1990s; in 2018, the top five groups were WPP, Omnicom, Publicis, Interpublic and Dentsu.

Two consequences for the relationship with the client:

- **Account conflict.** A client does not accept its agency working for a direct competitor. The Omnicom merger created a few such conflicts of interest, costing about $250 million in billings. Dentsu sidestepped the rule by physically separating its account groups and came to work for competitors such as Nissan and Honda.
- **The media spin-off.** In the 1990s, the holding companies pulled their media departments out into separate companies (MindShare, OMD, Starcom, Zenith). Creative agencies thus lost control over the money, since compensation had been tied to media; an executive quoted by Richards argued: "Separating media from creative was the worst mistake the industry ever made." Independent media agencies, on the other hand, helped boutiques with no media department.

## Application

- Before you go after a new client, check whether your agency (or a sister agency in the holding company) works for one of its competitors.
- For your career: a global network offers big brands and mobility, a boutique offers access to decisions and variety; the "middle" is the most unstable zone.

## Example

Reinhard's forecast came true through the very merger he led: his agency, the number 16 agency in the world and stuck in the middle, became part of the largest group of its time.

## Questions

- Does the account-conflict rule still make sense when a holding company has dozens of agencies in the same country?
- What does a client get from a boutique that a holding company cannot give it?
