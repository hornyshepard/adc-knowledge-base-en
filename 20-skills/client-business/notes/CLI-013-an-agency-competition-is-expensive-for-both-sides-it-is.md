# An agency competition is expensive for both sides: it is qualified, framed by contract and has only one winner

> **The idea in one sentence:** a pitch between agencies eats up weeks of unpaid work, so the agency decides whether to take part, and a serious client organizes it with a clear brief, decision criteria, a guaranteed winner and, ideally, compensation for those who lose.

## Explanation

*La boîte à outils de la publicité* sets the expectations of the two sides against each other:

| The client (*annonceur*) expects from the agency | The agency expects from the client |
|---|---|
| first, strategic thinking and creativity | a structured and precise brief that does not change along the way |
| then, seriousness in execution, responsiveness, deadlines met | that at the end of the competition an agency is actually chosen |
| | a fee that recognizes the value of strategic thinking and creativity |

The authors recommend that the competition stage be perfectly framed (*parfaitement cadrée*): the resources committed are large, so they have a cost, and the agency can negotiate in the contract a compensation (*dédommagement*) if it is not selected, set according to the investment required. Some agencies waive it and include it only if they are chosen. The authors also warn about consultations for a one-off operation, with no prospect of a long relationship.

The other sources add three lessons. Milavsky: a pitch can be lost even after the best evaluation, and in a competition with only one winner "being second is equivalent to being 43rd". Colenso: before any pitch, ask yourself whether you can get at least a B grade; if not, change the format or walk away. McTigue and Rucker, from the client side: if agencies decline to pitch for you, look at yourself first; and choose the people, not the pitch idea, even if their idea was your second favorite.

## Application

A qualification filter before you accept a pitch:
- Is there a written brief and known decision criteria?
- Who decides, and how (see the buying center)?
- How many agencies are taking part? Is there a pitch fee?
- Is it a one-off project or the start of a relationship?
- Can we get at least a B grade? What does it cost the team and our other clients if we take part?

## Example

Milavsky's agency had received the highest ratings from an oil company client; six months after a new vice president of marketing arrived, it was called into a competition. It took part, pitched very well and lost as a close second.

## Questions

- Is it ethical for a client to ask for complete creative ideas in an unpaid pitch?
- When is it worth it for a small agency to enter a competition it has almost no chance of winning?
