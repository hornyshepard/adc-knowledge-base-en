# Ideas erode in the approval chain: each level that asks for "a small change" lowers the risk and, with it, the power of the ad

> **The idea in one sentence:** middle management exists to reduce risk, and good advertising is risky by nature; if it is not protected, the idea comes out of the approval process safe, ordinary and ineffective.

## Explanation

Milavsky describes the journey of an idea as a game of snakes and ladders: levels in the agency, levels at the client, legal and technical departments, the TV network's clearance. Each step has more chances to say "no" than "yes".

**Ogilvy's experiment**: a training video imagined the Hathaway ad (the confident man with the eye patch) passing through the approval levels. In one meeting someone asks for inset shots of the whole shirt line, in the next the man is moved into a department store, then a woman admirer is added (women make most of the shirt purchases), then someone asks whether the eye patch suggests an eye infection. The result: a safe ad that looks like all the others in the category. The patch was a sign of a daring, risky life, not of conjunctivitis.

Phenomena he describes:

- **"Kilroy was here" changes**: small, unnecessary changes requested so that someone can leave their personal imprint;
- **Research as protection**: tests used not for diagnosis but as decision protection, especially in government agencies;
- **Organizational groupthink**, the opposite case: an off-strategy idea that gets through because an influential executive champions it;
- **Staff changes**: a new manager changes the campaign or the agency to put their stamp on it, even when results were good.

## Application

- Reread the strategy at the start of every approval meeting and say clearly that you are asking for approval or questions, not new copy.
- Ask for comments as questions or substantive objections; let the creative team find the solutions, so it keeps its authorship.
- Treat the deciders as a target: what they believe now, what personal goals they have, what the idea gives them. Do not mock Harvard if the person who approves went there.
- Propose campaigns, not one-off spots: once the prototype is approved, the *pool-outs* (variations on the same idea) go through much more easily.
- Fight for short chains: at Texaco, after a year of earned trust, ideas went through two levels, the marketing department and the CEO.

## Example

One of Milavsky's agencies, given the client's highest ratings by an oil company, was put into a review six months after a new vice president of marketing arrived, and lost: "you were a close second". In competitions with only one winner, being second is equivalent to being 43rd.

## Questions

- When is a change requested by the client a real improvement, and how do you tell it apart from a "Kilroy"?
- How far should an agency go in defending an idea before it puts the relationship at risk?
