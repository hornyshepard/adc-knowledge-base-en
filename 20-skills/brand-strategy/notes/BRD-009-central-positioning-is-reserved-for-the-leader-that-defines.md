# Central positioning is reserved for the leader that defines the category, and almost all other brands have to differentiate

> **The idea in one sentence:** to be centrally positioned means to deliver all the main benefits of the category and to be "the best"; that is credible only for the brand that is already the prototype of the category.

## Explanation

The first decision after the general model of positioning is the brand's relation to the category.

**Central positioning**: the brand delivers all the main benefits of the category and is positioned as the best brand in it. Because it more or less **defines the category**, the strategy suits only brands with a strong market position, often the first really successful brand in the category. Xerox came to mean copying, and Hoover vacuuming. Psychologically, such a brand works as a **prototype**: the representation by which people quickly identify the category. The leader has to keep confirming the perception that it does the best job in delivering the primary category benefit.

A "me too" brand can attempt a central positioning only if people can objectively determine that it delivers the primary benefit as well as the leader and at a lower price. Store brands do this, imitating the leader's positioning at a lower price. The authors say, however, that this is not easy to do.

**Differentiated positioning**, for almost all other brands: the answer to "what does it offer?" is a benefit *other* than the primary category benefit, which the leader already "occupies". Differentiation helps the brand stand out from the estimated 10,000 brands the average adult has represented in memory.

Whatever the choice, a second decision follows: whether the positioning is oriented toward the user or toward the product benefit.

**Where the sources converge.** The rule also appears in Alice Tybout (Kellogg): the leader claims the benefit that reflects the main reason for using the category (Tide cleans best), followers choose a niche benefit (Gain makes clothes smell fresh). Fennis and Stroebe give the psychological explanation: the first successful brand becomes the prototype by which all the others are judged.

## Application

- Ask honestly: are we the prototype of the category? If not, do not claim "the best at X", where X is the benefit everyone attributes to the leader.
- As a challenger, look for an important benefit that the leader has not taken on. The expectancy-value model helps you find it.
- As the leader, invest in confirming the primary benefit, not in moving to secondary benefits.

## Example

Buffalo Trace is positioned centrally and product-oriented, as "the world's most award-winning distillery": it claims the primary benefit of the category, the quality of the whiskey. Zendium, on the other hand, does not compete with Colgate on fluoride. It differentiates itself through natural enzymes and "0% SLS".

## Questions

- Can a new brand become central in a new category that it creates itself (Uber, Airbnb)?
- What happens when the leader's name becomes generic and loses trademark protection?
