# A brand is a set of associations that acts as a prism on the product

> **The idea in one sentence:** a name becomes a brand only when people link it to other things, and these associations change the way the product itself is perceived.

## Explanation

The branding program at Kellogg defines a brand as a set of associations linked to the name, mark or symbol associated with a product or service. The key word is "associations": everything you think of when you see Apple (innovation, simplicity, design, the late Steve Jobs in his black turtleneck) or Caterpillar (tough, rugged, big, construction). A name without associations is simply a name. Associations can be positive or negative (Malaysia Airlines after the crashes, Facebook and privacy), and many brands have both.

Tim Calkins describes the brand as a **prism** (the Branding Prism): consumers never see only the product, but the product seen through the brand. The brand can elevate or diminish the product. His study with MBA students: the same gold earrings with diamonds were valued at $550 unbranded, at $873 "from Tiffany" and at only $81 "from Walmart". The difference is not conspicuous consumption (you can't tell the earrings apart from a distance); it comes from the fact that "good quality" means something different under each name.

Calkins lists three ways brands add value: they shape perceptions, they differentiate products and services (without differentiation, competition slides toward price and zero margins), and they attract and retain employees (at companies with strong brands, executives accept lower salaries). Against the idea that the abundance of digital information makes brands useless, the author argues that they matter more: they simplify choice in a world with too much data of doubtful quality.

## Application

- Before any campaign, take an inventory of the brand's current associations (positive and negative) and of the desired ones; the gap between them is the job of communication.
- Treat every touchpoint as a source of associations, not only the advertising.
- In a pitch, use the Tiffany/Walmart example to show the client that the brand changes the price people are willing to pay.

## Example

Lululemon sells leggings for $98, while Target has a similar pair for $24.99. The difference comes from associations with wellness, balance and spirituality, built through stores, staff, events and social media.

## Questions

- What happens to a brand when negative associations become dominant? Can it be "cleaned up", or does the name have to change?
- Is the definition "brand = associations" enough for service brands, where the experience is the product?
