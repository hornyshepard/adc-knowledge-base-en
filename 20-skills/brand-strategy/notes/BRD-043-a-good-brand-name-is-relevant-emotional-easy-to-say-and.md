# A good brand name is relevant, emotional, easy to say and marketable

> **The idea in one sentence:** a name is essentially a short ad for the product; it creates associations through its very sounds, and a name met with indifference is a lousy name.

## Explanation

Paul Earle (former executive director of Farmhouse, the innovation center at Leo Burnett) argues against Shakespeare's line about the rose: names matter. His markers:

| Marker | What it asks | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Product relevance** | tells a story tied to the product and to consumer needs | Peloton (the group of cyclists at the front of the pack + a "virtual pack" of home cyclists); Angel's Envy (the master distiller reduced the "angel's share" that evaporates inside the barrel) |
| **Emotion** | evokes a feeling; even polarizing is better than indifference | Mush (oatmeal): most love the name, some hate it; Angel's Envy has creative tension (angel + deadly sin) |
| **Ease of use** | short, rolls off the tongue, "mellifluous" | Tide, Crest, Joy; names with many vowels: Hello, Olly, Mio |
| **Marketability** | the "campaignability" test: you can see a tagline, a launch act, a social campaign right away | Angel's Envy |

Earle does not care for portmanteaus (Accenture, Xfinity), which work only thanks to massive resources, though he allows exceptions (Swiffer) and grants that they can skirt trademark challenges.

Bobby Calder (ch. 7) adds **sound symbolism**, which works below awareness: the phonemes in "Tiffany," mastered late in childhood, are associated with things that are less common; "sharp" names (Jax) are associated with sourness, round names (Berry) with sweetness; front-of-the-tongue vowels (Priv) are perceived as smaller. Fluent names (shorter, more pronounceable) are preferred, even by investors. Failures: Priv (BlackBerry) evoked no associations; "nova" translates to "no go" in Spanish; NorthShore University HealthSystem suggested a university that did not exist. Calder also warns about fads: people's first names (Casper, Marcus, Oscar) lose their effect once they are overused.

The practical process described by strategist Myra El-Bayoumi in *Goddess Guide to Branding* (SRC-016) completes the criteria: you draft four or five hundred names with people at varying levels of closeness to the business, cut only the absolute no's (about two hundred remain), run the list through a trademark attorney, then "try on" the finalists: on a sticky note on your monitor, said out loud in the mirror.

## Application

- Score each candidate name on the four markers and say it out loud several times.
- Check the sound associations and the meanings in the languages of the target markets.
- Write three campaign headlines for each name; if nothing comes naturally, the name is weak.

## Example

BlackBerry (created by the naming firm Lexicon in place of "PocketLink") used the alliteration of the softer "BB" consonants to suggest high touch rather than high tech, and the association with constant connection became so strong that people coined the term "crackberry."

## Questions

- When is a descriptive name better than an evocative one?
- How do the criteria change for a name that has to work in voice commands (Alexa)?
