# Iconic brands (Holt) offer an identity myth that resolves a cultural tension of the moment, and lose their power when the tension fades

> **The idea in one sentence:** brands compete in myth markets, not only in product markets; a brand becomes an icon when it tells a story that soothes the contradiction between a society's dominant ideology and people's real lives.

## Explanation

Douglas Holt's theory (*How Brands Become Icons*, 2004) appears in four books of the library, which converge on its core. A cultural icon is a symbol accepted as shorthand for important ideas; an iconic brand reaches that value, especially in self-expressive categories (clothing, drinks, cars). Parsons, Maclaran and Chatzidakis sum up the mechanism in three steps: identify a **cultural tension**, offer an **identity myth** that mediates it, draw on historical and popular memory. Fernández Gómez details the principles:

| Principle | What it requires |
|---|---|
| Social tension | cultural research (history, sociology, literary criticism), not focus groups |
| Identity myth | a concrete story, not a concept: Corona does not stand for relaxation in the abstract, but for the beach where you do absolutely nothing |
| Ritual experience | the myth is lived through using the product |
| Populist worlds | inspiration from marginal, authentic worlds governed by values |
| Influential communication | a few memorable advertising coups, not an avalanche of ads |

Mini shows the mechanism: in the 1950s size was a marker of status, but the 1956 Suez crisis made fuel economy necessary, and the Mini resolved the contradiction (small, fun, British, "cool"). Coca-Cola's "Hilltop" (1971), cited by both Kotler and Fernández Gómez, offered a myth of reconciliation to an America divided by Vietnam.

Kotler, Kartajaya and Setiawan (*Marketing 3.0*) link the theory to the paradoxes of globalization: people under pressure to be both global and local citizens look for continuity, connection and direction, and a silent majority senses the paradox until a brand names it. Hence their conclusion: marketers need anthropology and sociology.

**Limits, acknowledged by all the sources.** The myth is contextual, so it has to be reinvented at every cultural disruption (Snapple lost its anti-corporate myth when it switched to mere humor). The brand can elude control (Burberry, "contaminated" by the *chav* image). The market co-opts the counterculture and turns it into a commodity (the *rebel sell*). Fernández Gómez adds that Holt's myth is not the Jungian archetype, but a story of legitimization that acquires meaning for a particular culture at a specific moment.

## Application

- Start planning with a cultural question: which promise of society no longer fits the target audience's life?
- Look for the myth in real worlds at the margins (subcultures, trades, communities), not in trend reports.
- Think of a manifesto execution and review it regularly: when the tension fades, the campaign stays memorable but is no longer relevant.

## Example

Jack Daniel's recovered the "rugged individualist" of the Old West as a response to the crisis of masculinity during the Cold War.

## Questions

- What cultural tension in today's Romania could a brand resolve (for example, tradition vs. emigration)?
- How do you reconcile reinventing the myth with the permanence that positioning demands?
