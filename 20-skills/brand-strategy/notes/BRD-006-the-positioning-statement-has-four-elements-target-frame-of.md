# The positioning statement has four elements: target, frame of reference, point of difference, reason to believe

> **The idea in one sentence:** a positioning is written as a single internal sentence (for [target], [brand] is the [frame of reference] that [point of difference], because [reason to believe]), and it guides every decision about the brand.

## Explanation

Alice Tybout defines the positioning statement as an internal document, not a slogan. It aligns brand design, touchpoints and advertising. It has four elements:

| Element | Role | Lite (Miller) example |
|---|---|---|
| **Target** | who; described demographically, psychographically or behaviorally, often with an insight about motivation | males 21 to 34 with blue-collar occupations |
| **Frame of reference** | the goal the brand serves, usually the category | beer |
| **Point of difference** | the abstract benefit, important and "owned" by the brand | lets you drink more without feeling filled up |
| **Reason to believe** | the concrete proof of the benefit | fewer calories |

The Apple example: for consumers who want to feel empowered by the technology they use regardless of their level of skill, Apple offers electronic devices that make you feel smarter, because they incorporate leading-edge technology that is sophisticated yet intuitive to use. The target does not have to be described exhaustively, only in enough detail to choose the frame and the difference. The insight in the target usually points to a pain point that the brand overcomes: for Lite, "the sin without the penalty".

Tybout proposes two tests. **Clarity and credibility**: show the statement to people outside the company and ask them in which situations they would use the brand, why it is claimed to be superior and whether they believe it. **Distinctiveness**: replace the brand name with a competitor's; if the statement still seems reasonable, the positioning is not distinctive.

**Where the sources converge.** The formula appears almost identically in other books: McTigue and Rucker put it into the brief, Percy and Rosenbaum-Elliott write it as "X is the brand for [target audience] in the market for [category need] that offers [benefit]", and marketing textbooks speak of the value proposition. The main difference: Tybout explicitly asks for the reason to believe, which is missing from Percy's three-variable formula.

## Application

- Write the positioning before the brief; the creative brief takes it over (target, insight, position), then adds the tone and the mandatory elements.
- Apply the name-replacement test to any brand platform you receive from the client.
- Check whether the chosen target is large enough for the sales goals and whether it informs pricing, distribution and media.

## Example

Blue Apron could have been positioned as a more efficient alternative to shopping and preparing dinner, or as a more fun substitute for takeout. The founders chose the first option: saving time mattered more, and grocery bills offer a larger pool of spending than takeout.

## Questions

- When should the target be defined by behavior rather than by demographics?
- How do you handle non-targeted consumers who adopt the brand (women who buy Gillette Fusion)?
