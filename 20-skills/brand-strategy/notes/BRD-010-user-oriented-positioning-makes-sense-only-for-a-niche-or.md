# User-oriented positioning makes sense only for a niche or for the social approval motive; otherwise the product is the hero

> **The idea in one sentence:** when the message is about "who uses it", the user's characteristics become the message; that works only if you are talking to a niche or if people choose the brand in order to be seen with it.

## Explanation

The second initial positioning decision: is the brand placed relative to competitors through the **user** or through the **product benefit**? Both options use brand benefits, but they differ in who is at the center. In a **product benefit oriented** positioning, the product's characteristics are the message (the product is the hero). In a **user oriented** positioning, the user's characteristics are the message.

The authors allow user oriented positioning only under **two conditions** (Table 8.2):

1. **The brand is marketed to a specific segment or niche**: performance wear for the serious runner, a single malt scotch for the discerning drinker.
2. **The dominant purchase motive is social approval.** You remind people how good they will feel using and *displaying* the brand. This is why luxury cars and high fashion often use this kind of positioning.

You can also tap into a latent social approval motive. Nobody buys peanut butter for social approval, but Jif has for many years positioned itself on mothers' (and recently also fathers') nurturing feelings for their children, that is, on "mothers who really care".

Two important points. User oriented positioning is *appropriate*, not *required*, in these situations: a product benefit oriented positioning can be used there too. And in most cases the default rule is positioning on the product benefit.

## Application

- Before you write "for those who…", check: is it a real niche, or is the purchase motive actually social approval?
- If the motive is the problem solved (informational), put the product at the center. An idealized user does not remove stains.
- In copy, user orientation shows in the subject ("You, the one who…"). Product orientation shows in the attributes and results.

## Example

An ad for a luxury watch shows the wearer at a gala (social approval), so it is user oriented. An ad for a sports watch for mountaineers shows the case holding up at -40°C (a niche, but product oriented). Both are legitimate.

## Questions

- How does this rule relate to "lifestyle" advertising on social media, where the user is almost always at the center?
- Is influencer marketing, by definition, a user oriented positioning?
