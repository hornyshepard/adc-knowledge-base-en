# Brands that adopt moral causes gain a halo, but become targets for the accusation of greenwashing

> **The idea in one sentence:** in the "moralised brandscape", almost every brand talks about values, which brings sympathy and sales, but also a public magnifying glass that quickly punishes any gap between statements and actions.

## Explanation

Parsons, Maclaran and Chatzidakis take up the concept of the **moralised brandscape** (Salzer-Mörling & Strannegård, 2007): a cultural landscape in which moral statements become part of the discourse of both brands and consumers. The boundary between "ethical brands" (Body Shop, Ben & Jerry's, Innocent, Ecover, Fairtrade) and ordinary brands is blurring.

Tools and data from the book:

- **Cause-related marketing**: American Express is credited with first using the phrase in 1983, for a campaign that donated 1 cent to the Statue of Liberty every time a customer used the card: 45 percent more new customers, 28 percent more card usage. The industry grew from $120 million (1990) to $1.62 billion (2010); 89 percent of respondents say they would switch brands for a cause if price and quality were the same. Examples: Pampers and Unicef, Tesco's "Free Computers for Schools", the knitted hats on Innocent bottles for Age UK.
- **Values campaigns**: Coca-Cola's "Small World Machine" (2013) brought India and Pakistan together through vending machines fitted with cameras, and won twenty awards at Cannes Lions.
- **Fairtrade** moved from charity shops into supermarkets (KitKat certified in 2010), but is accused of whitewashing the image of some companies (Nestlé and the infant formula scandal).
- **Greenwashing**, in three forms: *green spinning* (reputation PR in polluting industries), *green selling* (an existing product feature promoted as "green"), *green harvesting* (savings on "green" aspects, such as fewer towels washed at the hotel, without passing them on to the customer).

The risks: consumers do not know how much the cause receives (percentages of "profit"); a brand that takes a moral stance becomes vulnerable if it is caught with other practices; activists (Adbusters, Naomi Klein's *No Logo*) and social networks amplify hypocrisy. For Fairtrade, critics point out that producers' stories can soothe consumer guilt without structural change (Dolan).

## Application

- Choose the cause by its fit with the brand and with what the company can deliver, not by its popularity.
- Say clearly how much reaches the cause and how; transparency is the defense against the accusation of greenwashing.
- Check the "chain of proof" before launch: suppliers, packaging, employees; everything will be compared with the promise.

## Example

The Body Shop sold to L'Oréal (2006): Anita Roddick called herself a "Trojan horse", but part of the public saw a betrayal of values.

## Questions

- Which "purpose" campaign in Romania would you consider greenwashing, and why?
- Is cause-related marketing a win for both sides or a way of buying image?
