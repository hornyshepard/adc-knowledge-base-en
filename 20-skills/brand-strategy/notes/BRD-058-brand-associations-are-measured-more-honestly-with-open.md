# Brand associations are measured more honestly with open-ended questions

> **The idea in one sentence:** instead of asking people whether your brand is "innovative" or "trustworthy," ask them what comes to mind and then "what else?": you will get their words, not yours.

## Explanation

Julie Hennessy starts from an anecdote: Disney once had a voluminous Mickey Mouse brand standard that dictated what Mickey could do; today a Disney executive admits that the firm controls a very small fraction of the images of Mickey Mouse in circulation. Managing a brand is a conversation, and measurement has to listen, not just dictate.

The traditional methods have limits. **Aided awareness**, with a list of brands, often overstates actual awareness. **Attitudinal brand tracking** asks people to agree with attributes chosen by the brand's managers, often aspirational: they are the managers' words, not the consumers', and the survey suggests the answer.

The proposed method: you ask an open-ended question, "What comes to mind when you think of the brand X?", followed by "What else? What else?" The answers are tabulated across large enough groups of respondents, usually online, where people express themselves freely. The results have often been surprising:

- Toyota drivers' association with "safety" **increased** during the unintended acceleration scandal, as they needed to justify the daily decision to keep driving their cars.
- Many Volkswagen drivers saw the emissions "defeat" device as evidence of the sophistication of German engineering.
- Women were more likely than men to describe Hillary Clinton with negative adjectives, an early signal.
- After the 2017 scandals, Uber was associated with "unsafe," "sketchy," "be careful"; Lyft with "fun" and "friendly." Lyft's pink logo became an inadvertent asset, and women were at least three times more likely to lead with a negative comment about Uber than about Lyft.

Digital makes tracking faster, cheaper and continuous (short surveys, social sentiment analysis), adds new metrics (cost per click, per action, per customer acquired) and reveals the nonbuyers who are close to converting. But digital figures still need interpretation: does a high acquisition cost come from a lack of awareness or from weak associations?

## Application

- Add an open-ended association question to every campaign pre-test or post-test.
- Compare consumers' spontaneous words with the values in the brand platform; the gap is material for the brief.
- Code the answers into positive and negative themes and track them over time.

## Example

Lyft's policy of encouraging tipping, first seen as a negative, became evidence of friendliness after the Uber scandal.

## Questions

- What biases show up even in open-ended questions (order, the wish to seem smart)?
- How would you combine spontaneous associations with social listening?
