# A brand extension succeeds only if the brand helps the new product, the product helps the brand and the idea makes business sense: otherwise it dilutes the brand

> **The idea in one sentence:** putting a known name on a new product lowers the cost of the launch and transfers trust, but a poor fit weakens the very associations that made the brand valuable.

## Explanation

Grewal and Levy make a useful distinction: a **brand extension** takes the name into a different product line (Colgate, from toothpaste to toothbrushes), while a **line extension** stays within the same product line (new Häagen-Dazs flavors). The advantages: less spending on awareness, the carry-over of a positive perception (Ferrari clothing), synergy between complementary products. The risk is **brand dilution**: Harley-Davidson's cake decorating kit, Zippo's perfume for women, Virgin Brides. Tim Calkins (Kellogg) gives the Ralph Lauren case: the lines multiplied until the flagship in New York and the random Polo sweaters thrown onto a fixture at Macy's could no longer coexist.

The two sources offer checklists that complement each other:

| Calkins: three questions | Grewal and Levy: four rules |
|---|---|
| 1. Will the existing brand help the new product? (Clorox on salad dressings: no) | evaluate the fit between the product classes (VIA instant coffee for Starbucks customers) |
| 2. Will the new product help the existing brand? | look for the core brand's specific attributes in the extension (HP: reliability) |
| 3. Is the new product a good business idea? | do not extend the name to too many categories |
| (none) | decide whether the extension should be distanced (Ritz-Carlton does not carry the Marriott name) |

Both put fit first. Calkins adds the reverse direction (the effect on the parent brand) and the business test; Grewal and Levy add distancing, which is an architecture decision. Other Kellogg chapters confirm the risk: Gerber Singles, one-dish meals for young adults eating by themselves, carried the Gerber baby image; Carpenter and Nakamoto show that a pioneer has an advantage close to its original position but a disadvantage further away from it (Jeepster failed). **Co-branding** and **licensing** are related ways of borrowing equity, with the same risk (Burger King and Häagen-Dazs had customer profiles that were too different; Ferrari ended up on dice). Policarpio lists the same strategies only as advantages, without the risk of dilution.

## Application

- Answer the three questions and the distance rule in writing; if one answer is "no," propose a new brand or an endorsement.
- Map the parent brand's associations and mark which ones carry over into the new category and which ones do harm.
- The extension's communication has to show the link to the parent brand's attributes, not just the novelty.

## Example

Apple uses the same name on all its new products: the new products demonstrate the brand's innovative nature, and the brand brings its associations of excellence in design. Ferrari, with licenses from sunglasses to dice, is the opposite example, a brand exposed to dilution.

## Questions

- Where is the line between an aspirational extension and dilution? Who decides: the core customer or the new one?
- Why can a luxury brand (Aston Martin) stretch less than a mass brand?
