# Purpose or positioning: brands operate at different altitudes

> **The idea in one sentence:** not every brand needs a lofty purpose; some gain more from a clear positioning and from experiences that "do," not speeches that "say."

## Explanation

The same book holds an open tension. Chapter 2 (Stengel) presents purpose as a growth engine, with studies that show superior returns. Chapter 17, written by Silvia Lagnado (global chief marketing officer at McDonald's, who led Dove's "Real Beauty" campaign) and Colin Mitchell (former worldwide head of planning at Ogilvy & Mather), talks about "peak purpose" and identifies three problems:

1. **Convergence.** Brand laddering pushes the positioning higher and higher up a Maslovian ladder, and the tops of all these ladders reach the same place: if every airline is bringing people together, there is little differentiation. Hence the taglines built on "life" and "live."
2. **Disconnection from the product.** The top of the ladder is a long way from the bottom: toiletry brands take on police brutality, fast-food brands battle for net neutrality. Audi advocated equal pay for women in a Super Bowl ad, and critics pointed out that Volkswagen had no female board members.
3. **Definition creep.** Purpose has been conflated with corporate social responsibility and with causes, and has become vague or insincere.

Their solution has two parts. **Strategically**, accept that different brands operate at different altitudes: some at the level of purpose (Nike), others at the level of a good old-fashioned positioning (Uber, Amazon, McDonald's). **In execution**, the brand can "do" instead of "say": emotion can be found in experience too (unboxing an Apple product, the ease of Waze). McDonald's defined its positioning as "Making delicious feel-good moments easy for everyone" and expresses it through "little bubbles of happy": job applications through Snapchat (*Snaplications*), a public debate in Canada over bacon on the Big Mac, kiosks and table service.

Tybout (ch. 1) offers a middle position: purpose works when it extends the point of difference (Always: absorbency → confidence → confidence in facing stereotypes), but it holds the brand to the standard it espouses.

The critique echoes in other books: *Shareworthy* asks that purpose come from the brand's DNA and be backed with money (STO-037), and Percy and Rosenbaum-Elliott see "brand ideals" as just new jargon (ADV-084).

## Application

- Ask: at what altitude does the brand naturally sit, given the company's culture and its role in people's lives?
- Test any purpose with the question: "What does it have to do with the product and with what the company actually does?"
- For practical brands, put the creativity into small, frequent experiences, not into manifestos.

## Example

McDonald's mission statement, "To be our customers' favorite place and way to eat and drink," was praised by *The Economist* precisely because it is succinct and credible.

## Questions

- Is "peak purpose" a critique of purpose or only of its poor execution?
- Can a brand step down from purpose to positioning without seeming to abandon its values?
