# A good brand mission has three ingredients: business as unusual, a story that moves people and consumer empowerment

> **The idea in one sentence:** once a brand is successful it belongs to its consumers, and the company can only align its actions with a mission that is unusual, told through stories and realized together with people.

## Explanation

Chapter 3 starts from two crises: New Coke (1985), withdrawn in less than three months in the United States but well accepted in Canada, where Coca-Cola did not have iconic status; and IKEA (2009), which changed its font from Futura to Verdana and set off outrage on Twitter. The authors' diagnosis: both companies understood their own brand mission less well than their consumers did. Coca-Cola stood for the secret of American happiness; IKEA stood for making stylish furniture affordable for smart consumers.

A good mission has three characteristics (Fig. 3.1):

1. **Business as Unusual** (Anita Roddick's phrase) *creates* the mission: a small idea that can change a lot, seen through strategic foresight. The mission comes before profit (Amazon.com earned its first profit only in 2001), it is about transformation, and it is simple enough to allow a flexible business scope.
2. **A story that moves people** *spreads* the mission (detailed in STO-004).
3. **Consumer empowerment** *realizes* the mission. The power of a one-to-one network grows as n² (Metcalfe's Law), but that of a many-to-many network grows as 2ⁿ (Reed's Law). That is why, for the authors, conversation is worth more than a simple recommendation: the Net Promoter Score measures word of mouth, but conversation is many-to-many.

## Application

- The test of a mission: (1) what does it do that is unusual in the category? (2) what story carries it? (3) what can consumers concretely do to accomplish it?
- When a business decision (cost, design, recipe) touches the symbols of the mission, check its impact as you would for a rebranding.
- Do not buy conversation: bogus reviews get a brand dubbed one of the "phoniness-generating machines" (Pine & Gilmore).

## Example

Google's Project 10^100 (2008) asked the public for ideas on how to help as many people as possible, with $10 million for implementation. Tide's Loads of Hope brought free mobile laundromats to disaster sites, with the help of donations and volunteers. The Saab community kept telling its stories even when the brand was about to be closed.

## Questions

- A survey quoted in the book (Jack and Suzy Welch) shows that 60 percent of the participating CEOs did not have a mission statement. Does that matter to buyers?
- How much can a brand change without "betraying" the mission its consumers have made their own?
