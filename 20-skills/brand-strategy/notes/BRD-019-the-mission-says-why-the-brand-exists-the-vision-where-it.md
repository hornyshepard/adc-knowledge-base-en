# The mission says why the brand exists, the vision where it wants to go, and the values how it behaves on the way

> **The idea in one sentence:** the mission is the company's reason for being, the vision is the picture of the desirable future state, and the values are the standards of behavior that connect them; each answers a different question, and in practice they are often confused.

## Explanation

Kotler, Kartajaya and Setiawan (*Marketing 3.0*) give the most precise definitions, each with a symbol:

| Element | The question | The definition | The symbol |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Mission** | why? | the reason for being, the company's basic purpose for existence | Charles Handy's doughnut: the core is fixed, the operations around it are flexible but aligned with the core |
| **Vision** | what? | the picture of the desirable future state: what the company aspires to become | the compass that guides it |
| **Values** | how? | the institutional standards of behavior | the wheel that reinforces itself through practice |

The authors prefer a mission defined in enduring terms, not as the business scope, which changes quickly. Following Drucker, good businesses start with the performance of their mission, and financial returns come as results.

Carl Damgaard (*The Unbeatable Brand Blueprint*, a weak source, used here only for examples) separates the same three elements: Nike's mission (inspiration and innovation for every athlete), Tesla's vision (driving the world's transition to electric vehicles), Patagonia's values (environmental activism, quality, integrity). **Where the sources diverge:** for Kotler the mission is rooted in the past, when the company was established, and the vision is about inventing the future; for Damgaard the mission is the present-day action plan. Both agree that values matter only as lived behavior. Damgaard's examples also show how often the elements are confused: for Amazon, the mission and the vision he quotes are identical.

*Kellogg on Branding* (ch. 17) adds a quality criterion: *The Economist* praised McDonald's mission, "To be our customers' favorite place and way to eat and drink", precisely because it is short and credible, while many statements end up as pious platitudes.

## Application

- In a workshop with the client, write the mission, the vision and 3–5 values separately; if two say the same thing, one is useless.
- For each value, ask for an example of a real decision where it cost something (money, time, a client).
- Test the mission with a frontline employee: can they explain it in their own words?

## Example

Google: the mission is to organize the world's information and make it universally accessible and useful; the vision is to provide access to the world's information in one click.

## Questions

- How does the mission differ from purpose in Stengel's model?
- Is it worth a small firm writing a vision, or is it an exercise for corporations?
