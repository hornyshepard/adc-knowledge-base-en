# The Mexico case: countering a negative story only brings you back to neutral ground

> **The idea in one sentence:** when the press tells a bad story about you, data that counters it only continues the conversation; to change perceptions you need a new positive story and a real product that backs it.

## Explanation

In 2009–2010 Mexico went through a "perfect storm": the H1N1 flu, fighting among drug cartel gangs (concentrated in a few areas) and the financial crisis in the United States. Tourism, the third-largest contributor to the economy and 16 percent of GDP, lost four million international visitors. Gloria Guevara, appointed secretary of tourism in 2010, started from an insight: data showing that the violence was localized did not help, because it kept the conversation on violence. They needed to **change the conversation**.

The four-step plan:
1. **Inventory and alignment**: a coalition of government and private sector; 2011 declared the "Year of Tourism," a national agreement backed by all 32 governors.
2. **Diversification** of the product and the targets, beyond "sun, sand, and burritos" for Americans: ten "Routes of Mexico" (culture, adventure, gastronomy, luxury), including the Ruta Maya tied to the end of the Maya calendar cycle in 2012; simpler visas.
3. **Investment**, public and private, in infrastructure and experiences.
4. **Rebranding**: "Mexico, the place you thought you knew" for the United States and Canada, "Mexico Today" for Europe; more than 800 interviews in 12 months, bloggers, celebrities, live feeds in Times Square and the "Mexico Taxi Project": a hidden camera recording what tourists told taxi drivers on the way to the airport.

Results: record international arrivals from 2011 on; tourists from Russia up 85 percent, from Brazil up 51 percent; the U.S. share fell from 70 percent to 50 percent; media mentions went from 70 percent negative to 77 percent positive or neutral.

The author's five lessons: control your narrative; embrace brand management principles; diversification is crucial; personal voices (testimonials) are powerful; be prepared for the next crisis.

Guevara's insight has a psychological explanation, from a classic study by Tybout, Calder and Sternthal (1981) cited by Fennis and Stroebe: in the McDonald's rumor case, the "it is not true" argument reactivated the link between the brand and the negative idea, and the solution was to fill the brand's network with other associations (INS-041).

## Application

- In crisis communication, once you have answered the facts, build a positive story of your own instead of repeating the denial.
- Tie the campaign to a new, concrete product (routes, experiences); without a product the story is not credible.
- Use real testimonials, filmed naturally, not actors.

## Example

The U.S. campaign openly acknowledges the audience's prejudice ("the place you thought you knew") and turns it into curiosity.

## Questions

- What could a Romanian city or region with a negative image learn from this case?
- Where is the ethical line between "changing the conversation" and hiding real problems?
