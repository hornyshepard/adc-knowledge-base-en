# The brand is an asset, not an expense, and its strength is not its performance

> **The idea in one sentence:** marketing and finance can agree only if they treat the brand as an intangible asset and separate the brand's strength in the consumer's mind from its actual contribution to choice in the market.

## Explanation

Bobby Calder describes the classic conflict: marketing talks about targeting, positioning and engagement; finance treats branding expenditures as **expenses** used up in the current period, and so as a cost to reduce. Calder suggests speaking of **brand strength** (the brand's value to the consumer) instead of *brand equity*, a term finance may misread as a financial one.

The solution: the brand as an **intangible asset**, a resource controlled by the company (through its branding activities and its trademark rights) from which future economic benefits are expected to flow. The valuation methods have limits:

- **Market value** (the price paid in acquisitions): accountants accept it in M&A, but the value then stays frozen on the balance sheet, and the price paid also reflects other motives (acquiring companies often pay too much).
- **The income method**: discounted flows attributed to the brand; the preferred version is *relief from royalty* (what you would pay to lease the brand).
- **The direct approaches** of consulting companies (BrandZ: meaningful, different, salient; Interbrand): at least 39 models, often not transparent and not consistent with one another.

The key idea: **strength is not performance**. A loved brand can still perform poorly in the market because of price or distribution. That is why a **brand performance evaluation** is needed (the ISO 20671 standard), which isolates the brand's contribution to choice. MASB's project on 33 brands found that choice explained about 75 percent of the variation in sales, with the remainder due to price and distribution. Stated-preference methods (conjoint analysis, discrete choice experiments) separate the brand from the other factors: in an illustrative example, the Starbucks brand had a contribution of .276, against .470 for price and .249 for distribution.

A rare voice from finance confirms the view: Denise Beckles, a former CFO with experience in mergers and acquisitions (interviewed in *Goddess Guide to Branding*, SRC-016), says "I don't believe you can cut your way to success," that the numbers are an expression of the brand's strength and that, when she evaluates a company, she looks at margins and at how engaged customers are, not just at revenue growth.

## Application

- When you present a brand investment to a CFO, use the language of assets (future flows, risk), not just marketing metrics.
- Propose a conjoint-type test that shows how much the brand weighs against price and availability.
- Do not confuse growth in awareness or engagement with growth in financial value.

## Example

Nestlé paid $7.2 billion just to license the Starbucks brand for use on coffee (Introduction): a clear example of brand value with no physical assets.

## Questions

- Why does accounting refuse to increase the value of an internally developed brand?
- What risks arise when the consultancy that values the brand is also the one that advises it?
