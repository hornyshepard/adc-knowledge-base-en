# A new brand is built in four steps: hook, name, trademark, design

> **The idea in one sentence:** a brand created from scratch has to start from a real hook (something curiously strong about the product, a story or a purpose), then it gets a name, the name is cleared legally, and design brings it to life.

## Explanation

Paul Earle holds that a superb brand wrapped around an average product will outperform the inverse every time, and that e-commerce makes it possible for practically every category to be shaken up by a new brand. His method has four steps:

**1. The hook.** A new brand is never arbitrary. Look for the hook in three places:
- **Product**: what is unique, special, even a little weird about it? The "Altoids convention": what is "curiously strong" about your product? If there is nothing, you have a product problem, not a brand problem.
- **Story**: a scene (High Noon vodka: people enjoying each other's company outside, under blue skies, imagined before the name) or a founder origin story (Mush goes back to the co-founder's childhood practice of soaking her breakfast cereal in milk overnight).
- **Purpose**: articulated at the beginning, not at the end; consumers want brands they can join, not simply buy. What is happening right now that moves people's hearts?

**2. The name**: relevant, emotional, easy to say, marketable (see the note on naming).

**3. The trademark.** Over 400,000 new trademark applications a year in the United States alone; the name you love has probably been taken already. Rights are specific to a class of goods; the deciding standard is "confusingly similar." Unusual spellings can pass (Yoplait "Plenti"). The rule is "use it or lose it": a mark not in use for three years can be challenged.

**4. The design.** Logo, colors, packaging: design gives depth of meaning to an otherwise lifeless word, and it is a selling tool even before the product exists.

## Application

- In the launch workshop, work on the hook first (product, story, purpose) and only then on the name.
- Ask the Altoids question about every one of the client's products.
- Check that the trademark and the domain are available before you fall in love with a name.

## Example

Craig Dubitsky won over retail buyers and investors for Hello toothpaste with only the name, its backstory and the packaging, before he had a finished product. The angel's wings painted on the back of the Angel's Envy bottle seem to glow when backlit on a bar shelf.

## Questions

- What do you do when the product has nothing "curiously strong" about it?
- Is a purpose stated from the start a constraint or an advantage for a startup?
