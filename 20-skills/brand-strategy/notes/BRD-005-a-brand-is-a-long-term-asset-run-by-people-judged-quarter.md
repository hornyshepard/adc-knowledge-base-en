# A brand is a long-term asset run by people judged quarter by quarter: the Three Cs of branding and the branding doom loop

> **The idea in one sentence:** brands are long-term assets managed by people rewarded for the short term, and the promotions that save the quarter can weaken the brand in a downward spiral.

## Explanation

Tim Calkins sums up the difficulty of building brands in the "Three Cs of branding":

| C | The problem |
|---|---|
| **Cash** | Executives are judged on quarterly results, but virtually all of a brand's value resides in the future. The costs of investing in the brand are certain and immediate; the benefits are uncertain and in the future. |
| **Consistency** | The brand is formed at every touchpoint, from the receptionist to customer service. The CMO can't tell the cashier to smile, and in the hyper-connected era a small moment can go viral. |
| **Clutter** | People receive hundreds of messages a day. Breaking through the noise takes tactical excellence (data, a well-deployed budget) and creativity (stories that give people a reason to care). |

The most dangerous consequence of the first C is the **branding doom loop**. A manager with weak results launches price promotions or tactical advertising, funded from the budgets for brand building, quality and innovation. The quarter is saved, but: competitors respond, customers get used to the promoted price (a buy-one-get-one-free offer no longer excites the third time), and the brand weakens. Sales drop again, which calls for even more promotions. The irony: the manager who sacrifices the brand for short-term profit is often rewarded, while the one who invests in it is penalized.

Other sources confirm the mechanism: at Old Spice, the buy-one-get-one-free deals run alongside the repositioning campaign essentially eliminated its profit impact (Grewal and Levy), and Percy and Rosenbaum-Elliott ask that every promotion carry the same benefit as the advertising, so that it does not work against the brand.

## Application

- When you propose a brand campaign, set realistic expectations from the start about when results will appear, and prepare "quick wins" that justify the investment.
- When the client asks for one more promotion, ask what it does to price expectations and to the brand's associations a year from now.
- Use the Three Cs as a diagnostic grid: which of them is your brand's dominant problem?

## Example

Calkins notes that if a brand delivers a steady stream of cash flow in perpetuity, at a discount rate of 5%, only about 5% of its value resides in the first year. The brands Sony (1946), Tata (1846) and Veuve Clicquot (1772) show how long well-managed assets can live.

## Questions

- How can an agency defend brand investment to a CFO? (see the brand as an asset)
- Are there categories where permanent promotions are the positioning itself (discounters)? Does the doom loop apply there too?
