# Brand architecture runs from branded house to house of brands: the more the products differ, the more they need separate names

> **The idea in one sentence:** a company can put a single name on everything (branded house) or own separate brands (house of brands), with sub-brands, endorsers and ingredient brands in between; the choice depends on how different the products and the audiences are.

## Explanation

Tim Calkins (Kellogg) gives the vocabulary of the brand portfolio:

- **Primary brand**: the main name, the most prominent on the package; the simplest structure is a primary brand plus a product description (Google search engine).
- **Sub-brand**: a secondary brand that falls below the primary brand in prominence: Honda Civic. If it becomes more prominent than the primary brand, it becomes the primary brand (Ram, once under Dodge).
- **Endorser brand**: a distinct brand with a connection to another brand: Courtyard by Marriott, Marcus by Goldman Sachs.
- **Ingredient and service brands**: the Hemi engine, Westin's Heavenly Bed. They are legally protected, so they become a point of difference that, unlike a patent, can last forever.

| | House of brands | Branded house |
|---|---|---|
| Examples | P&G (Tide, Gillette, Always), LVMH | Virgin, Uber |
| Advantages | each brand precisely targeted; easy to build a global business | focus on the brand, efficiency, scale |
| Risks | complexity, small brands lacking scale | dilution; good ideas dropped because they don't fit under the brand's umbrella |

Grewal and Levy describe the same axis as **family brands** (Kraft Singles) versus **individual brands** (Philadelphia, Jell-O, not readily regarded as falling under the Kraft umbrella) and give a decision rule: the more the products vary in their usage or performance, the more likely it is that the firm should use individual brands. General Motors has Cadillac, Chevrolet and Buick for very different target markets; Hyundai uses one brand for cars that are relatively homogeneous. They add a second decision: **who owns the brand**, the manufacturer (national brand) or the retailer (store brand, private label). Store brands have grown with the scale of retail firms; Costco and Trader Joe's base their brand identities on them.

Calkins's rule: if you can't explain a brand structure in a sentence or two, it is too complex.

## Application

- Before you name a new product, decide where it sits: descriptor, sub-brand, endorsed brand or new brand.
- Use an endorsement when the new product needs the parent's credibility but could damage it.
- Propose an ingredient brand when the client has a technical advantage that is easy to copy.
- For a retailer's private label, the communication has to build an identity, not just announce a low price.

## Example

Novant Health (ch. 20) had become a classic house of brands, operating under 400 different brand names for hospitals and clinics; patients did not know they belonged to the same system. Unifying them under a single brand tripled brand awareness in five years. In the opposite direction, Marriott keeps its luxury hotels (Ritz-Carlton, Edition) away from its own name, so the associations do not mix.

## Questions

- When is it better to launch a new brand than a sub-brand (Toyota → Lexus)?
- Why would a manufacturer agree to produce for the private label of a retailer that competes with it?
