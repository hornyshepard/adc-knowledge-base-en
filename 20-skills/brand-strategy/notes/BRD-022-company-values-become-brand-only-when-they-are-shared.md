# Company values become brand only when they are shared values lived in employees' behavior

> **The idea in one sentence:** employees are the most intimate "consumers" of a company's practices and spot fake values at once, so values matter only when they are aligned with a common, everyday behavior.

## Explanation

Chapter 4 opens with Enron and AIG, which listed "respect" and "integrity" among their official values but did not practice them. To avoid such empty values, the authors use Patrick Lencioni's typology:

- **permission-to-play values**: the basic standards of conduct (professionalism, integrity); everyone has them, so they do not differentiate;
- **aspirational values**: what management hopes to achieve, but the company still lacks;
- **accidental values**: acquired from the common personality traits of employees; they can alienate people with different personalities;
- **core values**: the real corporate culture that guides employees' actions.

Only core values, which the authors call **shared values**, form one half of the corporate culture; the other half is the **common behavior** of employees. In Marketing 3.0, good values are collaborative, cultural and creative (Table 4.1): 3M (collaborative curiosity, time for pet projects), IDEO (multidisciplinary creativity), S.C. Johnson (family values: no meetings on Fridays), Wegmans (passion for food: employees trained to be food ambassadors), Whole Foods (democracy: decisions made through employee voting).

The gains: attracting talent (in an influential 1997 McKinsey survey, 58 percent of executives rated brand values and culture as the key motivation for employees, against 39 percent for career advancement and 29 percent for differentiated compensation), productivity, the quality of contact with the customer (living the brand, in Nicholas Ind's phrase) and managing differences (Enterprise Rent-A-Car).

## Application

- In an employer branding project, remove the permission-to-play values and the merely aspirational ones from the list of values.
- For each value, look for a **mechanism** that links it to action (Jim Collins): for example, 30 percent of revenues from new products for the value of innovation.
- Tell stories to employees too, not only to customers. But be careful: one misaligned action spoils the entire internal story.

## Example

Bagel Works, which counts health and safety among its values, buys smaller bags of flour, although they are more expensive, to protect its employees from back injuries (p. 95).

## Questions

- Can publicly listed companies keep their values as well as privately held firms, which grow without pressure from investors?
- What happens to employee commitment after an acquisition (Ben & Jerry's under Unilever, The Body Shop under L'Oréal)?
