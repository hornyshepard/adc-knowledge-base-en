# Timing of entry: pioneer, fast follower or late mover

> **The idea in one sentence:** pioneers, fast followers and late movers win through the same mechanism (they shape what consumers learn to prefer); each simply does it at a different stage of the market.

## Explanation

Gregory Carpenter and Kent Nakamoto start from the idea that most preferences are learned. Market-driving firms do more than discover the rules of the game: they change them (De Beers transformed carbon crystals into symbols of love and devotion).

**The pioneer.** In one study of consumer-goods markets, after adjusting for differences in marketing strategy, the second entrant earns on average 71 percent of the pioneer's market share, the third 58 percent, the sixth less than half. The advantage has three sources: a pioneer is a low-risk choice (later entrants are untried); consumers remember pioneers (they devote more thought to learning about the first brand); and a pioneer sets category standards, becoming prototypical (Coca-Cola, Google, Uber). Paradoxically, comparing yourself with the pioneer strengthens it: Pepsi's taste-test advertising raised Pepsi's market share, but Coca-Cola's rose more.

**The fast follower.** Golder and Tellis (50 product categories) found that the typical pioneer retained its leadership position for five years, while early followers led in 53 percent of the markets examined. Fast followers enter as the category accelerates, grow fastest and have better access to capital (Samsung; GE against EMI in CT scanners; Facebook against MySpace).

**The late mover** has two paths:
- **"Just different enough."** Position as close as possible to the pioneer, but far enough away that the pioneer cannot imitate you without contradicting its own image. Toyota positioned Lexus close to Mercedes-Benz, but with excellent service that Mercedes-Benz could not easily imitate.
- **Innovation.** A late mover with a meaningful innovation restarts consumer learning, creates a larger potential market and more loyal customers, and its success reduces the effectiveness of the pioneer's marketing. Google entered after nine other firms; Starbucks reinvented the coffee house four centuries after Kiva Han, "educating" its customers' palates.

Fennis and Stroebe (*The Psychology of Advertising*) use the same Carpenter and Nakamoto research to explain prototypicality (INS-013); Kellogg adds the competitive view of the brands that come after the pioneer.

## Application

- For a pioneer brand: invest in becoming synonymous with the category and in setting the standard of comparison.
- For a challenger: avoid "we are just like the leader" advertising; look for the right distance, or for an innovation that changes the criteria.
- In the brief, state what stage the category is in: it changes the job of the communication (education vs. differentiation).

## Example

Intuitive Surgical (the da Vinci system, 2000) has sold over 4,000 systems and has become the standard against which surgeons compare any competitor.

## Questions

- Why do the Golder and Tellis data seem to contradict the pioneer advantage? What does each study measure differently?
- How should a late mover communicate when it has no technological innovation?
