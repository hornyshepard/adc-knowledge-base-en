# Brand personality has five measurable dimensions (Aaker) and matters through its fit with the customer's self-image

> **The idea in one sentence:** people attribute human traits to brands, which Jennifer Aaker grouped into five dimensions (sincerity, excitement, competence, sophistication, ruggedness), and customers choose the brands whose personality fits how they see themselves or want to be seen.

## Explanation

Brand personality is the set of human characteristics associated with a brand. Jennifer Aaker (*Journal of Marketing Research*, 1997) built the model in line with the "big five" factor structure of human personality and made it measurable with the brand personality scale:

| Dimension | Traits | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Sincerity** | ethical, trustworthy, down-to-earth | Patagonia |
| **Excitement** | bold, creative, spirited | Red Bull, Tesla |
| **Competence** | intelligent, successful, confident | Hugo Boss, Volvo |
| **Sophistication** | glamorous, upper-class, charming | Chanel |
| **Ruggedness** | adventurous, tough, outdoorsy | Range Rover, Harley-Davidson |

Fennis and Stroebe (*The Psychology of Advertising*) explain why it matters. A brand's personality reflects the stereotypic image people have of the typical user of that brand: Golf and Mini are both small cars, but the Mini driver is seen as sportier. Consumers choose brands congruent with their self-image, and owning them helps them be the kind of person they want to be: driving a Range Rover conveys ruggedness to others and also makes the driver feel that way. Experiments confirm the effect: a person wearing Hugo Boss is judged more competent than one wearing a low-competence brand; participants primed subliminally with the Apple logo produced more creative answers than those primed with the IBM logo, although the authors doubt the finding would be replicated today.

Damgaard (a weak source) adds only the link to visual identity: the colors, fonts and logo have to express the same personality. The model's limit: it was developed on American consumers.

## Application

- Choose one dominant dimension and at most one secondary one; a brand that is "sincere, exciting, competent, sophisticated and rugged" has no personality.
- Translate the dimension into tone-of-voice rules (words allowed and forbidden) and into visual style rules.
- Check the fit with the target audience: how does the customer see themselves, and how do they want to be seen when they use the brand?

## Example

Apple devoted a major proportion of its advertising budget, with the "Think Different" campaign, to a personality of nonconformity, innovation and creativity; IBM, by contrast, was not particularly associated with creativity, and the difference showed up even in laboratory experiments.

## Questions

- Which dimension would a Romanian digital bank brand have? And a mineral water brand?
- Can a brand's personality change without repositioning?
