# Repositioning is expensive and risky: it is justified only in a few clear situations and succeeds more often by speaking to a new audience

> **The idea in one sentence:** changing the position of a brand that already has traction costs a lot, can alienate the core users and fails if the new position is not credible or if the firm has mistaken a fad for a trend, so it is a strategy of last resort.

## Explanation

Grewal and Levy define brand repositioning (rebranding) as changing a brand's focus to target new markets or to realign it with changing market preferences. The costs are tangible (products, packages) and intangible (promoting the new image). The main risks: the message is not credible, or the firm has mistaken a fad for a long-term market trend.

Alice Tybout (Kellogg) spells out when the step is warranted:

1. **The pioneer of a new category.** Miller Lite used regular beer as its frame; when Bud Light arrived, the frame became light beer, and "less filling" became a point of parity.
2. **The position is too broad for the reason to believe.** Aleve promised to relieve any pain, but 12-hour dosing implied slow acting; narrowed to arthritis pain, the same dosing became an advantage.
3. **The initial position failed to gain traction.** The Apple Watch, launched as a fashion item, was reframed as a device for health and fitness.
4. **The position has been diluted** through licensing or "hijacked" by consumers who are not the desired target (Burberry in the early 2000s).

Her counterexample: Old Navy cut back the very breadth of selection that was the reason to believe for families, and sales declined over 20 percent.

**Where the sources converge.** Both books use Old Spice. Grewal and Levy describe the "dad-like" brand that was losing to Axe and the dual targeting, because women frequently select and buy the personal care products that the men in their households use. Julie Hennessy generalizes: successful repositionings (Old Spice, IBM, Stella Artois, Havaianas) rarely convince the old users that they were wrong; they speak to a **new target**. The higher the awareness, the harder the change. Grewal and Levy add that repositioning is more than a campaign: Old Spice added new products, Weight Watchers became WW and changed its program, and the $45,000 logo of the city of Frederick, Maryland, was dropped after residents complained.

## Application

- First exhaust the strategies for sustaining a position (modern instantiation, laddering) and name explicitly the situation you are in.
- Check two things: is the trend real and lasting? Is the new position credible for this brand?
- Move the product, the range and the touchpoints along with the message; test new brand elements with the loyal audience.
- Do not let promotions cancel the effect: at Old Spice, the buy one, get one free deals run at the same time essentially eliminated the potential profit impact.

## Example

Old Spice ("The Man Your Man Could Smell Like") is the full case: diagnosis (Axe), new target, insight (who actually buys), self-mocking tone, expanded range, conversation on social media.
