# The positioning benefit is chosen with the expectancy-value model, which shows which benefits matter, who delivers them and where you can shift importance

> **The idea in one sentence:** brand attitude is the sum of the beliefs about the brand, each weighted by its importance; by breaking it down, you see exactly which benefit has to be communicated.

## Explanation

The benefit you look for has to meet three conditions (Box 8.1): it is **important** to the target, the target believes (or can be persuaded) that the brand **delivers** it, and that it delivers it **better** than others. The recommended tool is Fishbein's multi-attribute model:

**A₀ = Σ aᵢ · bᵢ**, where aᵢ is the importance weight of the benefit (3 = essential, 1 = desirable, 0 = of no importance), and bᵢ is the belief that the brand delivers it (3 = definitely delivers, 1 = does okay, 0 = does not deliver).

The authors' hypothetical example, with chocolate bars (Table 8.4):

| Benefit | Importance | Snickers | Cadbury |
|---|---|---|---|
| Chocolate | 3 | 3 | 3 |
| Indulgence | 3 | 1 | 3 |
| Caramel | 1 | 1 | 0 |
| Peanuts | 1 | 3 | 0 |
| Inexpensive snack | 1 | 3 | 3 |
| Provides energy | 0 | 1 | 1 |
| **Total** | | **19** | **21** |

The score alone does not mean much. What matters is the breakdown. If Snickers were perceived as an indulgence, it would jump to 25 and overtake Cadbury. Alternatively, if the importance of indulgence dropped to "desirable", Snickers would lead 17 to 15, and with caramel played up, 19 to 15. Among chocolate lovers, who see Snickers as more about caramel and peanuts than about chocolate, the score drops to 13. A strong chocolate positioning would win this segment without losing the others.

The strategic options the model shows:

1. strengthen or build uniqueness on important benefits;
2. exploit competitors' weaknesses on important benefits;
3. emphasize the important benefits you deliver better;
4. increase the importance of the benefits you are better at, if they are not already essential;
5. decrease the importance of the benefits you are not better at.

The model is not specific to advertising: Fennis and Stroebe present it as a general theory of attitude (INS-021). Percy and Rosenbaum-Elliott's contribution is to turn it into a tool for choosing the positioning.

## Application

- Run the exercise on the target audience, not on the general population, with your brand and two or three key competitors.
- Include the "potentially important" benefits too, not only those declared today.
- Translate the chosen option into a single benefit sentence for the brief.

## Example

A mineral water brand with a weak score on "taste" but a good one on "mineralization for athletes" can choose option 4: increase the importance of mineralization for the active segment, instead of trying to win on taste.

## Questions

- What are the limits of an additive model, when a single negative belief can cancel all the others (ch. 9)?
- How do you apply the model to emotional benefits, which are hard to put on a scale from 0 to 3?
