# The ideal brand community is distinct, exclusive and intermediate in size

> **The idea in one sentence:** a brand community that is too small does not deliver belonging, one that is too large no longer delivers distinctiveness; the greatest engagement comes in between, especially when there is a clear "foil".

## Explanation

Roese and Gardner's fifth principle for self-brand connections rests on optimal distinctiveness theory. Two needs sit in a state of dynamic tension: **communion** (to belong) and **agency** or distinctiveness (to be different). As a group becomes more inclusive, the need for inclusion is satisfied but the need for differentiation is activated; as it becomes more exclusive, the reverse happens.

Hence a U-shaped relationship between the size of the community and engagement: the most loyal communities are **relatively small**, with clear boundaries (who is in, who is out). Group psychology shows that numerical minorities foster greater group identification than majorities. A study the authors cite (Abrams) found the greatest engagement among fans of music genres that were neither too mainstream nor too niche.

The second ingredient is a **brand foil**: a competitor or a culture that clearly defines what the brand is not. The Harley-Davidson community of the 1950s and 1960s defined itself against conformist, middle-class society; the Apple community of the 1980s and 1990s against IBM and Microsoft. These were the periods of greatest passion.

The paradox of success: a brand that becomes market leader finds it harder and harder to keep its community. Leaders do not name their competitors (Coca-Cola never mentions Pepsi), and the size of the community no longer meets the need for distinctiveness. The authors see today's Apple as a victim of its own success.

Parsons, Maclaran and Chatzidakis describe the same communities from a sociological perspective (following Muniz and O'Guinn): consciousness of kind, shared rituals and traditions, and moral responsibility toward other members (SOC-015). Roese and Gardner add what is missing there: the optimal size and the role of the foil.

## Application

- When you build an online community, design rituals, language and entry thresholds that create a distinct "us".
- State the brand's cultural foil explicitly (an attitude, not necessarily a named competitor).
- For large brands, create sub-communities (by passion, city, level of experience) that keep the feeling of a small group.

## Example

Tough Mudder (ch. 10) grew for its first six years using only Facebook, because its target (military personnel, firefighters, police engaged in extreme physical activity) was already a close-knit community.

## Questions

- How do you reconcile the goal of growing sales with the "optimal" size of the community?
- Can a mass brand have a genuinely passionate community?
