# An innovation spreads through five groups of adopters, and the speed depends on five characteristics of the product

> **The idea in one sentence:** after Everett Rogers, the adoption of an innovation follows a bell-shaped curve (2.5% innovators, 13.5% early adopters, 34% early majority, 34% late majority, 16% laggards), and the marketer can speed up diffusion by working on relative advantage, compatibility, observability, simplicity and trialability.

## Explanation

**Who adopts and when** (Exhibit 12.2):

| Group | Share | Profile and role |
|---|---|---|
| Innovators | 2.5% | want to be first, love risk; through word of mouth they bring in the next group |
| Early adopters | 13.5% | wait for the first reviews, but love novelty; they are opinion leaders |
| Early majority | 34% | avoid risk; without them the product does not become profitable |
| Late majority | 34% | come in when the product has reached its potential |
| Laggards | 16% | avoid change; some never adopt |

**What speeds up diffusion**: relative advantage (Swiffer makes cleaning faster), compatibility with needs and culture (Electrolux's small, quiet vacuum for Asian apartments), observability (Blendtec, "Will It Blend?"), low complexity and trialability (Dyson's test areas). Products that people do not talk about diffuse more slowly, precisely because they are hard to observe.

**Pioneers** can win a large and lasting share, but imitators profit from their mistakes and their high prices. Only about 5% of new products succeed; failures come from insufficient testing, the wrong segment, weak positioning or expanding beyond the firm's competencies.

**Echoes in other sources.** Kotler (Marketing 3.0) uses the same curve for green products and adds Moore's "chasm" between the early market and the mainstream (MKT-066). Von Scheel draws an almost identical 5/15/80 curve, without citing Rogers (MKT-056). In consumer psychology, the pioneer advantage is stronger than the textbook makes it seem: the first brand becomes the prototype of the category (INS-013).

## Application

- Plan the launch by group: first the innovators and early adopters (communities, creators, niche PR), then the mass market (broad media, social proof).
- If the product is hard to observe, make it visible: demonstrations, challenges, content that shows the result.
- Use the five characteristics as a checklist for the adoption barrier that the communication has to solve.

## Example

"Will It Blend?" turned a $400 blender into an observable product: a spokesperson in a white lab coat blends iPads and golf balls, with more than 285 million views.

## Questions

- The percentages come from a theoretical normal distribution. Do they fit a real market?
- Are today's early adopters the influencers? What is lost if we treat them the same way?
