# Marketing 3.0 treats the customer as a whole human being: mind, heart and spirit

> **The idea in one sentence:** in Marketing 3.0, companies no longer differentiate only through the product (1.0) or through customer satisfaction (2.0) but through values, offering a "spiritual" benefit on top of the functional and emotional ones: a contribution to a better world.

## Explanation

Kotler, Kartajaya and Setiawan describe marketing as a sequence of stages that respond to macroeconomic change (Table 1.1):

| | 1.0: product-centric | 2.0: consumer-oriented | 3.0: values-driven |
|---|---|---|---|
| Objective | sell products | satisfy and retain the consumers | make the world a better place |
| How companies see the market | mass buyers with physical needs | smarter consumer with mind and heart | whole human with mind, heart and spirit |
| Key marketing concept | product development | differentiation | values |
| Value propositions | functional | functional and emotional | functional, emotional and spiritual |
| Interaction with consumers | one-to-many transaction | one-to-one relationship | many-to-many collaboration |

Three forces drive the shift to 3.0: **the age of participation** (consumers become *prosumers* and call for collaborative marketing), **the globalization paradox** (political, economic and cultural anxieties that call for cultural marketing) and **the creative society** (people who look for meaning, hence human spirit marketing). Segmentation, positioning and the 4Ps remain, but they are no longer enough.

**How to read it today.** The "value propositions" row extends the textbook definition of value (benefits relative to costs) with a third layer of benefit. The series itself revises its table: in Marketing 5.0, satisfaction becomes the goal of 1.0, and 3.0 is called "the ultimate stage of traditional marketing" (MKT-048).

## Application

- When you analyze a brand, ask at what level it speaks: does it solve a need (mind), create attachment (heart) or answer a social anxiety (spirit)?
- Write the value proposition on three lines: functional, emotional, spiritual. If the third is empty or sounds like a CSR slogan, the brand is still 2.0.
- Look for the target's collective anxiety, not only the individual need.

## Example

The book illustrates the three levels: Volvo took the mind with "safety", Starbucks the heart with its "third place", and The Body Shop the spirit, with social activism built into its business model.

## Questions

- Does every category need a "spiritual" benefit? What happens with low-involvement products?
- Where does values marketing end and *purpose-washing* begin? How would you measure the "spirit"?
