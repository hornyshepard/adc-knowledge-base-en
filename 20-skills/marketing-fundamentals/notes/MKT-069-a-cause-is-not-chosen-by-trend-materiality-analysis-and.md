# A cause is not chosen by trend: materiality analysis and alignment with the mission

> **The idea in one sentence:** a credible cause is one that matters both to stakeholders and to the business's real impact. Materiality analysis puts both on a matrix, and the causes in the upper right quadrant are the ones to pursue.

## Explanation

Viturino describes a process in three parts (ch. 2 and 4):

**1. Materiality analysis**: you identify and prioritize the social, environmental and governance issues that matter most to the company and its stakeholders.
- *Stakeholder mapping*: customers, employees, suppliers, investors, local communities.
- *Data collection*: surveys, interviews, workshops.
- *Impact assessment* of the company's own operations on each issue.
- *Prioritization* on a matrix with two axes: importance to stakeholders and impact on the company. The issues in the upper right quadrant are the most important.

**2. Market research and alignment with the mission**: opinion surveys, analysis of emerging trends, benchmarking. Then a review of the mission and vision, internal consultation, and integration of the cause into objectives and KPIs.

**3. Implementation**: partnerships with aligned NGOs, with shared measurable goals and formal agreements. Community engagement (consultations, joint projects), volunteer programs, transparent communication and measurement of results (money raised, people helped, engagement, change in brand perception).

**Convergence.** Kotler (Marketing 3.0) recommends almost the same selection, on three criteria: relevance to the mission and values, business impact and social impact (MKT-064). The materiality matrix puts them on two measurable axes.

## Application

- Before you propose a cause in a pitch, draw the matrix for the client: where does the company have real impact (for example water, packaging, working conditions in the supply chain), and what matters to its audience?
- Reject "fashionable" causes with no link to operations: they are the easiest to attack as opportunism.

## Example

The book gives three models (ch. 4): Ben & Jerry's (climate justice, immigrant rights, the "Justice ReMix'd" flavor about criminal justice), Patagonia (1% of sales to environmental groups, repairs through Worn Wear) and TOMS ("One for One": for every pair sold, one donated to a child in need, later extended to water, vision and mental health).

## Questions

- The "One for One" model is presented without criticism. What objections can you raise about the effect of product donations on local economies? Look for independent evaluations.
- Who finally decides the cause: the stakeholders, management or the agency?
