# The social business enterprise at the bottom of the pyramid calls for a redesigned marketing mix

> **The idea in one sentence:** poverty is reduced for good through entrepreneurship, not aid, and a company that wants to serve the bottom of the pyramid has to rethink every element of its marketing, from segmentation to process.

## Explanation

The goal is to transform the structure of wealth from a **pyramid** into a **diamond**, with a wider middle class. For the authors, foreign aid is like feeding people fish without teaching them how to fish; the key actors are corporations, working with governments and NGOs. Michael Chu sets four requirements for an innovation that actually reduces poverty: huge scale, solutions that last over generations, effectiveness, efficiency.

The **social business enterprise** (SBE, Muhammad Yunus's term) makes money, but its social objective remains primary. Its success shows in three ways: it **stretches** disposable income (lower prices: Unilever's Annapurna iodized salt), **expands** disposable income (goods not previously available: the XO laptop, essential medicines) and **increases** disposable income (new economic activity: Grameen Phone, with more than 250,000 income opportunities).

The marketing model (Table 8.2):

| Element | In the social business enterprise |
|---|---|
| Segmentation / targeting | bottom of the pyramid / high-volume communities |
| Positioning / differentiation | social business enterprise / social entrepreneurship |
| Product, price | products not currently accessible; affordability, measured as the **Total Purchasing Cost** (including transportation and time), the same idea as the "total sacrifice" in the Grewal-Levy textbook (MKT-024) |
| Promotion, place | word of mouth; community distribution |
| Selling, brand | a sales force of social entrepreneurs; an iconic brand |
| Service, process | no-frills; low cost |

The book also offers a segmentation of low-income consumers, modified from VALS: **believers** (traditional, loyal to familiar brands), **strivers** (driven by social approval, mimic the rich), **makers** (practical, functional), **survivors** (look for bargains).

## Application

- Treat packaging as a pricing tool: the single-use *sachet* has a higher per-item price, but it is affordable.
- Treat informal leaders (teachers, religious leaders, doctors, heads of villages) as credible "media".
- Recruit the sales force from the target community: it understands its peers' behavior best.

## Example

Hindustan Lever's Project Shakti turns thousands of underprivileged rural women into a sales force for products in small sachets, with sales training. Globe Telecom (Philippines) replaced physical top-up cards with over-the-air reloading through licensed individual distributors (p. 166).

## Questions

- Where is the line between serving the bottom of the pyramid and profiting from it (for example, the higher per-item price of sachets)?
- Which elements of the model apply in the poor rural areas of Eastern Europe?
