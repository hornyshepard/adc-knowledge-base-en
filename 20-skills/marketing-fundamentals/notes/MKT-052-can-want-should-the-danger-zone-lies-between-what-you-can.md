# Can–Want–Should: the danger zone lies between what you can do and what you want, when you skip what you should do

> **The idea in one sentence:** a strategic diagnosis compares three circles: what the organization can do, what the leadership wants to do and what should be done under the pressure of external forces; decisions made only from "can" and "want" are the riskiest.

## Explanation

Von Scheel presents "can, want, should do" as a structured sequence to outsmart your own biases, in order to get clarity on what not to do, what to do less of and what to focus on (pp. 191–192):

| Circle | What it contains (according to the author) |
|---|---|
| **CAN: what you can do** | key resources, unique value proposition, core competencies, assets, processes, partnerships |
| **WANT: what you want to do** | personal ideas and preferences, ambitions, value expectations, market research |
| **SHOULD: what you should do** | market changes, competition and new entrants, customer demands, suppliers, regulation |

The intersections give different kinds of opportunity (pp. 192, 195):
- **Can ∩ Should** = opportunities for **transformation**: you adapt with existing capabilities.
- **Should ∩ Want** = opportunities for **innovation**: you invest in new capabilities.
- **Can ∩ Want, without Should** = the **danger zone**. Most executives go straight there; the author compares it to a teenager who decides what's for dinner: pizza every night (p. 194).
- All three = the "sweet spot of efforts".

**Comparison with SWOT** (the note's own reading): "Can" corresponds to strengths and weaknesses, "Should" to opportunities and threats (MKT-007). What is new is the "Want" circle, which makes the leadership's wishes visible; the author also puts market research here, because it tends to reinforce existing conclusions. The claim that he came up with the concept "while working with Steve Jobs" is not documented.

## Application

- Workshop with the client: three flip charts, one for each circle, filled in separately (ideally by different people), then overlaid.
- A filter for the agency's own ideas: a creative idea the team loves (*want*) that does not answer a real change in the market (*should*) is in the danger zone.

## Example

The book's example: operational excellence. You start from what you *can* do in the operating model, find out what you *should* do for a better performance model (where to transform for effectiveness and where to innovate for efficiency), and arrive at the cost savings you *want* (p. 196).

## Questions

- Who should fill in the "Should" circle: the client, the agency or consumers?
- Where would you place market research, if not under "Want"?
