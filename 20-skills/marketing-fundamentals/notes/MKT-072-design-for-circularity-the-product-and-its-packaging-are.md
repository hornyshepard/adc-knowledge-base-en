# Design for circularity: the product and its packaging are thought through from the end of their life

> **The idea in one sentence:** the circular economy replaces the "extract, produce, discard" model with a closed cycle. That starts in the design brief: what the product is made of, how long it lasts, how it is repaired, how it comes apart and what happens to the packaging.

## Explanation

Viturino gathers the practices into a list an art director can use directly (ch. 3):

**The product**
- *Materials*: recycled, recyclable, biodegradable or from sustainable sources (bamboo, organic cotton).
- *Energy efficiency* in use: low consumption, energy-saving modes, built-in renewable energy.
- *Durability and reuse*: design for longevity, easy to repair, modular (components that can be replaced one by one).
- *Design for disassembly*: components that come apart without damage, labeled materials, modularity.

**The packaging**
- *Reduction*: as little material as possible, removing unnecessary components (secondary and tertiary packaging), technologies for lighter packaging.
- *Ecological materials*: bioplastics made from corn starch or sugar cane, compostable materials (ASTM D6400 certification), recyclable materials (paper, glass, aluminum), with clear disposal instructions.
- *Reuse*: refillable jars, return and refill systems, versatile design (the delivery box that becomes a storage box).

**The business model**
- *Leasing / rental*: ownership remains with the company, which gets the product back.
- *Take-back programs* with discounts or credit.
- *Sharing platforms* instead of purchase.

## Application

- Add an "end of life" section to every packaging brief: what does the consumer do with the packaging after use? Is it clearly written on the pack?
- Communicate only what can be verified. "Biodegradable" without conditions (where, in how long) is a vague promise.
- Turn reuse into a brand idea: packaging so good that people keep it also advertises.

## Example

The book's cases: Interface's ReEntry program (used carpets collected, separated and recycled into new carpets), IKEA's Buy Back program (old furniture bought back, resold or recycled), IKEA's KUNGSBACKA line (made from recycled plastic bottles and reused industrial wood) and Nike Grind (old products turned into new materials) (ch. 2–3).

## Questions

- How do you show reduced packaging visually without it looking "cheap"?
- Bioplastics and "compostable" only work if there is a collection infrastructure. Who answers for the promise when it is missing?
