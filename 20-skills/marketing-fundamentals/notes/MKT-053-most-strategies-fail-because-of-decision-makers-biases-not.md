# Most strategies fail because of decision makers' biases, not for lack of ideas

> **The idea in one sentence:** strategies usually die because decision makers make no real choices, overestimate their strengths or mistake one type of opportunity for another; all of these are biases you can anticipate.

## Explanation

Von Scheel starts from a gap between perception and reality: 9 out of 10 strategies are declared a success, but 70% of strategy initiatives are never successfully implemented (pp. 48–50). He calls failure "F.A.I.L." (*First Attempt In Learning*) and ties each cause to a bias (pp. 50–51):

| Cause | Associated bias | Share (per the author) |
|---|---|---|
| No strategy at all | none | 30% |
| No real choice; selection left to happen "organically", bottom-up | confirmation | 18% |
| No follow-through from formulation to execution | emotional | 14% |
| Overestimating strengths | chronic metacognition | 11% |
| Leadership's blind spot; failure to define differentiation | overconfidence | 11% |
| Mistaking transformation for innovation | availability | 10% |
| "Worst practices" turned into best practices | anchoring | 6% |

The remedy has three steps: decide which decisions justify the effort, identify the biases likely to affect them, and embed practices to counter them (p. 52). Among those practices: the **premortem** (you imagine the project has failed and look for the reasons), the momentum case (what happens if the company takes no strategic action), anticipating competitors' moves, and the courage to kill projects (pp. 52–53, 189). The author also notes that 80% of decisions are based on market research, which mainly serves to reinforce existing conclusions (p. 191).

**How to use it.** The percentages come with no published methodology; they are worth something as a checklist, not as evidence. The solid part is the premortem, which does not depend on the numbers.

## Application

- Before you present a recommendation, run a 20-minute premortem with the team: "It is a year later and the campaign has failed. Why?"
- Check whether the research you rely on tested the hypothesis or only confirmed it.

## Example

The availability bias is common in agencies: a client asks for "an innovation like the competitor's", although the real problem is cost or distribution, that is, a matter of transformation.
