# Strategy means three choices (what not to do, where to be different, what to focus on), made in order and then checked as a system

> **The idea in one sentence:** a strategy exists only if it makes explicit choices, and the first choice is giving something up; once you have gone through the three choices in order, you check that they support one another.

## Explanation

Henrik von Scheel defines strategy as "the big picture of how the organization is to win" in its environment and reduces it to three choices, to be made in that order (pp. 57, 164):

| # | The choice | The working question |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **What not to do** | What do we stop, what do we do less, which markets, products or activities do we leave aside? |
| 2 | **Where to be different** | Where are we unique and hard to copy? |
| 3 | **What to focus on** | Where do we put resources: what do we do more of, where do we innovate, when do we transform? |

The order follows the logic of giving up: you free money, people and attention, and differentiation and focus make sense only in a space that has already been cleared. The reverse test is useful: many "strategies" are just presentations, reports and a set of actions with no real choices (p. 50). If a document does not say what it gives up, it is not a strategy.

**A tension in the same book.** A few chapters later (p. 220), the author warns that a common pitfall is to make the choices independently of each other: strategic choices need to be made simultaneously, not sequentially. "Where to play" and "how to win" make sense only together, as matched sets of choices that reinforce one another; the logic is that of *Playing to Win* (Lafley and Martin), uncited. In the same place he separates routine decisions (avoiding biases is enough) from strategic ones (you need clear-eyed analysis and bold action). The reconciliation proposed by the note: the order is a sequence for thinking and presenting, so you do not skip the giving up; simultaneity is the coherence test at the end.

**The value of the source.** The book has low relevance (2/5), but the three choices are one of its few tools worth teaching. The idea of giving up converges with the literature on the brief: a single benefit, not five (BRF-012).

## Application

- In a brand or communication strategy, write the "we don't" list first: segments we do not target, channels we abandon, messages we stop saying.
- Tie choice 2 to the positioning and choice 3 to the single-minded proposition in the brief.
- At the end, check everything at once: do the chosen audience, the proposition and the channels support one another? If you change the audience, rewrite the proposition.

## Example

A regional coffee brand wants "to be everywhere". The strategic version: we do *not* compete on price with the supermarket private labels; we are *different* through local roasting and the traceability of the beans; we *focus* on partner cafés and online subscriptions. The coherence test: do the online subscriptions call for a different target than the cafés? If so, the choices do not yet form a system.

## Questions

- Why is it politically harder to say "we don't" than "we'll also do"?
- When is the strict order dangerous, for example when you give up too early something that turns out to be a differentiator?
