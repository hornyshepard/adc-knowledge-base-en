# The value proposition sits where the customer's needs meet your offering but not the competitor's, and it is written in four elements

> **The idea in one sentence:** the value proposition is the unique benefit the customer gets from you and not from others; you find it with the Grewal-Levy "circles" and you formulate it with the template "To [target], [brand] is the [category] that [difference]".

## Explanation

**How you find it.** Grewal and Levy overlap three circles: customer needs, the firm's benefits and the competitor's benefits (Exhibit 9.8). The result is seven spaces, four of which matter:

| Space | What it is | What you do |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | needs met only by you | this is **your value proposition** (Southwest: you choose your own seat at boarding) |
| 2 | needs met by no one | an opportunity for a new product |
| 3 | your benefits that the customer does not want | educate the customer or redesign the product |
| 4 | needs met both by you and by the competitor | you compete on performance |

The other three spaces belong to the competitor (its value proposition, to be monitored, and the unwanted benefits). Space 1 does not last: competitors copy.

**How you write it.** The statement (Exhibit 9.9) has four elements: the target market, the offering name or brand, the product category and the unique point of difference. For example: "To [athletes around the world] [Gatorade] is the [sports drink]", which gives fuel for working muscles, fluid for hydration and electrolytes before, during and after activity. In advertising, the proposition is often condensed into a USP.

**Where the authors disagree.** Zahay and her co-authors (SRC-029) use the Value Proposition Canvas (Osterwalder and Pigneur): you start from the job the customer wants done (the *job-to-be-done*), from their pains and gains, then match the products that relieve them. They insist that the value proposition is *not* a positioning statement but a core element of the business strategy; Grewal and Levy treat them as the same thing. Tybout and Calkins call the category the "frame of reference" and add the reason to believe to the formula (BRD-006). Percy and Rosenbaum-Elliott, on the contrary, reduce it to three variables: the audience, the category need and the benefit (BRF-010).

## Application

- Write the statement before any creative concept. If you cannot fill the four brackets, you do not yet have a strategy.
- Check the fourth bracket against the circles: is the benefit in space 1, or in space 4, so "the same as everyone"?
- The statement is an internal document, not a slogan. The creative work dramatizes it, it does not recite it.

## Example

Southwest and Emirates (ch. 9) have opposite value propositions, both valid: good value with cattle-call seating vs. space and luxury. Each chose a different space 1. For Tesla, the canvas shows that the customer's job is not only commuting but also conveying an image of success (SRC-029).

## Questions

- What do you do when the real value proposition is in space 4 (parity)?
- Is Zahay's distinction between proposition and positioning useful, or does it only complicate the brief?
