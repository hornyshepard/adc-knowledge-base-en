# Secondary data are cheap but general, primary data are targeted but expensive; qualitative research opens, quantitative research confirms

> **The idea in one sentence:** a research project usually starts with existing data, continues with qualitative exploration that formulates hypotheses and ends with quantitative methods that test them; big data add volume, not certainty.

## Explanation

Grewal and Levy organize the sources as follows:

**Secondary data** (collected before the project): free or inexpensive (the census, trade associations), but often unsuitable or out of date; **syndicated data**, bought from firms such as Nielsen or IRI (scanner and panel data); internal data (invoices, customer lists).

**Primary data** (collected for the problem at hand), in two families (Exhibit 10.5):

| Qualitative: broad, open-ended questions | Quantitative: structured, testable answers |
|---|---|
| observation (including sensors on shelves) | survey research |
| social media monitoring | panel research |
| in-depth interviews | scanner research |
| focus groups (8 to 12 people, a moderator) | experimental research (manipulated variables) |

Qualitative research formulates objectives and hypotheses; quantitative research confirms them. The McDonald's price experiment ($4, $5, $6, $7 in four markets) shows that the most profitable price is $5, not the highest one.

**What to avoid in a questionnaire** (Exhibit 10.7): questions the respondent cannot answer accurately, unnecessary sensitive questions, double-barreled questions ("Do you like to shop for clothing and food?"), leading questions and questions that present only one side of the issue. **Big data** are described by the 5 Vs: volume, variety, velocity, veracity, value.

**Where the sources agree and where they differ.** Barre and Gayrard-Carrera (*La boîte à outils de la publicité*, INS-076) arrive at the same split: quantitative research measures "how much", qualitative research explains "why". Struhl (INS-077) is harsher on focus groups than the textbook: they are good for language and for detecting disasters, not for decisions, and Percy rules out focus groups for pretesting ads (ADV-079).

## Application

- For a campaign insight, start qualitative (observation, interviews), not with a survey: a survey only confirms what you already know to ask.
- Use the list of mistakes as a checklist when you write a questionnaire.
- Ask about the "veracity" of any data set: who collected it, when and for what purpose.

## Example

Campbell's Soup ran focus groups with women who would buy ready-to-eat soups. They wanted the ingredients they would use at home and rejected high fructose corn syrup, MSG and names they could not pronounce. The result guided the product line.

## Questions

- Why do people say one thing in a focus group and do another in the store? Which method corrects for this?
- Can big data replace qualitative research in the search for an insight?
