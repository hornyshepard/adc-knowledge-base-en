# Price is the customer's overall sacrifice and is set through the five Cs: company objectives, customers, costs, competition, channel members

> **The idea in one sentence:** price is not only the amount on the label but everything the buyer sacrifices to obtain the product, and a good pricing strategy starts from five sources, not only from costs.

## Explanation

Grewal and Levy define price as the **overall sacrifice** a consumer is willing to make: money, but also time, travel, taxes, shipping. It is the only P that brings in revenue, yet managers often treat it as a secondary problem: they copy the competition or add a markup to the cost.

The **five Cs of pricing** (Exhibit 14.1), with value at the center:

| C | What you analyze |
|---|---|
| Company objectives | profit, sales, market share, competitor or customer orientation (premium pricing) |
| Customers | the demand curve and **elasticity**: an elastic market (steak) or an inelastic one (milk) |
| Costs | variable costs, fixed costs, the break-even point |
| Competition | monopoly, oligopoly (risk of a price war), monopolistic competition (the most common), pure competition. Morton decommoditized salt through branding |
| Channel members | the manufacturer wants an image price, the retailer wants volume |

Two ideas useful for brands:

- **Prestige products break the law of demand up to a point.** Demand for a Club Med vacation rises from $1,000 to $5,000 and falls only at $8,000. Fender created the separate Squier brand for beginners, so as not to dilute its prestige.
- **Customers are more sensitive to price increases than to price decreases.**

In Chapter 15, the authors put the five Cs into practice through three methods: cost-based, competitor-based and **value-based** (the perceived benefits compared with the sacrifice).

**Where they agree.** Policarpio (SRC-002) adds survival to the objectives and observes that for services, where quality is hard to judge, customers use price as a **signal of quality**: too low suggests poor quality, too high creates expectations that are hard to meet. Consumer psychology confirms it: price, brand and country of origin are heuristics that change the experience of the product itself (INS-027).

## Application

- In a brief for a premium brand, price is part of the message: communication builds the perceived value that makes the price credible.
- Reduce the perceived "total cost": time saved, risk removed, no hidden fees.
- Ask which C dominates the client's pricing decision; that tells you which value argument they will accept.

## Example

Paradigm sells speakers from $219 a pair up to $17,500. Few people buy the expensive model, but it shows what the company is capable of and raises the image of the whole range.

## Questions

- When a luxury brand lowers its price, who loses: the new customer or the old one?
- Is "price as a signal of quality" a legitimate argument or a manipulation?
