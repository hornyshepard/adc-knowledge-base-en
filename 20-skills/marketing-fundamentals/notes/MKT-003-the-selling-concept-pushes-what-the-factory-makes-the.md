# The selling concept pushes what the factory makes, the marketing concept starts from what the market wants: an evolution described as eras (Grewal-Levy) or as philosophies (Kotler)

> **The idea in one sentence:** firms moved from "a good product sells itself" and "it has to be pushed" to "we start from the needs of the market and give more value than the competition"; the textbooks describe the same move either as a historical sequence or as philosophies that coexist.

## Explanation

The two schemes in the library, side by side:

| Grewal and Levy: eras (U.S., Exhibit 1.5) | Kotler-Armstrong, via Policarpio: philosophies | The assumption |
|---|---|---|
| Production-oriented era (turn of the 20th century) | production concept; product concept | customers want products that are available and inexpensive, or better; you win through efficiency or quality |
| Sales-oriented era (1920–1950) | selling concept | capacity exceeds demand, so people have to be persuaded |
| Market-oriented era (after World War II) | marketing concept | you first find out what the target wants and deliver better than the competitors |
| Value-based marketing era (toward the 2000s) | societal concept | you give more than the competition for what the customer gives (Grewal); you serve both the customer and society (Kotler) |

**Selling vs. marketing.** Selling is transactional: it persuades one customer, once, and sells what the factory makes. Marketing is relational: it creates demand, builds trust and prepares the ground for sales.

**Where the authors differ.** Grewal and Levy present the stages as a linear history that arrives at value-based marketing. Kotler treats them as options that coexist, and the final step is the societal one. The Kotler X.0 series retells the same story in other terms: 1.0 product, 2.0 customer, 3.0 values (MKT-032). Both schemes are American and linear, and marketing historians contest them. Policarpio is confused: the paragraph on the selling concept repeats arguments about product quality, and one grid contradicts the chapter; use it only for the table of the five concepts. He also describes **consumerism** (the consumer movement of the 1960s) as a reaction to the excesses of the selling concept.

## Application

- Diagnose the client: a brief that says "we have to sell the stock" or "the product is superior, people just need to find out" thinks in the logic of selling or of production.
- When the client asks for "more selling", ask whether the problem is one of demand (marketing) or of conversion (selling).
- The orientations coexist: different departments of the same firm can think in different eras.

## Example

The Ford Model T ("Customers can have any color they want so long as it's black") is the classic case of production orientation (SRC-001). Life insurance sold through aggressive tactics and appeals to fear is the typical case of the selling concept (SRC-002). Hermès, which launches lipsticks to the standards of its bags to protect its reputation, illustrates the logic of value.

## Questions

- Does the American periodization fit a market like Romania, which switched abruptly to a market economy after 1990?
- Is the selling concept always wrong? In B2B, isn't personal selling itself the marketing?
