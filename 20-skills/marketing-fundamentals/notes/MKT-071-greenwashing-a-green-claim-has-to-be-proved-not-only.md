# Greenwashing: a green claim has to be proved, not only communicated, and the enthusiastic textbooks sidestep the problem

> **The idea in one sentence:** greenwashing happens when the communication of an environmental benefit goes beyond what the company actually does. The library's books on sustainability offer tools against it, but hardly name it.

## Explanation

**What is missing.** Viturino (SRC-011) cites *The Drivers of Greenwashing* (Delmas and Burbano) and *The Sins of Greenwashing* (TerraChoice) in her bibliography, but does not discuss greenwashing in the text. Ejeke (SRC-010) defines green marketing by actual *or perceived* sustainability, and CSR as a way to look more humanitarian. Both put the emphasis on image, not on impact.

**What the sources do offer as an antidote** (SRC-011, ch. 2–3, 5):
- recognized certifications (FSC, Fair Trade, ISO 14001, LEED) and clear information labels;
- sustainability reports with successes *and* failures (the Patagonia model);
- independent external audits and measured indicators;
- Kotler & Lee's ethics: avoiding misleading practices in all communication.

Wuebben (SRC-008, ch. 9) cites Paul Mason: the move to a world of sharing might prompt a brand to say "Buy our stuff because… there is a measurable social good", beyond the usual CSR metrics.

**A question mark from the book's own examples.** Targets such as "Mission Zero by 2020" (Interface) are presented without saying whether they were met.

**Five tests for a green claim** (this note's synthesis, based on the tools above):
1. **Specific and quantified**: "-30% plastic compared with 2020", not "kinder to nature".
2. **Across the whole life cycle**, not a single conveniently chosen attribute.
3. **Verified by a third party** (certification, audit).
4. **Proportionate**: what share of the business does it cover?
5. **Consistent with behavior**: does the rest of the business contradict it?

**Who does name it.** The library's general textbook is more direct than the sustainability ones: Grewal and Levy define greenwashing as spending more money on saying you are green than on being green (MKT-063). Parsons, Maclaran and Chatzidakis show that brands with moral causes gain a halo, but become targets for exactly this accusation (BRD-041). The mechanism is the communication gap of classic services: the promise exceeds the delivery (MKT-028).

## Application

- Run every "green" line of copy through the five tests. Whatever fails gets rewritten or cut.
- Avoid the visual clichés (leaves, green, the globe) that suggest more than the text says.

## Example

The contrast within the library: Patagonia communicates its practices *after* it has them (BRD-024), while a brand that promises climate neutrality "by 2030" with no published plan communicates the intention instead of the deed.

## Questions

- Is greenwashing a communication problem (the agency) or an operations problem (the client)? Who is accountable?
- Where does a target's legitimate optimism end and misleading the public begin?
