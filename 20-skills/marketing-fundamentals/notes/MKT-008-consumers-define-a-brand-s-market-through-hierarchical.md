# Consumers define a brand's market, through hierarchical partitioning by type of product, end benefit, usage situation and brand

> **The idea in one sentence:** the same beer can have a 10%, 35% or 3% market share, depending on how you define the market; the right definition is the one by which consumers see the brands as substitutes.

## Explanation

Percy and Rosenbaum-Elliott give the example of Amstel Light: a 10% share of the European beer market, 35% of the lower-calorie beer market or 3% of the light-alcoholic beverage market. The real market is the group of brands that consumers see as **substitutes for the same usage situation**. A wrong definition spoils the share objectives and, with them, the communication strategy. **Cannibalization** is also predicted from here: a new product placed on the same "branch" as the firm's other products takes sales away from them.

Industry definitions are product-oriented. Brewers talk about "premium" and "imported" beers, while consumers simply call Beck's, Heineken or Stella Artois "regular beers". The Clorox case: Procter & Gamble was forced to divest the brand because its share was calculated on liquid bleach only; with dry bleaches included, the share would have been much smaller.

The consumer-centered methods increase in precision:

1. **Perceived similarity**: which products do you use for task X? It is the foundation of category need.
2. **Perceptual maps**: brands perceived as similar appear close together.
3. **Hierarchical partitioning**, the preferred one: the consumer divides the category from the abstract to the concrete. The lower you go, the more substitutable the brands seem, so people switch between them more easily.

There are four bases for partitions: **type of product**, **end benefit**, **usage situation** and **brand**. The order in which the consumer uses them defines the market.

**The link with the rest of the skill.** Grewal and Levy use the perceptual map *after* choosing the target, to position the brand (MKT-018); Percy uses it *before*, to find out whom you compete with. The same definition decides what "substitute" means in the five forces analysis (MKT-006).

## Application

- Ask the target why, when and how they use the category, not only which brands they know.
- Position the category need at the right level of the hierarchy: a luxury chocolate bar competes with other indulgences, not with potato chips.
- For launches, check which branch the new product falls on, to avoid cannibalization.

## Example

Snacks divide into salty vs. sweet, sweet snacks into pastries vs. confectionery, and chocolate bars into luxury (an exotic indulgence), plain (just chocolate) and filled. Each branch is a different category need.
