# A customer is worth the relationship, not the transaction: customer lifetime value (CLV)

> **The idea in one sentence:** the relational orientation judges each customer by the profit expected over the whole relationship, and CLV turns this idea into a number that justifies retention, service and loyalty programs.

## Explanation

Grewal and Levy describe the move from transaction to relationship (the *relational orientation*): the firm looks at the customer's lifetime profitability, not at what it earns from one sale. The tool is customer relationship management (CRM), a philosophy plus a set of systems that identify the most valuable customers and build their loyalty. A household that buys 50 cases of carbonated beverages a year for 20 years is not a $15 customer but a $15,000 one. The loyal customer drinks Pepsi even when Coca-Cola is on sale.

Appendix 10A formalizes the calculation. **Customer lifetime value (CLV)** is the expected financial contribution from a customer over the whole relationship:

CLV = Σ [profit in year t × retention rate^(t−1)] / (1 + i)^(t−1) − acquisition cost

Four questions feed the formula: how many years the relationship lasts, what annual profit the customer brings, what percentage of customers stay from one year to the next, and what tomorrow's money is worth today (the discount rate i).

Policarpio (SRC-002) calls the sum of all CLVs **customer equity** and proposes it as a better measure of performance than current sales or market share.

**Tension.** The textbooks measure loyalty by retention and repurchase. Kotler and his co-authors (Marketing 4.0) move it elsewhere: in the connected world, the loyal customer is the one who *recommends*, and some advocates have never bought (MKT-036).

## Application

- When the client wants only "acquisition", work out together how much a retained customer is worth. That justifies budgets for retention, CRM and post-purchase communication.
- Differentiate the messages: customers with a high CLV get service and recognition, not just discounts.
- Loyalty programs (Sally Beauty: a $5 membership in exchange for an e-mail address) are in fact machines for collecting data for CRM.

## Example

Very Clean Cleaners broke a button on Greg's shirt. With $1,000 profit a year, 90% retention, a 10% discount rate, five years and a $100 acquisition cost, Greg is worth $3,383.40. He is not a $2.50 customer, the cost of laundering a shirt.

## Questions

- CLV assumes constant profit and stable retention. What happens with fashion brands or in categories bought rarely?
- Can an obsession with CLV lead to neglecting the "small" customers who make recommendations?
