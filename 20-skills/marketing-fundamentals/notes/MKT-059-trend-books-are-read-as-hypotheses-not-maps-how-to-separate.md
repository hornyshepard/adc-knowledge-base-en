# Trend books are read as hypotheses, not maps: how to separate signal from filler

> **The idea in one sentence:** the literature on "the future of marketing" mixes solid trends with tech enthusiasm, unsourced statistics and self-promotion. A strategist uses it as a list of hypotheses to check, not as an action plan.

## Explanation

Four books in the library show four kinds of problem:

| The problem | Example from the sources |
|---|---|
| **Hasty technology forecasts** | VR as the standard by 2021, hologram-enabled handheld devices, paid media with a nearly perfect conversion rate by 2030 (SRC-008, ch. 10, 12) |
| **Myth statistics** | more than 50% of search queries made through voice by 2024 (SRC-009, ch. 4); the 8-second attention span (SRC-010, trend 47) |
| **Seasonal hype** | "Quantum Computing Is Making a Big Impact" in marketing, NFTs in social commerce, the metaverse (SRC-010, trends 39, 51–52) |
| **Undocumented authority** | the author billed as the originator of the 4th Industrial Revolution, a tool created while working with Steve Jobs, failure percentages with no method, inconsistent trend counts (SRC-007) |
| **Generated filler** | the same six themes repeated in every subchapter, zero examples, zero sources (SRC-009) |

The counterpoint: the same books correctly anticipated brands as platforms, memes, niches and AI as an amplifier of work (SRC-008, ch. 10). The problem is not that forecasts are wrong, but that they are stated with no degree of certainty.

**Reading grid** (this note's synthesis):
1. **The source and date** of every number: a primary study or a blog citing a blog?
2. **The horizon**: is it a checkable claim ("by 2021…") or one that cannot be disproved?
3. **The author's interest**: do they sell consulting or other books? (Wuebben, von Scheel and Ejeke promote their services in the text.)
4. **Level and stage**: is it a megatrend, a macrotrend or a microtrend? Is it still a driver or already a force?
5. **Behavior or device?** Forecasts about what *people want* (connection, convenience, meaning) hold up better than forecasts about what *hardware* they will use.
6. **Specificity**: missing examples and repetition are signs of filler.

**Not only the weak books.** The same grid applies to the Kotler series, which quietly rewrites its stages from one book to the next (MKT-048), and to agency methodologies that repackage old ideas under new names (HIS-015).

## Application

- In a trend deck for a client, put next to each trend the primary source, a confidence level (high / medium / speculative) and a signal you would watch to confirm it.
- In the seminar: pick a forecast from SRC-008 and check it against 2026 data.

## Example

The statistic that attention spans have dropped to 8 seconds, less than a goldfish's (SRC-010, trend 47), turns up often in agency presentations. Run through the grid, it fails at the first step: it has no checkable primary source, and the claim cannot be disproved. Wuebben's forecast about brands as platforms passes the test: it is about behavior and it came true.

## Questions

- Is a trend book that gets things wrong still useful if it makes you think about the future?
- What would a trend report that is honest about its own uncertainties look like?
