# Value is what you get for what you give, not a low price

> **The idea in one sentence:** perceived value is the ratio between the benefits received and everything the customer gives (money, time, effort, data), so an expensive product can be a "good value" and a cheap one may not be.

## Explanation

Grewal and Levy define value as the relationship of benefits to costs, in short "what you get for what you give." The cost is not only the price: it includes the time, the energy and the information given up. A good value does not automatically mean inexpensive; if it did, luxury makers would go out of business. Hermès sells a $67 lipstick, and its customers see it as a good value: they buy exclusivity, reputation and the certainty of quality, not just a pigment.

The authors add **value cocreation**: the customer becomes a collaborator (Betabrand lets its customers vote on the style and fabric of its clothes). They also describe four activities through which firms become value driven: they add value through the whole mix, they use data analytics, they balance the benefits and costs for society, and they connect with customers through social and mobile media.

**Where the authors agree.** Tybout and Calkins (Kellogg, SRC-014) write the same idea as an equation: physical and emotional benefits divided by monetary and time costs; a brand competes either in the numerator or in the denominator (BRD-013). In Marketing 3.0, Kotler, Kartajaya and Setiawan add a third layer of benefit, the "spiritual" one (the contribution to a better world), on top of the functional and the emotional (MKT-033). **Where they differ:** cocreation, which Grewal and Levy present as a gain for both sides, is read by the critical authors as unpaid work by the customer (MKT-047).

## Application

- In a brief, do not write "affordable product". Write what the customer gets and what they give up: money, time, risk, the effort of learning. The ad either raises the perceived benefit or lowers the perceived cost.
- For premium products, the communication does not justify the price with technical features but with meaning (exclusivity, status, trust).
- Look for places where the customer can cocreate, but check what they get in return.

## Example

The case study in Chapter 1 compares the water market: inexpensive bottled water for those who want a basic commodity, $60 reusable bottles for those who want sustainability, a luxury flask of around $5,000 for those who want prestige. The same need, three different value equations.

## Questions

- Can a $45 Timex and a $10,000 Rolex both be a "good value"? For whom?
- Is the personal data given up in a loyalty program a cost that the customer perceives?
