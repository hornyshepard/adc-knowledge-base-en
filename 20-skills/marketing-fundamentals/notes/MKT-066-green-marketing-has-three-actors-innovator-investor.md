# Green marketing has three actors (Innovator, Investor, Propagator) and four segments that call for different positionings

> **The idea in one sentence:** green products reach the mainstream only when three types of companies collaborate and when the message shifts from "eco-advantage" to "eco-efficiency" and then to "eco-standard", as the product moves from one segment to the next.

## Explanation

**Three roles** (from the DuPont, Wal-Mart and Timberland cases):

| Role | What it does | Example | Typical motivations (from *Green to Gold*) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Innovator** | creates products that reverse the damage done to the environment; disruptive, high-risk innovation | DuPont: from the worst U.S. polluter, $5 billion of its $29 billion in revenue comes from sustainable products | natural resources dependence, current and potential regulation |
| **Investor** | funds green projects and brings them to the mass market | Wal-Mart: $500 million in 2005 so its stores use less energy | high brand exposure, big environmental impact |
| **Propagator** | builds awareness and "environmental ambassadors"; plays in niches | Timberland: a "nutrition label" on shoes, showing their environmental impact | competitive market for talent, low market power, good environmental track record |

Propagators start the buzz, Investors make green a mass standard, and Innovators supply the products (Fig. 9.2).

**Four segments** of the green market (Table 9.1): **trendsetters** (enthusiasts, emotional and spiritual motivation; *eco-advantage* positioning), **value-seekers** (pragmatic, rational, want savings; *eco-efficiency*: more value with less impact), **standard matchers** (conservative, wait for the product to become standard; *eco-standard*) and **cautious buyers** (skeptics; not worth pursuing). The segments form a chain of influence (Fig. 9.3), with Geoffrey Moore's "chasm" between the early market and the mainstream.

The life cycle (Fig. 9.4): at launch, green is the main differentiator, backed by trendsetters and word of mouth; in growth, you communicate efficiency, win the value-seekers and lower the price through economies of scale; at maturity, when green competitors appear, you differentiate on something else.

## Application

- Do not sell the same green story to everyone: talk to the trendsetter about being first, to the pragmatist about money saved.
- Use the *nudge* (Thaler and Sunstein): put the green option next to the usual one and let it be chosen.
- Do not spend budget on skeptics.

## Example

Tide Coldwater (a detergent formulated to wash clothes best in cold water) shows how major corporations green their mainstream brands, with the argument of energy savings, not the moral one (p. 182).

## Questions

- Wal-Mart is criticized for going green for cost reasons. Does the motivation matter if the impact is large?
- Does the 2010 segmentation still fit a market where sustainability is regulated (ESG reporting, for example)?
