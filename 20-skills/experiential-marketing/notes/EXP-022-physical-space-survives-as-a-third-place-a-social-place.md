# Physical space survives as a "third place": a social place with a coherent story and a sense of homeyness, not a point of transaction

> **The idea in one sentence:** even for the digital generations, the store and the coffee shop remain the center of the experience, but only if they offer relationships and meaning; a purely transactional space loses to e-commerce.

## Explanation

*Marketing 6.0* starts from the numbers: e-commerce is less than 15 percent of US retail sales, and Apple has the highest sales per square foot of any retailer ($5,500). Younger generations call physical marketing **IRL** ("in real life"). The core concept is the **third place**, coined by sociologist Ray Oldenburg: the social place away from home and work, inclusive and welcoming. Generation Z has given the digital space the role of a third place, yet the Cigna US Loneliness Index calls it the loneliest generation (73 percent report loneliness). The authors' conclusion: the primary purpose of a physical location is to nurture social relationships.

Parsons, Maclaran and Chatzidakis (citing Rosenbaum) explain *how* people bond with a commercial space. Attachment emerges from a sense of **homeyness**: a blend of familiarity, authenticity and security (Debenedetti et al.). It arises from the spatial features, but also from the way proprietors and employees make customers feel unique and appreciated: genuine conversations, back-stage access, unexpected gifts. Customers give back through tipping, volunteering and acting as informal brand ambassadors. Fast food restaurants, designed to move customers through quickly, are unlikely to become third places.

Kotler 6.0 also gives the design grid (Fig. 6.3, "Components of the In-Real-Life Experience"): the space tells a story through three components, the same ones added to the marketing mix for services:

| Component | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Physical evidence** | space design, music, scent, uniforms, the IKEA path; limited seating and standing desks discourage lingering, comfortable seating invites people to stay |
| **Process** | customer-facing and behind the scenes |
| **People** | the hardest to replicate: the "Singapore Girl" defines the airline more than its aircraft |

The two sources converge: people matter more than the decor. Kotler then adds the digital layers ([[EXP-025-the-phygital-experience-links-the-store-and-digital-without|EXP-025]]), and Parsons et al. raise the critical question: how much of the "home" is commercial strategy?

## Application

- When you design a pop-up or a flagship, write the story first, then choose the physical evidence, the processes and the profile of the people who carry it.
- Ask: what social reason does someone have to come here instead of ordering online?
- For everyday locations, invest in small rituals and in staff, not only in the fit-out.

## Example

Starbucks protects its signature coffee scent: no indoor smoking, no strong perfumes for baristas, and a food menu that avoids strongly scented items. The scent is part of the brand's physical evidence.

## Questions

- Which coffee shop or bookstore in your city works as a third place? What makes it one?
- When does the "third place" become a mere excuse to keep customers in the store longer?
