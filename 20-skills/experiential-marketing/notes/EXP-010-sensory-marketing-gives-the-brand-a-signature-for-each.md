# Sensory marketing gives the brand a signature for each sense: coherent with the brand's story and measured, not spectacular on all five

> **The idea in one sentence:** a brand translates its identity into olfactory, auditory, tactile, visual and gustatory signatures, and the effect comes from their coherence, not from their intensity.

## Explanation

Batat presents sensory marketing as one of the four kinds of action through which the firm triggers experiences: a customer who feels good in a place stays longer, consumes more and speaks well of it. *Marketing 6.0* adds the reason the topic is urgent today: average daily Internet usage exceeds 6.5 hours, and one in three people shows signs of **digital fatigue** (Deloitte). Digital media engage only sight and hearing, so the other senses become free space.

| Sense | Role | Batat | Kotler 6.0 |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Visual** | the first impression; shape suggests function | Lush stacks its products by color, as on a market stall | 80 percent recognize Mastercard without its name |
| **Auditory** | the rhythm of the music changes time spent and spending; *sonic branding* | Intel's four notes: almost immediate attribution, but weak personality | Mastercard's "Priceless" album, soundscapes at Selfridges |
| **Olfactory** | memory and nostalgia | Cineplex's popcorn fragrance | "White Tea" at Westin |
| **Tactile** | touch suggests quality and increases the wish to buy (the endowment effect) | the Diesel "Fuel for Life" bottle in brown burlap | trying out products at the Apple Store |
| **Gustatory** | the hardest to use; it links taste to values | Badoit Express, the dinner in the subway | 30 percent of IKEA store visitors come for the food |

Kotler proposes three steps: objectives (brand associations, emotions, sales), touchpoints (point of sale, pop-up, digital), then **coherent delivery** under an umbrella story, as in Disney's theme parks. Both books repeat the figure of about 80 percent for sight (Kotler adds 10 percent for hearing), without a solid source.

## Application

- Run a sensory audit: what does the customer perceive through each sense in the store, at the event, when opening the packaging? What is accidental and what is designed?
- Choose one strong signature and 2–3 secondary ones; test their congruence. A smell, a sound and an image that tell different stories cancel each other out.
- In a rebranding, work on the full sensory system (sound logo, packaging texture), not just the logo and colors.

## Example

Abercrombie & Fitch shows where the authors part ways. Batat (2019) gives it as a model: dim lighting, clothes to touch, nightclub music, the brand perfume sprayed on clothing every hour. Kotler (2024) tells how the overpowering "Fierce" fragrance drove customers away, so the brand switched to the more subtle "Ellwood". His conclusion: "Often, the most successful cues are subtle and implicit."

## Questions

- How far can you rely on the 80/10/1–4 percent figures in a client presentation?
- Where does sensory stimulation become manipulation or aggression (loud music, strong perfume)?
