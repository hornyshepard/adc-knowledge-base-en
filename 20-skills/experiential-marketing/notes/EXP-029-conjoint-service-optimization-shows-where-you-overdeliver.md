# Conjoint service optimization shows where you overdeliver and where you underdeliver

> **The idea in one sentence:** when the basic products are almost identical, the services around them decide the relationship; rated as a whole package, they reveal their real importance and the best level of delivery.

## Explanation

In complex service relationships (utilities with commercial customers, telecoms, insurance, pharmaceutical companies with doctors), the company delivers far more than the product: installation, maintenance, samples, training, ongoing education, newsletters. Struhl calls these **touch points**. Asked directly, customers rate everything as important (see ratings). Service conjoint shows **entire service profiles** and asks for a rating of how satisfied the person would be if the package were delivered consistently at that level. The result is a ratio-level ranking (one service is *n* times as important as another) and satisfaction curves for each level of frequency.

This avoids two symmetrical mistakes: **underdelivering** where it matters and **overdelivering**, which costs money without bringing satisfaction.

## Application

- In customer experience projects, list the touch points and their possible levels before asking for budgets for "more".
- Communicate only the services that matter. A monthly newsletter with nothing to say is not a benefit.
- For the agency's B2B client: the same logic can be applied to the agency's own service package (reports, meetings, response time).

## Example

**NewInnova**, a "me too" pharmaceutical company that offered nothing distinctive, decided to compete through its relationships with doctors. It tested 11 service areas (42 profiles, 14 per respondent, HB analysis). Sampling came out as the most important: over 16 times as important as the newsletter (index 100 vs. 6) and nearly three times as important as continuing medical education (index 37). Satisfaction was nearly identical whether samples came every two or every three weeks. So the company was overdelivering to about 22% of its customers, while about 40% received samples too infrequently (14% every six weeks or less, a highly dissatisfying level). The senior marketing person's eyes lit up on learning that the newsletter did not have to be monthly.

## Questions

- Why does "more service" not automatically mean "more satisfaction"?
- Which touch points of a retail brand would be worth testing this way?
