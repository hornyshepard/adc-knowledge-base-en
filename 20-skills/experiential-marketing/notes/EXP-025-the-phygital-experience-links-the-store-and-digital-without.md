# The phygital experience links the store and digital without breaks: five ways to bring digital into the physical space

> **The idea in one sentence:** *phygital* (physical + digital) does not mean tablets in the store but gateways through which customers pass from one channel to another without difficulty; Batat and Kotler 6.0 both ask that the technology solve a concrete problem of the visit.

## Explanation

Batat attributes the term to the Australian agency Momentum (2013) and describes the phygital experience as a third context of consumption, neither exclusively physical nor fully digital. The transformation starts well before the customer opens the door of the store and continues after they leave. *Marketing 6.0* calls the same thing "extended reality" (the physical space augmented with digital experiences) and proposes five ways to build it. Batat's tools match them almost one to one:

| Kotler 6.0 (Fig. 6.2) | Kotler's examples | Batat's equivalent |
|---|---|---|
| **Seamless transactions** | Amazon Go, the Nike Speed Shop lockers | mobile payment, click and collect, e-booking |
| **Contextual recommendations** | virtual fitting rooms (Ralph Lauren, COS) | personalized messages through micro-location (iBeacon) |
| **Interactive engagements** | interactive window displays (Bloomingdale's), Timberland's window controlled by hand gestures, Burberry and WeChat gamification | Timberland's NFC label, the digital wall |
| **Augmented discoveries** | apps with in-store modes, QR codes, AR wayfinding (Home Depot, Lowe's) | range extension: the store orders items that are not on the shelf |
| **Pre- and post-experience** | the app as a continuous thread (Nike Live) | re-engaging the customer after they leave the store |

The differences are mostly of emphasis. Batat insists that a successful phygital experience gives both a cognitive answer (convenience, time savings) and an emotional one (pleasure, exchange). Kotler treats fluidity as a hygiene factor and asks you to start from the frustrations of the visit (queues, products that are hard to find). Batat's examples (iBeacon, 2013–2016) have aged; the principle has not.

## Application

- Map the journey of a customer who starts on Instagram and ends in the store: where does the thread break?
- For each technology you propose, ask: does it help the shopper at that very moment, or does it only look "innovative"?
- Plan re-engagement after the customer leaves, and ask for consent for any location tracking or recognition.

## Example

Timberland appears in both books. In Batat (New York, 2016), customers tap the product label with an NFC mini tablet, build a list that is sent to them by email, and a digital wall extends the store. In Kotler, the window display is controlled by hand gestures, with a touchless try-on, without entering. In both, customers who just want to shop can simply shop.

## Questions

- Which principle stays valid when the emblematic technology (iBeacon, Google Glass) disappears?
- When does in-store personalization become an intrusion on privacy?
