# A new product is told through something familiar

> **The idea in one sentence:** people accept change only if they can associate continuity with it, so the story of a new product connects it with something old and known.

## Explanation

Mueller and Rajaram open the book with a contrast: Casady & Greene created the first industrial MP3 player and the SoundJam MP software, but failed to develop a convincing story for it. Apple bought SoundJam MP in 2000 and gave the player an intuitive design, and Steve Jobs marketed it as the iPod with the story claim "The Walkman of the Twenty-first Century". What made the difference was the connection between the new and the old.

The authors show that the language of technology is full of such bridges: "horsepower" for cars that have long stopped resembling carriages, "folder" for digital data structures, "filter" and "lens" for computer graphic 3D effects, and Clippy, Microsoft's assistant shaped like an office paper clip, for one of the first conversational agents. The reverse also works: "Magic Vision Control" names an AI-based windshield wiper; the term is new, the visual impression stays familiar.

The psychological mechanism, according to the authors: if you don't tell the connection, the fear of something new may exceed the benefit of the new product in people's perception. Familiar words translate new and disruptive things into familiar experiences and lay the foundation for a relationship, then for buying and using the product. The same logic explains why early social media posts translated traditional formats (articles, advertisements, galleries) before the native genres appeared.

## Application

- For a launch, look for "the X of Y" (the Walkman of the twenty-first century, "Uber for…") and test whether the bridge is accurate and flattering.
- In naming and in the claim, combine a new element with a known one.
- Check that the analogy does not narrow the product (a bridge that is too literal can make it look ordinary).

## Example

Broadcasters sold radio as "Cinema in the mind"; Streem sums up its AR app in "Better than being there". Both connect the new with an experience the audience already knows.

## Questions

- When is it stronger to stress the break ("it is like nothing else") than the continuity?
- How does the principle relate to the theory of the diffusion of innovations (compatibility with previous experiences)?
