# The hero's journey: the protagonist leaves the ordinary world, goes through trials and returns transformed; in advertising the hero is usually the customer, not the brand

> **The idea in one sentence:** the hero's journey (Joseph Campbell, 1949) is the narrative pattern advertising reuses most often, because an ad's story is almost always about a transformation; the authors in the library agree that the hero is the customer, but not that the pattern still works on social media.

## Explanation

Campbell found the same sequence of situations and the same characters in the myths of many cultures; Christopher Vogler turned it into the screenwriting matrix of three acts and 12 steps. The books in the library compress it differently:

| Source | The version |
|---|---|
| Batat (SRC-036) | five steps: the hero's dream of flight → the decision to leave, with its doubts and fears → the discovery of the extraordinary world → mixed feelings at the hardships of the trip → the triumphal return, with a transformed identity |
| Mueller and Rajaram (SRC-034) | three phases: setting out from a comfort zone → going on a journey and overcoming challenges → arriving in a new comfort zone |
| Braun and Landa (SRC-025) | the protagonist leaves the ordinary world and reemerges changed (and Dan Harmon's "Story Circle"); the key moment is the apotheosis |
| Richards and McLean (SRC-040) | the three acts and the classic stages: the ordinary world, the call, the refusal, the mentor, the threshold… |

**Why it works.** Batat: the story produces *narrative transportation*: it reduces rational judgment of the message, and the persuasion lasts longer. Braun and Landa: unlike other forms of storytelling, an ad has to offer a transformation through the brand.

**Who the hero is.** Richards and McLean say it outright: the customer is the hero, not the brand. Sawhney (SRC-014) arrives at the same rule through the metaphor of the brand as oxygen ([[STO-006-in-brand-storytelling-the-brand-is-oxygen-everywhere-but|STO-006]]), and Braun and Landa give the brand the role of the "wizard" who facilitates the quest. Batat leaves the role open: the hero can also be the founder. **Where they disagree.** Mueller and Rajaram reject the pattern for social media ([[STO-014-on-social-media-the-guide-replaces-the-hero-of-the-classic|STO-014]]) because it is too long and too closed. Richards and McLean add a caveat: structure is not enough if the audience does not care about the character.

## Application

- Write on one line: who the hero is, what world they leave, what stops them, how the brand helps, what they become at the end.
- Is the trial or the transformation missing? Then you have a list of benefits, not a journey.
- Batat also offers an alternative: start from the positioning (identity → relation → story → incarnation), not from the arc.

## Example

"Inside Chanel" is narrated by the personified camellia ("I remember…"), which links the flower to the life of Coco Chanel: the founder becomes a mythical heroine (Batat). In "The Song" (Apple), a granddaughter brings her grandmother's melody back to life with a MacBook: the hero is the customer, the product is the tool.

## Questions

- Everyone uses the hero's arc. How do you avoid the cliché?
- What changes when the hero is the founder, the brand or the customer?
