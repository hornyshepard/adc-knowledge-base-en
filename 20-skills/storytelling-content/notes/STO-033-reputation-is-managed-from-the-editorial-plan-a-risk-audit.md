# Reputation is managed from the editorial plan: a risk audit by content pillar, alignment with the business, monitoring, double approval

> **The idea in one sentence:** fear of communicating openly does not protect a reputation; an editorial process that anticipates the risks before publication does.

## Explanation

Richards and McLean start from a finding: most businesses do not have a crisis communications plan, yet many are afraid to engage online precisely because of their reputation. The authors give five reasons why reputation and content are linked:

1. If you do not manage your reputation, the public and your customers will.
2. Social media amplifies every opinion, positive or negative.
3. Without an **opinion**, content becomes uninteresting. The fear of having a position undermines your strategy.
4. You can score an **own goal** with your own content: an article about how to save money published just before the business announces a price rise.
5. Vocal detractors exist anyway.

The solution they propose is a process of **four steps**, built into content planning:

| Step | What it means |
|---|---|
| 1. Reputation risk audit around content themes | for each content pillar, which reputation issues could arise and how you avoid them |
| 2. Align the editorial calendar with business activity | nothing is published until you know what the other departments are preparing (prices, restructuring, launches) |
| 3. Real-time event monitoring | how the piece could be read in the context of the day's news; alerts before publication |
| 4. Approvals process | modeled on newspapers: the copy passes through at least two people (the news editor, then the sub-editor), but quickly, not in two weeks |

On top of these comes a **detractor engagement plan**: how and when you respond, in what tone of voice, with what content, the same online, in-store and in the contact center. For sustained criticism, the authors see steady positive content as the most effective way to dilute negative sentiment and develop advocates.

## Application

- Add a "risk" column and a "checked with" column to the editorial calendar (the department that confirms there is no conflict).
- Make a list of sensitive events for the brand (tragedies, elections, holidays) with clear rules: "we don't post" or "we post only this way".
- Require two sign-offs on every public piece, with a maximum response time.

## Example

The authors mention the yearly round-ups of social media campaigns that backfired and the brands that hooked themselves inappropriately onto real-world events. That is why many social media teams now avoid any link to the day's news unless it is direct and obvious.

## Questions

- How do you keep the speed of social media with a double approval process?
- When is it better for the brand to stay silent than to have an opinion?
