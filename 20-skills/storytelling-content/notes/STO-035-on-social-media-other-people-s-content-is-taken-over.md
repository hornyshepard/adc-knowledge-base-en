# On social media, other people's content is taken over through the share function or with written permission, and the people filmed keep the right to their own image

> **The idea in one sentence:** on social media, "it's on the internet" does not mean "it's free": every photo, video, text or song is protected from the moment it is created, and every recognizable person decides alone whether they appear in your content.

## Explanation

In *Social Media Storytelling*, the journalist Deana Mrkaja gives the basic rules for anyone producing social content. On the production side, they complete the general legal rules on copyright, trademarks and licenses in [[DIG-068-in-digital-content-other-people-s-trademarks-texts-and|DIG-068]]:

- **Your own content** raises no problems, as long as it contains no hate speech, threats, violence or nudity. But on upload you cede a license to the platform: it reserves the right to use your material.
- **Other people's content** is taken over most safely through the platform's **sharing function**: the material stays unchanged and it is obvious that it is not yours. Downloading it, taking a screenshot and reposting it as your own material are not allowed.
- **Licenses** (the six Creative Commons licenses, the terms of image databases) are checked before publication. No license and no identifiable rights holder: you do not publish. A known creator but no license: you ask for permission, ideally in writing. Crediting the author alone does not replace permission.
- **Music** in the platforms' built-in editors is licensed by the platform; outside them, you use royalty-free music or pay the royalty fees.
- **The right to one's own image**: ask the people you film for their permission, especially young people; for children the parents decide, but the protection of the minor weighs most.
- **"Private use"** is a blurred line: with more than 100 friends on Facebook, a share can hardly still be called private.

## Application

- For every external piece, record in the production plan: the source, the license, the permission (with its date) and the form of credit.
- Ask for the creator's written permission before you turn a piece of UGC into a paid ad.
- The rules in the book are German; check local law and each platform's terms before the campaign.

## Example

The borderline case the author describes is a photo taken at an event or of a building in which other people are clearly visible. German law (the KUG) allows publication without consent only in exceptions: persons of contemporary history, people who appear only as "accessories" next to a landscape, participants in public gatherings, and pictures not made to order.
