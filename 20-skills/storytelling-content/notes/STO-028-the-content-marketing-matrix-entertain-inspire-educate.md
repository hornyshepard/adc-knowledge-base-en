# The content marketing matrix: entertain, inspire, educate, convince, by emotion and stage of the decision

> **The idea in one sentence:** the right type of content depends on two questions (is the decision emotional or rational? is the person at the start, at awareness, or close to purchase?), and the answers give four roles: entertain, inspire, educate, convince.

## Explanation

Dave Chaffey's "Content Marketing Matrix" (Smart Insights), taken up by the authors, has the stage (awareness → purchase) on the horizontal axis and the nature of the decision (emotional ↔ rational) on the vertical axis:

| | Awareness (start) | Purchase (end) |
|---|---|---|
| **Emotional** | **Entertain**: quizzes, viral videos, competitions, games | **Inspire**: celebrity endorsements, reviews, community forums |
| **Rational** | **Educate**: infographics, guides, trend reports, articles | **Convince**: webinars, demos, calculators, checklists, product and price sheets |

The book's examples: emotional products (dating websites, fitness plans) call for interactive content at the start; rational products (investments) call for infographics and reports; for impulse or image purchases come the celebrity collaborations ("The Travis Scott" meal at McDonald's, the Shaq-a-Roni pizza at Papa John's); for rational services (insurance, coaching), webinars and calculators. "Mixed" content (blogs, case studies) sits in the middle.

An important warning: as an objective, "viral" is a lottery; the most viral moments of the year (the Depp vs. Heard trial, the death of Queen Elizabeth II, the slap at the Oscars) were not branded. The authors advise objectives in terms of **shareable** content, as BuzzFeed does with listicles, or the Huffington Post. Connors (SRC-030) offers a parallel grid, in three stages: awareness (educational content), consideration (case studies, comparisons, webinars), decision (testimonials, demos, free trials).

## Application

- Place each piece of the content plan on the matrix; a good plan covers all four quadrants.
- For a brief that says "we want something viral", rephrase it as "we want something our audience has a reason to share".
- Use the matrix as an idea generator: for the same product, what would a quiz, a guide, a review, a calculator be?

## Example

BuzzFeed has become very good at shareable content: one analysis cited by the authors shows that 65 percent of its viral articles are listicles ("Top 7 Reasons…"). They look like clickbait, but they are deliberately built to be easy to read and to pass on: an objective of shareability, not a bet on virality.

## Questions

- Where in the matrix would a purpose campaign (a social cause) sit?
- Does the emotional/rational distinction hold, if every decision has emotional components?
