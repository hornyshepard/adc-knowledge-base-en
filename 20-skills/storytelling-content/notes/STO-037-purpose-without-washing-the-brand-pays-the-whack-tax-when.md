# Purpose without "washing": the brand pays the "whack tax" when it says more than it does

> **The idea in one sentence:** purpose-driven communication works only when the purpose comes from the brand's DNA, is linked to its industry, makes the brand part of the solution and is funded; otherwise the audience taxes the hypocrisy.

## Explanation

Braun and Landa argue that young consumers now demand, beyond simply expecting, that brands take a stand. The book cites several figures: 57 percent of U.S. consumers say they have increased loyalty to brands committed to addressing social causes; after "Dream Crazy", Nike's stock increased in value by $6 billion; Dove's sales rose 8 percent in 2021, against an industry-wide average of 4.5 percent. The authors also admit the risk: H&M lost $74 million in sales for the quarter after the boycott in China.

**The "whack tax".** In ad agency slang, it is the price paid by a brand that inauthentically trumpets its CSR efforts: negative sentiment, boycotts, brand switching, negative word of mouth. The remedy: *walk the walk*, do before you say.

Three conditions for a purpose that drives growth (Alessandro Manfredi, chief marketing officer of Dove, quoted in the book):

1. the purpose is linked to the brand's industry;
2. it is authentic, which means the brand is part of a solution;
3. the brand invests in it.

Other voices in the book complete the list. Harsh Kapadia: ask what the brand is obsessed about, because purpose extends naturally from that obsession; a charitable campaign unrelated to how the company does business is a temporary feel-good but has no impact. Craig Bagno (McCann): "Often the magic is in the mechanism", and the best ideas offer real solutions. Pancho González: the most common problem is the lack of a legitimate link between the core business and the cause. On top of this comes *rainbow washing*: support for the LGBTQIA+ community signaled only during Pride Month.

On credibility, a study cited in the book names six dimensions of CSR communication: informativeness, third-party endorsement, personal relevance, message tone, consistency and transparency. The more self-congratulatory the tone, the more skeptical consumers become.

## Application

- Before a "purpose" idea, fill in: the link to the industry, what the brand actually does, how much money, for how long.
- Write the copy without self-praise; let the facts and third parties speak.
- Prepare answers to the awkward questions (production practices, the supply chain) before the launch.

## Example

IKEA #BuyBackFriday: on Black Friday, a date associated with irresponsible consumption, IKEA announced it would buy back used furniture, refurbish it and resell it. According to the book, the program spans twenty countries, and the total number of resold items exceeded 61,000.

## Questions

- Can a brand without a cause "in its DNA" credibly build one? How long does it take?
- How does the demand for *purpose* square with a figure from the same book: 59 percent prefer brands to remain neutral?
