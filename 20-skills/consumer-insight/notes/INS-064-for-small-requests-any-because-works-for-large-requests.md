# For small requests any "because" works; for large requests only real reasons do (mindlessness in compliance)

> **The idea in one sentence:** when the stakes are low, people follow the social script automatically and do not check the reason given; when the stakes rise, they start to judge the content.

## Explanation

Fennis and Stroebe explain the effectiveness of influence techniques through **mindlessness** (Langer): people replay learned scripts and apply heuristics without examining the request. The authors reject, however, Cialdini's metaphor of the "click–whirr" response (a fixed, innate response, as in animals): the process is automatic in how it is triggered and shallow in how it is checked, not fully automatic.

The classic experiment of Langer, Blank and Chanowitz (1978), at the copy machine of a university library:

| Request | 5 pages | 20 copies |
|---|---|---|
| No reason given | 60% | lower |
| Real reason (being in a rush) | 94% | increases |
| Placebic reason (having to make copies) | 93% | the same as with no reason |

Pollock et al. (1998) replicated it with the sale of chocolates: a trivial reason ("made of chocolate and sold in this box") sold as well as a real one (68%), but only for the small $1 box, not for the $5 one.

Other ideas from the chapter:

- **The sufficiency principle** (Chen & Chaiken): people make the minimum effort compatible with their motivation at that moment; heuristics can be adaptive: the recognition heuristic predicted 70% of the Wimbledon matches.
- **It is enough to ask.** Flynn and Lake (2008): those who ask systematically underestimate how willing people are to say "yes", because refusing has a social cost.
- **Self-control resources.** Fennis et al. (2009) propose that the first requests of sequential techniques (foot-in-the-door, successive questions) deplete self-control and increase reliance on heuristics. The authors note that the idea of ego depletion is hotly contested in replication.

## Application

- In copy for small actions (subscribing, clicking, a free trial), a simple "because" increases compliance; do not complicate it.
- For large decisions, the reason has to be real and verifiable; the audience will examine it.
- Do not overestimate resistance: sometimes a clear, direct request is all it takes.

## Example

"…may I use the Xerox machine because I have to make copies?": 93% compliance for five pages.

## Questions

- Which "placebic reasons" do you recognize in the ads you see every day?
- Where does effectiveness end and manipulation begin in the use of mindlessness?
