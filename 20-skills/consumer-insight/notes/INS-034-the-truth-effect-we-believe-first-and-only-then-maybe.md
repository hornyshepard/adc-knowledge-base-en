# The truth effect: we believe first, and only then (maybe) correct

> **The idea in one sentence:** to comprehend a claim is, by default, to accept it; rejection is an extra, effortful step that a distracted person no longer takes.

## Explanation

Fennis and Stroebe start from a striking finding: Jacoby and Hoyer (1982) found that about 80 per cent of television messages are miscomprehended in some way. Yet incomplete comprehension does not prevent persuasion, because of the truth effect.

Gilbert's studies (Gilbert et al., 1990, 1993): participants learned statements in what they thought was an unknown language and received feedback that the statements were true or false. When distracted, they came to classify false statements as true, but not the reverse. In another study, false information about crimes influenced the prison terms recommended by distracted participants. The conclusion: believing is the default, low-effort response; rejection is the correction that takes resources.

Consequences for advertising:

- In a cluttered media environment, consumers resemble the distracted participant: they accept more easily than they reject.
- **Repetition increases believability** (Hawkins & Hoch, 1992), because familiarity increases the impression of truth, up to the point of wear-out.
- Warnings can backfire: Skurnik and colleagues (2005) showed that repeatedly saying a claim is false makes it, after a delay, remembered as true, especially among older consumers. The familiarity of the claim remains; the context (the notice that it is false) is forgotten.

## Application

- For the brand: a simple claim, repeated consistently over time, gains credibility.
- For myth-correction campaigns: do not repeat the myth in the headline; state the correct fact directly.
- Ethically: the effect makes the choice of claims a responsibility; what is repeated will be believed even if it is false.

## Example

Half-hour tell-sell (teleshopping) programs repeat the same claims dozens of times: a direct application of the effect.

## Questions

- What is the difference between credibility created through repetition and trust in the brand?
- How should an anti-disinformation campaign be built, knowing the Skurnik result?
