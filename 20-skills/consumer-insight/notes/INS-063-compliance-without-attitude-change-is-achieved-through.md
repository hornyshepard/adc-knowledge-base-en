# Compliance without attitude change is achieved through seven principles of influence (Cialdini plus confusion)

> **The idea in one sentence:** at the point of sale and in direct response advertising, people say "yes" by following simple rules, and each rule can be triggered by an experimentally tested technique.

## Explanation

Fennis and Stroebe distinguish **compliance** (accepting a concrete request) from persuasion. Compliance techniques work close to the moment of action, aim at a single act (buy, donate, sign) and influence purchase, not awareness or loyalty. They rely on heuristics applied "mindlessly" (*mindlessness*).

| Principle | Technique / example | Result in the book |
|---|---|---|
| Reciprocity | Door-in-the-face: a huge request that is refused, then a moderate request | 50% agreed to take the juvenile delinquents to the zoo, against 17% (Cialdini et al., 1975); it stops working if the second request comes from another person |
| | That's-not-all: the offer is improved on the spot | 73% vs. 45% bought the cupcake (Burger, 1986); it disappears at high prices |
| | The free sample | creates an uninvited debt, often repaid with more |
| Commitment / consistency | Foot-in-the-door: a small request, then a large one | 76% accepted the large sign after a small one, against 17% (Freedman & Fraser, 1966); doing matters, not only agreeing |
| | Lowball: a good offer, then made worse after the commitment | 95% of those who agreed showed up at 7.00 a.m. |
| Social validation | "Over 700 million VW Golfs sold"; testimonials; the "nerd alert" | works for experience and credence attributes, for collectivists, for people with a need to make an impression; a growing minority can also persuade |
| Liking | Tupperware, "bring a friend", similarity (even the initial of a name), praise after purchase | Tupperware: purchase rates twice as high as those of conventional sales |
| Authority | status symbols (white coat, title, "competent" brands) | a conversation partner wearing Hugo Boss and Coca-Cola induced submissive nonverbal behavior; Milgram: 65% went up to 450 volts |
| Scarcity | limited editions, deadlines, H&M's collaborations with designers | 2 cookies in the jar > 10; scarcity can also increase the scrutiny of arguments |
| Confusion | disrupt-then-reframe (DTR) | see the dedicated note |

## Application

- In calls to action and promotional offers, choose the principle that fits the situation: social validation for services that are hard to evaluate, scarcity for desired products, reciprocity for sampling.
- Combine with care: scarcity fueled by demand is also social validation ("everyone wants it, only a few are left").
- Be aware of the ethical limit: false deadlines or the lowball exploit the consumer.

## Example

Bernard Madoff (pp. 329–330): even financial institutions entrusted him with money because of his authority and reputation.

## Questions

- Which principle is used most in Romanian e-commerce? Which one is abused most?
- Why do these techniques work for a one-off purchase but not for loyalty?
