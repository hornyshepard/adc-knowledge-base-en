# Projective techniques bring to the surface what consumers do not say directly, because they make them talk about others

> **The idea in one sentence:** when people cannot or will not state their motives, you ask them to interpret someone else's behavior in an ambiguous situation, and they project their own feelings onto it.

## Explanation

Interviews and questionnaires run into language, social and psychological obstacles: respondents give socially acceptable answers or avoid uncomfortable questions. Projective techniques get around these obstacles: the participant describes what someone else thinks, and the more vague and ambiguous the stimulus, the more they project. Unlike in a focus group, the participant does not know the real purpose of the exercise.

Batat describes four families:

| Family | How it works | Example from the book |
|---|---|---|
| **Association** | you answer with the first word that comes to mind; test words are mixed with neutral words; what counts is frequency, response time and silences | for detergents, "dirt" is linked to food habits for one woman and to pollution for another: two possible segments |
| **Elements to be completed** | you finish a sentence or a story that has been started | none |
| **Construction** | you invent a story from an image (TAT type) or fill in what a comic character says | American respondents "defended" the people in images who were eating high-fat foods, citing stress: the market for comfort foods |
| **Expression** | role-playing or the third-person technique (what a neighbor or a colleague would think) | none |

The methods answer the "how," "why" and "what" questions about behavior and are rarely used in marketing, although they have large advantages.

## Application

- Add at least one projective exercise to every qualitative session; it costs less than another focus group.
- Use the "third person" for embarrassing topics (money, hygiene, unhealthy eating).
- Treat the results as insight hypotheses, to be checked quantitatively afterwards.
- Simple projective tasks (Parsons, Maclaran and Chatzidakis): completing "Mary bought a new iPhone because ____"; projecting onto a third party, "What would your friends think of people who are driving SUVs?"; interpreting an object that matters to the respondent.

## Example

Mason Haire's study (1950) of Nescafé: instant coffee was failing on the American market although blind tasting pre-tests had been satisfactory, and housewives blamed "the taste". Haire gave two groups two identical shopping lists with one difference (traditional coffee vs. Nescafé). The woman who bought Nescafé was described as a bad wife, poorly organized and spendthrift, and the one who bought traditional coffee as a good wife, good housekeeper and thrifty. The real barrier was the housewife's self-image, not the taste.

## Questions

- How much does the interpretation of projective answers depend on the researcher who reads them?
- How would you adapt the techniques for online research?
