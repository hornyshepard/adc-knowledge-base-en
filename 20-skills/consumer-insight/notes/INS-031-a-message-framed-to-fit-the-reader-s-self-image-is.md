# A message framed to fit the reader's self-image is processed more carefully

> **The idea in one sentence:** a match between the frame and the self-schema does not make the message automatically more persuasive; it makes people read it more carefully, so good arguments gain more and weak ones lose more.

## Explanation

A self-schema is a person's cognitive generalization about his or her own traits, values and beliefs. Fennis and Stroebe present the studies by Wheeler, Petty and Bizer (2005). Extraverts and introverts were shown an ad for a VCR framed either for extraverts (you will be the life of the party) or for introverts (all the luxuries of a movie theater without the crowds), with either strong or weak arguments. When the frame matched the personality, the attitude depended strongly on the quality of the arguments; when it did not match, the arguments mattered less.

The second study replicated the effect with need for cognition: the toothpaste Fluorident was presented either to people who want to know why or to people who do not like to sit around and think about the details.

Other findings: self-related processing increases elaboration, but the effect can turn into irritation and skepticism when the message is too insistent. In addition, the self-schema can bias processing to protect the self-image, and attitudes formed this way resist correction.

## Application

- Personalization (by segment, by trait) makes sense only if you have good arguments: it increases attention, it does not hide weaknesses.
- Write the opening for who the reader is ("You're the type who…"), then deliver substance.
- Test two frames on the same set of arguments: if the matching frame does not help, the problem is the content.

## Example

Fluorident (the Wheeler et al. study): the frame "When you think about it, it's the only choice!" for those who love details, and a no-need-to-think-twice frame for the others.
