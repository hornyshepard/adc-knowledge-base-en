# Attitudes can form without arguments, through three affective routes: mere exposure, conditioning and affect as information

> **The idea in one sentence:** a new brand can come to be liked simply because it was seen often, because it appeared next to pleasant things or because the person was in a good mood when thinking about it.

## Explanation

Fennis and Stroebe see attitude formation (as opposed to attitude change) as a low-effort process, especially for new objects. Besides the cognitive route, there are three affective routes:

| Route | Mechanism | Key evidence | Limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| *Mere exposure* (Zajonc) | familiarity increases fluency, which is felt as liking | Bornstein's meta-analysis (1989): a robust effect, stronger when subliminal, leveling off after 10 to 20 exposures; the Pfeffi/Zitro sweets chosen after the logo had appeared in photos; Eurovision songs performed twice received higher ratings | works for unfamiliar stimuli |
| *Evaluative conditioning* | the valence of a pleasant stimulus (scenery, music, model) passes to the brand | the fictitious mouthwash Garra paired with tropical beaches, an effect that persisted for 3 weeks (Grossman & Till) | does not change the explicit attitude toward well-known, strong brands |
| *Affect as information* | "how do I feel about it?": the mood is attributed to the object | greater life satisfaction on sunny days, which disappears when people are asked about the weather (Schwarz & Clore) | works for consummatory, hedonic motives, not for instrumental ones (Pham, 1998) |

Mere exposure also explains the "wear-in" phase of a campaign. Affect as information explains why clothing shops have music and champagne and hardware stores do not: mood matters for an addition to one's wardrobe, not for a garden hose.

## Application

- For new brands: frequent exposure to a constant visual code (logo, color), even without a message.
- For hedonic products, invest in atmosphere (music, light, tone). For instrumental products, invest in arguments.
- Do not expect beautiful music to change a brand that people know well; there you need new beliefs.

## Example

For decades, Coca-Cola has associated the brand with pleasant music and happy people; the positive attitude remains even when the drink is consumed at home without them (p. 168).

## Questions

- When does repeated exposure become irritating and reduce liking (wear-out)?
- Which local brand could use affect as information at the point of sale?
