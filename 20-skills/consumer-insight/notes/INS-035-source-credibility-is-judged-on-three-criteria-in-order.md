# Source credibility is judged on three criteria, in order (expertise, trustworthiness, motivation), and the receiver grants it, not the sender

> **The idea in one sentence:** a message is more persuasive the more competent, the more trustworthy and the less personally interested the source seems; advertising always starts with a handicap on the third criterion.

## Explanation

Drawing on the research of Carl Hovland and William McGuire, Milavsky describes credibility as the bridge between noticing a message and accepting it as true. It is not binary but a scale. It depends on the subject (you believe your dentist about your teeth, not about fashion), it changes over time, and the receiver assigns it, quickly, from cues.

| Criterion | Cues the fast brain reads |
|---|---|
| **Expertise** (the most important) | credentials, work in the field, champion competitors, respected organizations (ADA, NASA), a little jargon, looking the part (the mechanic with grease on his forehead, the white coat) |
| **Trustworthiness** | consistent behavior over time, familiarity, belonging to the receiver's tribe, guarantees and money-back offers |
| **Motivation** (the weakest) | the more the source gains from the message, the less credible it is |

Milavsky's exercise on motivation: "I am great at understanding how advertising works!" (said by me) < the same praise of Barry said by someone else < the same sentence **overheard**. Hence the power of well-acted slice-of-life commercials and of the unprompted testimonial.

How to build credibility without changing the strategy:

- **the media context**: a medium the audience trusts transfers its aura to the ad (the Texaco commercials embedded in a national news update);
- **the context of the scene**: a hand-written *Farm Fresh Eggs* sign at a rustic cabin, yes; *Laser Vasectomies*, no;
- **the name**: what has a name seems real. A named ingredient (with a capital letter), a named sales event, a full name for an important person all add authority;
- **the narcissism of small differences**: the advertising spotlight makes even a 5 percent difference important.

The limit: a truth that contradicts the target's beliefs is not believed. Tang could have claimed to be 99 percent natural (mostly sugar), but consumers scoffed: the powder and the sugar did not seem natural to them.

**Convergence.** Fennis and Stroebe ([[INS-036-the-celebrity-in-an-ad-is-a-peripheral-cue-at-low|INS-036]]) and the VisCAP model ([[INS-037-the-viscap-model-chooses-the-presenter-by-the-communication|INS-037]]) use the same breakdown (expertise plus trustworthiness or objectivity) and the same observation about the overheard message (Walster & Festinger). The difference: Percy and Rosenbaum-Elliott require objectivity only at high involvement.

## Application

- For every ad, ask: who is speaking, why would I believe them on this subject, and what do they gain?
- Choose the spokesperson for expertise relevant to the product, not for fame.
- Do not use your client, the owner, as presenter unless they have personality; their motivation is obvious.

## Example

Tang grew by borrowing NASA's expertise. When the space flights became rarer, the brand switched to real mothers with PhDs in biological sciences. Some of the target did not believe these women were real scientists, because they were too good looking to fit the stereotype.

## Questions

- Are influencers credible sources by the three criteria? On which criterion do they fail most often?
- Is it ethical to build expertise through a white coat, jargon and casting?
