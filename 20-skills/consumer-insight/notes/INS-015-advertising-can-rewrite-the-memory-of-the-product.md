# Advertising can rewrite the memory of the product experience, especially when the experience is ambiguous

> **The idea in one sentence:** an ad seen before the experience changes what we perceive, and an ad seen after it changes what we remember having perceived; this holds for qualities that are hard to judge.

## Explanation

Fennis and Stroebe bring together several studies:

- **Levin and Gaeth (1988):** ground beef described as "75 per cent lean" was rated better than the same meat described as "25 per cent fat", whether the description came before or after the tasting. The effect was largest on the dimension most related to the frame (fat/lean) and smallest on taste.
- **Hoch and Ha (1986):** ads for JCPenney polo shirts increased their perceived quality, more strongly when the ad came before the inspection. The effect appeared for the shirts (hard to judge) but not for paper towels (easy to judge). The mechanisms: selective information search (you experience what you expect to experience) and selective retrieval.
- **Braun (1999) and Braun-LaTour & LaTour (2005):** after an ad that asked readers to "imagine the taste of fresh-squeezed orange juice … it's sweet, pulpy and pure", participants were worse at identifying the juice they had tasted and erred toward the better version. With a delay between the tasting and the test, the ad seen **after** the experience had a greater effect than the one seen before.

The explanation: memory is reconstructive (Loftus). At every act of remembering, one's own experience is mixed with the information from the ad, especially when the memory has faded. The limits: the effect appears for ambiguous experiences, for "experience" and "credence" attributes more than for verifiable "search" attributes, and it weakens among consumers who are familiar with the product.

## Application

- Post-purchase communication (email, packaging, maintenance advertising) can "fix" a positive memory of the experience.
- For products of taste, comfort and service, where the experience is vague, the sensory language of the ad matters.
- Ethically: you cannot hide a bad product; familiar consumers and clear experiences resist.

## Example

The orange juice study: 46% correct identifications without the ad, against 26% after the ad.

## Questions

- How should a brief that asks to "make people remember the experience as better than it was" be reworded?
- Where does legitimate persuasion end and manipulation begin?
