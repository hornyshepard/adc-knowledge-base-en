# Persuasion follows the central or the peripheral route, depending on the motivation and ability to process (ELM and HSM)

> **The idea in one sentence:** when people want to and can think, good arguments persuade them and they form a lasting attitude; when they do not want to or cannot, simple cues persuade them (the source, the brand name, the length of the message, the mood), and the attitude stays fragile.

## Explanation

Fennis and Stroebe reconstruct the history: the Yale approach (Hovland) and McGuire's chain (attention → comprehension → yielding → retention → behavior, with probabilities that multiply), then the **cognitive response model** (what counts is not the arguments retained but the **own thoughts** generated for or against), then the dual-process theories: the *Elaboration Likelihood Model* (Petty & Cacioppo) and the *Heuristic-Systematic Model* (Chaiken).

| | Central / systematic route | Peripheral / heuristic route |
|---|---|---|
| When | motivation (personal relevance) **and** ability (knowledge, time, no distraction) | one of them is missing |
| What counts | the quality of the arguments (price, material, construction) | cues: the expertise or attractiveness of the source, the brand name, the number of arguments |
| The attitude | lasting, resistant, predictive | weak, easy to change |

The classic study (Petty, Cacioppo & Schumann, 1983), the Edge disposable razor: those who expected to choose a razor were persuaded by the arguments, the uninvolved ones by the celebrity athlete.

Nuances: distraction helps weak arguments (it blocks counterarguments) and hurts strong ones; experts judge arguments, novices cues; a good mood reduces scrutiny; the same variable can play **multiple roles** (Lindsey Vonn's skis: a cue at a glance, an argument on reflection); processing can be biased (defending a position).

**Where the authors converge.** Grewal and Levy teach the textbook version: involvement (interest in the product, which differs from person to person) decides processing, and Lauren, who is looking for an outfit for a job interview, reads the price and the material, while a passer-by retains only the Macy's logo or the celebrity. From the ELM they derive three types of decision (see [[INS-053-involvement-and-perceived-brand-differences-set-the-type-of|INS-053]]). Percy and Rosenbaum-Elliott use the ELM to justify their grid: with low risk the execution may be enough for trial, with high risk the argument must be believed ([[INS-051-involvement-is-the-target-audience-s-perceived-risk-not-an|INS-051]]). Fennis and Stroebe add what the textbooks leave out: the second condition, **ability**. The theoretical disagreement comes from the unimodel ([[INS-030-the-unimodel-cues-and-arguments-are-not-two-routes-but-two|INS-030]]).

## Application

- Diagnose the target audience: is it motivated *and* able right now? For loans and cars, solid arguments; for chewing gum and detergent, a strong, simple, repeated cue.
- What you say matters, and so do the thoughts you provoke: test with *thought listing* (people write down what went through their minds).
- Weak arguments are dangerous with an involved audience: they produce counterarguments and a boomerang effect.

## Example

Edge (pp. 210–211): the same ad, different effects, depending on how relevant the product was to the reader.

## Questions

- Most ads reach an uninvolved audience. Does that mean arguments do not matter?
- On social media, where attention lasts a fraction of a second, is there still central processing?
