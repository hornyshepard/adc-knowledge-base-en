# The purchase decision is shaped by four families of factors: psychological, social, situational and the marketing mix

> **The idea in one sentence:** besides the 4Ps, the consumer's decision is influenced by what goes on in their mind (motives, attitudes, perception, memory, lifestyle), by the people around them (family, reference groups, culture) and by the situation (occasion, senses, temporal state).

## Explanation

Grewal and Levy group the influences into four families (Exhibit 6.4):

**Psychological.**
- *Motives*: a need or want strong enough to demand satisfaction. Maslow's hierarchy (physiological, safety, love, esteem, self-actualization) is applied to Starbucks, which touches all of them: food and drink, nutritional information, baristas who call you by name, a place to read or work. The authors recommend pushing people up the needs hierarchy.
- *Attitudes* (cognitive, affective, behavioral).
- *Perception*: the process by which we select, organize and interpret information. It gives meaning to colors, symbols, packaging.
- *Learning and memory*: encoding, storage and retrieval of information about brands.
- *Lifestyle*: the way you spend your time and money, actual or perceived (the North Face jacket worn for an expedition that will never happen).

**Social.** The family (different roles in the decision), **reference groups** (they inform and build self-image: Birkenstock for earthy, Dior for high fashion), culture.

**Situational.** They can override the usual influences:
- *the purchase situation*: a thrifty person goes to Tiffany & Co. for a best friend's birthday present;
- *the sensory situation*: visual (Dylan's Candy Bar, "retail-tainment"), auditory (loud music at Abercrombie & Fitch, calm music at Starbucks), olfactory (the Cinnabon ovens), tactile, gustatory; the right mix depends on the target;
- *the temporal state*: the time of day, mood (a parking ticket just before shopping).

**The marketing mix**: the 4Ps.

Policarpio (SRC-002) groups the influences differently, in the Kotler tradition: **personal** factors (age, occupation, personality, lifestyle, life-cycle stage, self-image), **economic** factors (income, inflation, savings), **cultural and social** factors (culture, social class, groups, opinion leaders, roles and status) and **psychological** factors (motivation, involvement, learning, attitude, perception). The main difference: Grewal and Levy highlight the situational and sensory factors, which are useful in retail and in activations.

## Application

- Use the list as a research grid for the target: for each family, note one factor that helps and one that holds back.
- In activations and retail, design the sensory situation deliberately for the specific target. The same atmosphere that draws Lauren in puts off her much older friend.
- Online, the sensory distance increases perceived risk, so compensate with images, reviews and return policies.

## Example

Dylan's Candy Bar draws in over two million visitors a year, largely through its decor: candy-filled stairs, dripping chocolate shelves, an oversized lollipop tree. The candy is good, but the presentation sells.

## Questions

- Maslow's hierarchy is contested empirically (needs are not strictly hierarchical). Is it still useful as a working tool?
- Which factor is hardest for a brand to control, and how can you still use it?
