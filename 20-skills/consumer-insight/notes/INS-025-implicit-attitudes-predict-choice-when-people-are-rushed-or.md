# Implicit attitudes predict choice when people are rushed or tired, explicit ones when they have time to control themselves

> **The idea in one sentence:** what consumers declare in a questionnaire and what they automatically feel toward a brand can diverge, and which one wins depends on how much control the person has at the moment of choice.

## Explanation

Fennis and Stroebe define attitude as "the categorization of a stimulus object along an evaluative dimension" (Zanna & Rempel) and show that it can be measured in two ways:

- **explicitly**: self-report scales (semantic differential, Likert); people can "edit" their answer;
- **implicitly**: from automatic reactions, without people knowing that their attitude is being measured: the implicit association test (*IAT*, reaction times to pairs of categories), the affective priming method (*APM*, Fazio) and the affective misattribution procedure (*AMP*, Payne). The authors note that the IAT is popular but controversial.

Why do they diverge? Three explanations: (1) different processes, associative vs. belief-based; (2) **dual attitudes** (Wilson): a person raised on fast food has a positive implicit attitude and a negative explicit one, built later, and advertising can create new attitudes "next to" the old ones; (3) a methodological explanation: the two measures are not phrased with the same specificity (Payne et al., 2008). The evidence is compatible with both interpretations.

The practical rule: the gap appears with ambivalence (dieting and chocolate) or with social desirability (prejudice, "green" behaviors). Friese, Wänke and Plessner (2006): German consumers say generic products are just as good, but they have a bit more trust in branded products. Given ample time, they chose according to their explicit attitude; under time pressure, according to the implicit one. Likewise, under cognitive load, the choice between chocolate and fruit was predicted only by the implicit measure.

## Application

- When you research "embarrassing" or ambivalent categories (fast food, alcohol, luxury, sustainability), do not rely on declarations alone.
- Buying at the shelf is often rushed and distracted: the automatic attitude wins, and with it the brand's emotional associations. That is why chocolate sits next to the till, while the store's own brands do better in areas where shoppers have time to compare prices (ch. 6, p. 277).
- Education campaigns can change the explicit attitude without erasing the implicit one.

## Example

The study by Friese et al. (2006), brands vs. generic products: the same person chooses differently depending on how much time they have.
