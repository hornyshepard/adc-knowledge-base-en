# Online, consumers process heuristically and move their memory to Google: the battle shifts from the consideration set to the place of search

> **The idea in one sentence:** being permanently connected and multitasking creates a chronic cognitive load that favors heuristics, and knowing where to find information makes us stop memorizing it.

## Explanation

Fennis and Stroebe bring together several phenomena specific to the online environment:

- **Chronic cognitive load.** Guadagno et al. (2013) propose that being always online, with several tasks at once, creates a state of load that favors heuristic processing. The threaded cognition framework: multitasking is free of cost only when the tasks use different resources (music + chat), not the same ones (reading a page and an ad on it).
- **The "if it comes from a computer it must be true" heuristic.** The same information about a football player produced higher ratings on a computer screen than in print, especially among novices. Online ads with expert evidence got more clicks than those with causal evidence (Haans et al., 2013).
- **Social proof works online, liking less so.** Supportive comments on a blog increased volunteering, and rejecting ones decreased it; liking of the blog's author did not matter. The implication: reviews and stars (Amazon).
- **Decision support systems.** Comparison matrices (the information function) and recommendation agents (the persuasion function) improve decision quality; only recommendations produce brand switching (Häubl & Trifts, 2000). But a recommendation that contradicts an existing preference, especially from an expert source, provokes reactance and "boomerang choice" (Fitzsimons & Lehmann, 2004). Personalization can narrow exposure and reinforce stereotyping (a hypothesis of Sparrow & Chatman).
- **The Google effect.** Sparrow, Liu and Wegner (2011): after hard questions, the words "Google" and "Yahoo" become more accessible; when people believe information will be saved, they remember it worse, but they remember better **where** it is stored. The internet becomes a transactive memory.
- **The truth effect online** is probably amplified: cognitive load, the computer heuristic and new information looked up with no basis for evaluating it.

The strategic implication the authors draw: if decisions rely on the memory of the internet rather than on one's own, the emphasis moves from getting into the consideration set to **being present where people search and decide**: from content to placement.

## Application

- Invest in presence at the place of decision: search, review platforms, comparison sites.
- Use simple social and expert evidence (stars, number of reviews, "recommended by"), not long arguments.
- Let personalized recommendations complement preferences, not contradict them head-on.

## Example

The Sparrow et al. (2011) study: "we know less facts, but we know better where to find them!"

## Questions

- Does the goal of being "top of mind" still make sense when people look everything up on their phones?
- What responsibility do platforms have for the "bubbles" created by personalization?
