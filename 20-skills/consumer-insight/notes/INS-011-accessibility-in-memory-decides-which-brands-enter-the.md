# Accessibility in memory decides which brands enter the consideration set, not necessarily what is bought

> **The idea in one sentence:** being easy to recall puts you on the shortlist (3–7 brands), but the choice depends on evaluation, except in situations with no clear preference.

## Explanation

Fennis and Stroebe describe long-term memory as an **associative network** (Collins & Loftus): concepts and attributes are nodes connected by links. The links are formed through contiguity (beer and "thirst-quenching" experienced or seen together) and change slowly; the activation of nodes varies quickly and spreads (*spreading activation*). A thirsty person activates "thirst-quenching", and the drink most strongly linked to it comes to mind first.

Consumers do not choose among all the brands they know but from a **consideration set** of 3 to 7 alternatives brought to mind in a particular choice situation. Choices can be:

- **memory-based** (a car: you decide before the dealer): the brand's accessibility is decisive; Lee (2002) shows that presenting the brand in an elaborate context (a sentence) helps;
- **stimulus-based** (the supermarket shelf): simple exposure to the brand name helps.

Nedungadi (1990): a trade ad for the St Hubert chain (nothing about the food) increased the accessibility of the "chicken restaurants" subcategory and led more participants to choose Swiss Chalet, the leader of the subcategory, rather than St Hubert. Priming increased consideration, not liking.

The authors' conclusion: accessibility influences choice only when (1) there is no strong preference, (2) the preferred brand is not available or (3) an attractive but less accessible subcategory is activated.

## Application

- For categories decided from memory, invest in linking the brand to consumption situations (*category entry points*), not only in awareness.
- For the shelf, visual recognition of the name and the packaging is what counts.
- If you lead a subcategory, reminding people of the subcategory can bring you customers from the dominant category.
- Awareness without an argument does not change the preference of a customer loyal to another brand.

## Example

Swiss Chalet (Nedungadi, 1990): choice rose from 4% to 12% after the "chicken" category was activated, even though the ad was for the competitor.

## Questions

- What does this research mean for the idea that "awareness is the first step"?
- How would you measure the consideration set of your target audience?
