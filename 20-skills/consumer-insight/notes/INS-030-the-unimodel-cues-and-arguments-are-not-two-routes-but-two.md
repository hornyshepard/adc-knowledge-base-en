# The unimodel: cues and arguments are not two routes but two kinds of evidence judged by the same rule

> **The idea in one sentence:** Kruglanski argues that a cue (the expertise of the source) and an argument (the performance of the product) are both "if X then Y" premises; the apparent difference between the routes comes only from the fact that cues are usually easier to process and come first.

## Explanation

Fennis and Stroebe acknowledge that dual-process theories became more and more complicated: a variable can be both a cue and an argument (multiple roles), and the two modes of processing can co-occur. Against this background, in the authors' words, "It was thus not an act of murder but more of euthanasia" when the unimodel (Kruglanski & Thompson, 1999) dropped the classic distinction.

The positions in the debate:

| | Dual-process theories (ELM, HSM) | The unimodel |
|---|---|---|
| The nature of the information | cues vs. arguments, qualitatively different | both are evidence used in "if-then" inferences |
| Validity | arguments are usually more valid | there are strong cues (an independent consumer test) and weak arguments |
| Why cues dominate under low involvement | it is the peripheral route | cues are shorter, simpler and presented first in experiments |

The key test (Kruglanski & Thompson, 1999, Study 1): the source's expertise was described in a lengthy one-page résumé, so it was hard to process. The result: expertise influenced attitudes **only** among involved participants; in other words, it behaved like an argument.

The authors' assessment: the unimodel can account for all the findings of dual-process theories and brings theoretical clarity, but it produced few new predictions that the dual-process logic cannot explain. Its value is mostly conceptual.

## Application

- Do not automatically split the elements of an ad into "rational" and "decorative": ask what evidence each one offers and how easy it is to process.
- A strong, simple cue (an independent award, a test by a consumer organization) can persuade even an involved audience.
- If you want an argument to work under low involvement, make it as simple as a cue.

## Example

A television that was the only one in its class to come out highly recommended in a test by a respected consumer report journal: according to the authors, a strong cue, probably stronger than most statements about a technical attribute (p. 220).

## Questions

- Is the central/peripheral distinction useful for creatives, even if the unimodel is right in theory?
- Which elements of a TV ad would be "light evidence" and which "heavy evidence"?
