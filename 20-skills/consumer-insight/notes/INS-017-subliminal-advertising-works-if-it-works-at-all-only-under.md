# Subliminal advertising works, if it works at all, only under very narrow conditions

> **The idea in one sentence:** a brand presented below the threshold of awareness can increase its choice, but only for people who already have the relevant goal (they are thirsty), are not habitual users and choose immediately, and even this effect does not always replicate.

## Explanation

Fennis and Stroebe reconstruct the debate:

1. **The Vicary myth (1957).** James Vicary claimed that the messages "Drink Coca-Cola" and "Eat popcorn", flashed subliminally during a movie, increased sales by 18.1 per cent and 57.7 per cent. The study was never published, was never replicated and is now believed to have been a publicity hoax; even so, subliminal advertising was banned in Britain and Australia.
2. **The self-help tapes.** Greenwald et al. (1991): the subliminal content (self-esteem vs. memory) had no effect at all; the label on the tape produced only a placebo effect: people *believed* they had improved.
3. **The revival.** Karremans, Stroebe and Claus (2006): "Lipton Ice" presented subliminally (25 × 23 ms) increased choice of the drink over mineral water, **only among thirsty participants**. Smarandescu and Shimp (2014) replicated this with Powerade, but the effect disappeared after a 15-minute delay. Verwijmeren et al. (2012): an effect only among those who did not drink Lipton Ice regularly. For Coca-Cola, already highly accessible, subliminal advertising is unlikely to have an effect.
4. **The failed replication.** BBC (2015): 46% vs. 37% chose Lipton Ice, a non-significant difference; more than half preferred water.

The conditions the authors derive: the prime must match the choice situation (the logo, not the name, if the choice is made at the shelf); the product must be seen as a means to an active goal; the brand must not already be the first choice; the choice must be possible immediately.

The authors' ethical conclusion: priming only increases the accessibility of concepts and goals that already exist; it does not change attitudes toward established brands, and it "would not guide us in directions we had no intention of going."

**Tension.** All the sources in the base reject the Vicary myth ([[HIS-026-subliminal-advertising-is-a-myth-that-survives-the-evidence|HIS-026]], [[INS-091-motivation-research-and-psychoanalysis-look-for-the-hidden|INS-091]]), but they disagree about the rest. Fennis and Stroebe (Stroebe co-authored the Lipton Ice study) accept small, conditional effects; Percy and Rosenbaum-Elliott ([[INS-018-percy-and-rosenbaum-elliott-argue-that-unconscious-or|INS-018]]) argue that subliminal processing cannot change attitudes at all.

## Application

- When a client or a journalist asks about "hidden messages", the evidence-based answer is: small, short-lived effects, conditional on existing goals.
- The useful, non-subliminal lesson: linking the brand to a goal that is active in the moment (thirst, tiredness) matters more than exposure itself.

## Example

Lipton Ice (Karremans et al., 2006) and the BBC replication (2015): the same design, different results.

## Questions

- Should subliminal advertising be banned even if its effects are small?
- Why is the Vicary myth still taught as fact in some courses?
