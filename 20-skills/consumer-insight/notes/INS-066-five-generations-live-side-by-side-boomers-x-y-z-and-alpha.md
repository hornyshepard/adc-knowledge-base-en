# Five generations live side by side (Boomers, X, Y, Z and Alpha) and go through four life stages ever faster

> **The idea in one sentence:** for the first time, five generations with contrasting attitudes make up the market and the workforce at the same time, and marketing has to create value today (for the older ones) and brand equity for tomorrow (with the young ones).

## Explanation

The generational cohort starts from the premise that people who grew up in the same period experienced the same significant events and share similar values. *Marketing 5.0* describes five cohorts:

| Generation | Years | Portrait in the book |
|---|---|---|
| **Baby Boomers** | 1946–1964 | "the aging economic powerhouse"; the counterculture; they delay retirement and are criticized for their unwillingness to adopt new technologies |
| **Generation X** | 1965–1980 | the "forgotten middle child"; friends and family, adaptable to technology shifts, leaders in business, many become entrepreneurs in their forties |
| **Generation Y** (Millennials) | 1981–1996 | the first generation of social media; they seek validation from their peers, prefer experience over ownership (Uber, Spotify), question everything |
| **Generation Z** | 1997–2009 | the first digital natives; pragmatic, careful with money, they want authenticity (they hate imagery that is "too good to be true"), personalization, constant engagement |
| **Generation Alpha** | 2010–2025 | the children of Millennials; attached to the iPad, open to branded content, already influencing family decisions |

The targeting dilemma: **value** is created mostly by Boomers and X (resources, willingness to pay), but **brand equity** is created by Y and Z (cool factor, digital savvy), who also influence their parents.

The book proposes four **life stages**, each spanning around 20 years: *Fundamental* (learning, identity), *Forefront* (work, risk, relationships), *Fostering* (family, mentoring, contributing to society), *Final* (adapting to old age, wisdom). Generations Y, Z and Alpha go through them faster and embrace "mature" mindsets at a younger age.

The authors link the generations to the evolution of marketing: 1.0 served the wealthy Boomers, 2.0 responded to the frugality of Generation X, 3.0 emerged with Y and the 2008 crisis, 4.0 with the digitalization of Y and Z, and 5.0 with Z and Alpha.

***Marketing 6.0*** calls Z and Alpha **phygital natives**: they see no boundary between physical and digital, they are "AI natives" and "metaverse natives", yet they shop in physical stores more than Millennials do (McKinsey). Kids are "getting older younger" (KGOY); they are pragmatic (they put more weight on functional benefits than on emotional appeal and are less swayed by brand recognition), they demand unfiltered authenticity and build a consistent digital persona across all platforms.

## Application

- When you target a generation, check two things: the real life stage (not only the year of birth) and who influences whom in the family.
- For Gen Z: drop the polished imagery and give control (personalization, customization).
- In mixed agency teams, discuss generational differences in taste openly: the book's case is a minimalist ad for Millennials, rejected by a 50-year-old manager who wanted detailed benefits.

## Example

Google/Ipsos research shows that 74% of Millennial parents involve their Generation Alpha children in household decisions.

## Questions

- Generational cohorts are criticized as stereotypes: the differences within a generation can be larger than those between generations. When is the lens still useful?
- The book also says the future is "segments of one". How does that fit with segmenting by cohort?
