# Advertising does not create needs, but it can cultivate desires and materialism, especially in children

> **The idea in one sentence:** longitudinal evidence shows that exposure to advertising increases product desire and materialism in children, but the same exposure can also have indirect positive effects on well-being.

## Explanation

The old debate: does advertising create needs, or does it only channel existing needs into wants for brands (Kotler's position)? Fennis and Stroebe accept that many ads are ineffective and that needs are not created, but they show that advertising can cultivate **desires** and **materialism**: the tendency to place a particular value on obtaining and displaying material possessions.

The evidence, in order of solidity:

- **Sirgy et al. (2012)**, a cross-national survey: advertising perceptions linked to materialism, more frequent social comparisons, dissatisfaction and lower life satisfaction. The authors warn: cross-sectional data, so no causal conclusions, and the measure is partly tautological.
- **Opree et al. (2014)**, a 12-month longitudinal study of children aged 8–11: exposure to shows with a high advertising density increased desire for the advertised products, and this in turn increased materialism. Children are vulnerable because they do not yet have **persuasion knowledge**, the understanding of the tactics marketers use.
- **Opree et al. (2016)**, a panel of 1,133 children: no direct effect of exposure on life satisfaction, but an indirect positive effect, through psychological well-being.

The authors' conclusion: advertising can be both a blessing and a curse; the useful question is for whom and under what conditions.

Parsons, Maclaran and Chatzidakis (SRC-013) frame the same debate as "mirror or mould": Pollay (1986) sees advertising as a shaping force that reinforces materialism, anxiety and social competition; Holbrook (1987) answers that it mirrors the audience's values rather than shaping them. They also mention the effects on the body image of young women (very thin models) and the vulnerability of children and older people.

## Application

- In campaigns for children, take their vulnerability seriously: they do not "decode" an ad the way adults do.
- In debates about advertising ethics, distinguish between need (it is not created) and desire (it can be cultivated).
- Use the distinction in responsible briefs too: which desire are we feeding, and at what cost?

## Example

The Opree et al. (2014) study: exposure measured by how often children watched nine shows high in advertising, a more reliable measure than asking "which ads have you seen?".

## Questions

- Should advertising to children under a certain age be banned?
- Does the same mechanism apply to teenagers exposed to influencers?
