# Consumers declare ethical values but buy out of habit, convenience and social pressure (the attitude-behavior gap)

> **The idea in one sentence:** the ethical consumer is a situation more than a type of person: what they buy depends on habits, on the effort of getting informed, on what the people around them do and on where they live.

## Explanation

Parsons, Maclaran and Chatzidakis define ethical consumerism as any conscious and deliberate decision to make certain consumption choices because of personal moral beliefs (Crane & Matten). Ethical consumption has left its niche, but there is a well-documented gap between what people declare and what they buy. The reasons identified in the book:

| Reason | Example |
|---|---|
| Habits built by marketing | the cupboard full of Jif, Mr Muscle, Ajax; the natural alternatives (bicarbonate of soda, vinegar) have to be learned |
| The cost of information | palm oil is in around one in ten supermarket products; avoiding it means reading labels, in a "culture of speed" |
| The separation of production from consumption | Nike, accused of exploitation, keeps being bought; the person wearing Converse feels cool, not complicit |
| Social context | ethics are contextual: we recycle if others do too (Devinney et al., *The Myth of the Ethical Consumer*) |
| Place | the Exarcheia neighborhood of Athens encourages ethical and anti-consumption practices through its ethos (Chatzidakis et al., 2012) |
| Trade-offs | farmers' markets are local, but often expensive and in richer neighborhoods; palm oil in biofuels destroys forests |

Other nuances: **political consumerism** (boycott and "buycott") is a form of "individualized collective action" (Micheletti), and studies show that political consumers are more involved in traditional politics too, not less. The critique: individual solutions to structural problems, even a "fetishism of guilt" (we consume ethical signs to reassure ourselves). A psychoanalytic reading (ch. 5) suggests that inconsistency can discharge unconscious guilt.

**Convergence with Fennis and Stroebe (SRC-012):** the theory of planned behavior explains the gap through social norms and perceived control, and the research on habits shows that, in stable contexts, past behavior beats intention.

## Application

- Do not assume that the attitude declared in a survey ("I would pay more for green") will translate into sales.
- Reduce the cost of the ethical choice: make it the default, visible, convenient, at the same price.
- Use social norms ("your neighbors recycle") and place (the point of sale), not only moral arguments.
- Target the moments when habits change.

## Example

Nike in the 1990s: boycotts and negative press over factory conditions, yet sales continued; only the pressure on its image led to changes (and, in 2011, to its quick response to the Greenpeace Detox campaign).

## Questions

- Which "ethical" product in Romania enjoys a favorable public attitude but low sales? Why?
- Is it the responsibility of the consumer or of the state to close this gap?
