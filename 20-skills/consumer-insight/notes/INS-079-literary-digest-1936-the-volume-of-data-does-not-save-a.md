# Literary Digest, 1936: the volume of data does not save a wrong sample frame

> **The idea in one sentence:** millions of answers from the wrong people give a wrong conclusion with an air of certainty; a few thousand well-chosen people give a correct one.

## Explanation

In 1936, the magazine *Literary Digest* mailed 10 million letters to US voters and got back some 2.4 million responses. It predicted that Alf Landon would win 53% to 47%. Roosevelt won 62% to 38%. The names had come from telephone directories, magazine subscription lists and club memberships, that is, from the better-off people of the Great Depression. George Gallup, with a much smaller but scientifically selected sample, got the result right.

Struhl uses the case to explain the **sample frame**: the place where you gather your data. Like the frame of a picture, if it does not fit, you do not see the picture correctly. The lesson applies directly to digital data. All the people who say on a social media site that they drink beer may number in the millions and still not represent beer drinkers in general.

The idea connects to the distinction between **data and information**: data is anything that can be measured (lies and exaggerations included); information is what results after processing and helps you act. More data of the wrong kind makes the search harder: "You do not find a needle more easily by having a bigger haystack." A data lake without a purpose becomes a data swamp.

## Application

- Before you read any figure (social listening, an online survey, CRM), ask: "who is missing from this frame?"
- Check the client's databases. At a bank, 60% of the customers contacted in a survey no longer had a working relationship with the bank, and 25% of the contact persons were dead. The salespeople had not entered the data properly.
- Prefer a clear question and a suitable sample to a huge volume collected "just in case".

## Example

The Literary Digest case is the standard example. Today's equivalent: a brand that assesses its image only from comments on Instagram and ignores the customers who never post.

## Questions

- Which groups are systematically absent from the social media data of a consumer goods brand?
- When is a non-representative sample still useful (for generating ideas, for example)?
