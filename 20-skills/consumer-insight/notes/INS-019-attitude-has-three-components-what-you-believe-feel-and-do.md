# Attitude has three components (what you believe, feel and do), and communication can change the first two

> **The idea in one sentence:** an attitude is an enduring evaluation of one's feelings and behavioral tendencies toward an object; it has a cognitive, an affective and a behavioral component, and persuasion succeeds when the first two work together.

## Explanation

Grewal and Levy define attitude as a person's enduring evaluation of his or her feelings about and behavioral tendencies toward an object or idea. Attitudes are learned and long lasting and slow to change, though they can also change abruptly: you like your instructor until she returns your first exam.

The three components:

| Component | What it contains | The Matt and Lisa example (the Avengers movie) |
|---|---|---|
| Cognitive | beliefs, what you believe to be true | the critics say the film is great, so it is probably good |
| Affective | emotions, liking or dislike | an interview with Robert Downey Jr. and their enjoyment of Marvel films make them feel it will be fun |
| Behavioral | the action that follows from what you know and feel | they weigh the alternatives and go to the movie |

Ideally, the three agree. When they do not (good reviews, but they do not like the actor), cognitive dissonance can occur. People reduce it by convincing themselves that the decision was a good one: they focus on the special effects and gloss over the scenes with the actor they do not like.

The marketing conclusion: communication (salespeople, ads, free samples) can change what people believe about a product (the cognitive component) or how they feel toward it (the affective component). That is why, the authors say, marketers have an ethical responsibility to communicate truthfully. When it succeeds, cognition and affect align and produce the behavior.

**Disagreement.** Percy and Rosenbaum-Elliott ([[INS-020-brand-attitude-is-a-relative-evaluation-made-of-beliefs-and|INS-020]]) keep only the cognitive and affective components and treat behavioral intention as a separate effect. Fennis and Stroebe ([[INS-022-attitudes-can-form-without-arguments-through-three|INS-022]]) show that an attitude can even be purely affective, with no belief behind it.

## Application

- Diagnose where the block is: the target audience does not know (cognitive), does not feel (affective), or knows and feels but does not act (behavioral, so a trigger, an offer or access is needed)?
- The rational message targets the cognitive component, the emotional one the affective component. A good campaign links them coherently.
- Use samples and experiences when you want to change the affective component directly.

## Example

Matt and Lisa first wanted to see Fast and Furious. The good reviews for Avengers changed their cognitive component and made it consistent with the affective one (they already enjoyed Marvel films), so they chose Avengers.

## Questions

- Is the order "believe → feel → do" always the same? With impulse purchases, is it not reversed?
- Can an ad change an entrenched attitude, or only activate an existing one?
