# Recall and recognition are weak measures of an ad's effectiveness

> **The idea in one sentence:** an ad can change attitudes without being remembered, and a remembered ad can change nothing, so tests of explicit memory say little about persuasion.

## Explanation

Industry practice measures effectiveness with recall tests (what do you remember?) and recognition tests (have you seen this?). Fennis and Stroebe give two reasons why this logic is wrong.

**1. Incidental exposure leaves traces in implicit memory.** Yoo (2008): users who browsed web pages concentrating on the text did not recognize the banners any better than the control group, yet their attitudes towards the brand were as positive as those of the people who had been told to pay attention to the ads. Shapiro and Krishnan (2001): the effect on implicit memory (choosing the brand from a list) was as strong under divided attention and after a week, while recognition had disappeared.

**2. Judgments are formed "online".** Hastie and Park (1986): when people know they have to make a judgment, they form their evaluation while receiving the information and no longer need to retain the arguments; the correlation between what they remember and what they believe disappears. Kardes (1986): the overall evaluation (which predicts purchase intention) is stored separately from the concrete attributes. What is more, some arguments are remembered precisely because they were rejected.

That is why, the authors say, the right criterion of effectiveness is attitude change (and behavior change), measured experimentally, not memory for the ad.

**Convergence.** Percy and Rosenbaum-Elliott reach the same rule from pretesting practice ([[ADV-079-the-pretest-is-built-on-the-communication-objectives-with|ADV-079]]): you do not measure recall of the ad, but attitude and intention. Their reason is different, because they require conscious processing, but the conclusion for research is identical.

## Application

- In pretests and post-tests, measure attitude and preference towards the brand (against a control group), not only recall.
- Use implicit measures too (word stem completion, choice from a list) for ads in cluttered media (banners, OOH).
- Do not reject an execution only because it has low recall; do not approve one only because it is remembered.

## Example

The "unnoticed" banner from the Yoo study: no recognition above the control group, but an attitude as good as that of the attentive group.

## Questions

- Why does recall persist as a KPI in agencies, although it is criticized?
- When does recall remain a useful measure (for example, for memory-based choices)?
