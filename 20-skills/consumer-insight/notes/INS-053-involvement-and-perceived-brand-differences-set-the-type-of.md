# Involvement and perceived brand differences set the type of decision: extended, limited, habitual or variety-seeking

> **The idea in one sentence:** how involved people are (and how different the brands seem to them) decides how much of the decision process they go through, and a brief for a detergent and one for an SUV cannot follow the same logic.

## Explanation

**On a single axis: involvement** (Grewal and Levy, derived from the ELM):

- **Extended problem solving**: high perceived risk, a lot of search (Lauren and the interview outfit: financial and social risk).
- **Limited problem solving**: moderate effort, prior experience. It includes **impulse buying**: popcorn and Dr Pepper picked up near the checkout for a movie night.
- **Habitual decision making**: almost no conscious effort (In-N-Out Burger on the way home). Customers are kept through strong brands and loyalty.

**On two axes: the Assael matrix** (Policarpio, in the Kotler-Armstrong tradition):

| | Large brand differences | Small brand differences |
|---|---|---|
| **High involvement** | **Complex**: research and comparison (a car) | **Dissonance-reducing**: a quick purchase, then doubts (an air conditioner) |
| **Low involvement** | **Variety-seeking**: you switch brands out of curiosity | **Habitual**: the same toothpaste, without evaluation |

The communication tasks: in complex buying, you help people learn the attributes of the product class and show where the brand wins; with dissonance, reassurance after the purchase; with habit, the brand is learned passively, through repetition, visual symbols and short messages (familiarity, not conviction); with variety-seeking, the leader reinforces the habit (a full shelf, reminders), while the challenger prompts trial (coupons, samples).

**Read critically.** Policarpio wrongly describes dissonance-reducing buying as a FOMO-based tactic; in the original model, dissonance appears *after* the purchase. His example of variety-seeking (researching a car) contradicts the very definition. Both sources, however, tie involvement to the category, even though Grewal and Levy admit that it differs from person to person: exactly the criticism that Percy and Fennis carry all the way through ([[INS-051-involvement-is-the-target-audience-s-perceived-risk-not-an|INS-051]]).

## Application

- Place the target (not only the category) in the matrix before choosing the creative strategy.
- With habit, frequency and visual distinctiveness matter more than argument; the challenger has to break the leader's routine (a trial offer).
- With impulse purchases, work with retail: the cue at the point of sale is worth as much as a commercial.

## Example

Toothpaste and shampoo: customers buy the same brand again without comparing, so the leader wins through constant presence on the shelf and in the media (SRC-002, p. 66).

## Questions

- Is involvement a trait of the product or of the person? Where would a fast-fashion product bought online sit?
- In impulse purchases, which stages of the decision disappear?
