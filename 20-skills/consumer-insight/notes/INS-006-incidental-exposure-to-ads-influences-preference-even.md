# Incidental exposure to ads influences preference even without conscious attention

> **The idea in one sentence:** ads "on the side", the ones we do not look at on purpose, are processed preattentively, including at the level of meaning, and they can change later choices.

## Explanation

Most exposure to advertising is incidental: a banner next to an article, an ad between news items. Fennis and Stroebe show that preattentive processing does not stop at physical features (contours, brightness and contrast: *feature analysis*). It can also include a **semantic analysis**: what the product is and what it does. The difference matters: if the memory trace were only perceptual, the ad would help only when the product on the shelf looked identical to the product in the ad.

Shapiro (1999) placed ads in the left-hand column of a mock magazine while participants read the articles in the right-hand column. Products shown **in a usage context** (a person carrying a briefcase) were included more often in later choices than isolated products, even when the choice was made from a list of verbal labels. The influence operated outside conscious awareness.

Janiszewski (1988–1993) used the hypothesis of **matching activation**: when one brain hemisphere is busy with the focal stimulus (pictures → right; text → left), the other one is mobilized and processes the non-focal material. In the ad for Guerlain's Shalimar perfume, the brand name was evaluated more favorably when it was placed **to the right of the lady's face** or **to the left of the slogan**.

The authors also note that a negative urgency signal (pain, danger) can be detected preattentively and can then capture conscious attention.

**Tension.** Percy and Rosenbaum-Elliott ([[INS-018-percy-and-rosenbaum-elliott-argue-that-unconscious-or|INS-018]]) argue that without conscious processing, the message does not reach declarative memory and cannot form attitudes. Fennis and Stroebe answer with effects on implicit memory, measured through choice, not recall ([[INS-082-recall-and-recognition-are-weak-measures-of-an-ad-s|INS-082]]).

## Application

- Show the product in use, not only the packshot: you anchor the concept, not only the shape.
- In the layout, think about where a person who only glances at the ad looks (the face) and put the brand where it can be picked up peripherally.
- In media, take into account which ad appears on the opposite page (text vs. picture).

## Example

The outdoor ad for Advil, with a man holding his head: the visible pain stirs a preattentive sense of urgency, immediately channeled to the brand and to the slogan "more powerful than pain" (p. 61).

## Questions

- How solid are the hemispheric lateralization effects? They should be tested before they become layout rules.
- What do these results mean for measuring effectiveness through recall?
