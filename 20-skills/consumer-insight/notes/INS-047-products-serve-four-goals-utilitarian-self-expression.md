# Products serve four goals (utilitarian, self-expression, identity-building and hedonic), and the message has to hit the right one

> **The idea in one sentence:** to change someone's attitude toward a product, you need to know what they want it for; arguments that match the product's function generate more favorable thoughts.

## Explanation

Functional theory (Katz, 1960) says that people hold attitudes because these serve goals: adjustment (maximizing rewards), value expression, ego defense and knowledge. Fennis and Stroebe shift the emphasis from the function of the attitude to the **function of the product** and distinguish four consumer goals:

| Goal | What people look for | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Utilitarian | performance, reliability, price | washing machine, knives, tools |
| Self-expression | to show others who they are | designer clothes, Cartier, Mini Cooper |
| Identity-building | to become who they want to be (Belk: possessions as the extended self) | a Range Rover makes you feel rugged, not just look it |
| Hedonic | pleasure in itself | good wine, jewelry, "Freude am Fahren" (BMW) |

Evidence: Shavitt (1990): ads with arguments matching the product's function (utilitarian for coffee, self-expression for perfume) produced more favorable thoughts. Snyder and DeBono (1985): **high self-monitors** (people concerned about the image they project) preferred the image-oriented whisky ad ("You're not just moving in, you're moving up"), low self-monitors the quality-oriented one; replications, however, are inconsistent. Rucker and Galinsky (2009): people who feel low in power prefer stylish products of lower quality and visible logos; conspicuous consumption (Veblen) is more frequent among those who want to appear higher up, not among the real elite.

Many brands serve several goals. In print, the text often sells the utilitarian side (the BMW's safety), while the picture sells self-expression (the car in front of an expensive hotel).

## Application

- In the brief, write down explicitly the target's dominant goal: utility, image, identity or pleasure.
- Use the text/image division for products with a dual function.
- With status brands, do not assume the audience is wealthy: often it is the one who aspires.

## Example

Nike (p. 175): a no-brand running shoe serves the goal of jogging comfortably just as well, but not the goal of looking fashionable; consumers pay for that one through Nike's advertising budget.

## Questions

- What goal does an iPhone serve for a student? And for a director?
- Is the goal a property of the product or of the person (the same criticism as for the FCB grids)?
