# Asked directly what matters, people say "everything": importance ratings do not predict behavior

> **The idea in one sentence:** on a scale from 1 to 5, saying that everything is important costs nothing; only when they have to choose do people reveal their real hierarchy.

## Explanation

Struhl cites a review by Morwitz, who went through more than 60 years of research on ratings and found no consistently good way to predict behavior from them. The theoretical exception would be a product category that is not changing, with buyers who are not changing and a rich history of sales and ratings data. That practically never happens.

Four biases explain the failure:

| Bias | What happens |
|---|---|
| Acquiescence bias | people avoid negative answers; most responses fall into the top two points of the scale |
| Straight-line or extreme responses | the same answer over and over, from lack of effort |
| Socially desirable responses | the "right" answer (safety features in a car are "essential"), although cars with poor safety ratings sell well |
| Cultural skew | Japanese doctors rate their favorite product 7 out of 10; Latin American doctors give 9–10 even to products they would never use |

On top of that, people do not describe accurately how they decide. About a product they rejected, they say only that it was not for them. That is why **trade-off methods** appeared: they force people to prioritize, as in real life.

## Application

- Do not build a sentence of the brief on an attribute that came out "very important" in a survey with rating scales. Check whether it was weighed against others.
- In your own questionnaires, replace "how important is X?" with forced choices: "which of these would you keep if you had to give one up?"
- When you compare countries, do not compare the raw scores.

## Example

**The essential 3 am banker.** A major bank entered the Chicago market with signs in its windows: "Talk to a live banker 24 hours a day!!!" The survey with importance ratings said that talking to a live banker was critical. Within a few months, the signs changed to until 1 am, then until midnight, then until 10 pm, then came down. Nobody needed a banker at 3 am; people simply had not said no to what looked like a free offer.

## Questions

- Which attributes do consumers declare "essential" out of social desirability (sustainability, natural ingredients)?
- How can a copywriter use the difference between what people say and what they choose?
