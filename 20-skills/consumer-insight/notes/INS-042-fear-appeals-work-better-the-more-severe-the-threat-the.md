# Fear appeals work better the more severe the threat: the idea that strong fear blocks the message has not been confirmed

> **The idea in one sentence:** meta-analyses show that the severity of the threat increases acceptance of the recommendation, and people who feel vulnerable tend to minimize the danger but to overrate the remedy offered.

## Explanation

Fennis and Stroebe (Stroebe contributed directly to this research) trace a 70-year debate:

| Position | Claim | State of the evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Yale, Janis & Feshbach (1953) | the weakest appeal worked best in the dental hygiene experiment; strong fear leads to defensive avoidance | rarely replicated |
| The drive reduction model | more fear → more persuasion, if the recommended action is effective | partial |
| Leventhal; Witte | parallel responses: danger control vs. fear control; the efficacy of the recommendation decides | the predicted interaction does not appear empirically |
| The stage model (Das, De Wit & Stroebe; De Hoog et al.) | severity and vulnerability decide the intensity of processing; vulnerable people process the threat defensively, but the recommendation positively | supported by the meta-analysis of 95 studies (De Hoog et al., 2007) |

Results of the meta-analysis: the attitude toward the recommendation depends on severity and on argument quality, not on personal vulnerability. Intention and behavior depend on both, with no moderation by the efficacy of the recommended action. Pictures added no effect, so pictures of diseased lungs on cigarette packs are unlikely to increase the efficacy of the warning. The explanation for the appeal of unproven treatments: frightened people want to believe that the remedy works.

The book names the types of risk used in ads: physical, social, product performance, financial and opportunity. Fear can also be detected preattentively (as an urgency signal), provided that the ad immediately offers a simple remedy.

**The tension.** Percy and Rosenbaum-Elliott ([[INS-032-personality-does-not-choose-the-target-audience-but-it|INS-032]], [[ADV-048-social-marketing-communication-must-change-attitude-and|ADV-048]]) still recommend moderate fear, at the peak of an inverted U, which is exactly the hypothesis that the De Hoog et al. meta-analysis does not confirm. The difference lies partly in what each measures: attention to the ad or acceptance of the recommendation.

## Application

- For social campaigns: communicate the severity clearly and offer a concrete, easy action; do not automatically fear too much fear.
- Put as much care into the recommendation as into the threat: vulnerable people will process it favorably.
- Use social risk ethically: it can sell, but it can also create unjustified anxiety.

## Example

Listerine (1920s–1930s): the medical-sounding term "halitosis", slogans about social rejection ("Even your best friend won't tell you"), with a remedy at hand. A very successful campaign, even though, according to the American Medical Association, mouthwashes do not correct bad breath for any length of time.

## Questions

- Why does the belief that fear does not work persist in agencies?
- Where is the ethical line between informing about a real risk and creating a problem that does not exist?
