# Do not contradict the target's belief: start from it and make it evolve; confrontation triggers defense

> **The idea in one sentence:** people's first reaction to new information is to defend their current beliefs; an ad that negotiates with what they already believe has a chance, one that attacks those beliefs is ignored or refuted.

## Explanation

The first element of the creative strategy for Milavsky is the target's **currently held belief**: about the brand, the competitor, the category, the problem the product solves. It is the leverage point of the whole strategy.

The psychological argument: our opinions sit in the fast brain, ready for use. Anything that challenges them directly produces a defensive reaction of the fight-or-flee kind: counterarguments or avoidance. Milavsky cites McGuire's work on resistance to persuasion and Kahneman's observation that people asked a difficult question answer an easier one, without noticing. Social media and cable news channels have exploited the same tendency: we choose the sources that confirm our beliefs.

Hence the rule: promise something that answers a need **from inside** the existing belief, so that people adjust their conviction without feeling attacked. Persuasion changes minds by making them evolve, not by beating them in an argument. The chosen belief does not have to be universal, but the majority of the target should agree with it right away. Depending on it, the strategy becomes one of differentiation (if all products seem to deliver at parity) or of strengthening the perception (if the product is seen as weak).

## Application

- Before the promise, write the sentence "Right now, the target believes that…". Check with real people whether they would sign it.
- Look for the common ground you can negotiate on, not the argument that proves the target wrong.
- The same logic works inside the client too: find out what the decision-makers already believe before you propose an idea to them.

## Example

**Ortega Taco** (Heublein, the 1970s): the accepted belief among Americans was that Mexican food was weird, foreign and very spicy. Instead of fighting the idea or selling authenticity, the ads showed blond Midwestern families and nerdy little kids filling the taco shell with common ingredients: hamburger meat, lettuce, tomatoes, cheese. The taco was positioned as a crunchy hamburger. The alternative ("use Ortega Tacos to feel more Mexican") would have limited the brand to an ethnic niche and to Cinco de Mayo.

## Questions

- Are there moments when directly confronting a belief works (shock social campaigns, new categories)?
- Where does starting from the existing belief turn into simply confirming prejudices?
