# Personas, not segments: Ozzy Osbourne and Prince Charles have the same demographic profile

> **The idea in one sentence:** socio-demographic segmentation tells you how many people you have to reach and with what budget; the persona tells you what interests them, what stops them and how to talk to them.

## Explanation

Richards and McLean give a striking example: two men born in 1948, who grew up in the UK, have divorced and re-married, have grown-up children, are very wealthy, like to spend their holidays in the Alps and are world famous. On paper they are twins. They are Ozzy Osbourne and Prince Charles, and it is hard to believe they would respond to the same message.

The authors do not throw segmentation away: it stays useful for sizing the market and the budget. The closest to what you need is **needs-based** segmentation. For content and conversation, though, they propose the **persona**, built from:

| Element | Example (the bank, the "Working Parents" persona) |
|---|---|
| Start point | with the same bank for years; changing seems more trouble than it is worth |
| Needs / pains / goals | would like to extend the house; moving would be too expensive |
| Barriers | getting by as they are; they do not want to take on any more debt |
| Influencers | the papers, friends, family |
| Attitude | "Won't be able to top up the mortgage so why even think about it?" |
| Purchase trigger points | an easy top-up process that consolidates other debt into the mortgage |

Attitudes can be placed on a **heat map** (negative / neutral / positive toward the bank, innovation, risk, investments) that shows where each persona starts. The book's rule of thumb: **no more than seven personas**, because you cannot differentiate one piece of content more than seven different ways. And the persona changes not only the topic but also **how the story is told**: the same venue is described one way for Mum (where is the cafe, where is the childcare) and another way for the child (what games can I play).

Bridgette Braig (*Kellogg on Branding*, ch. 13) gives the persona the same function: a concrete person, with goals, frustrations and habits, who becomes a filter for decisions and messages.

## Application

- Build the persona from the table above, not from age and income. The "barriers" column is the most useful one for the creative work.
- Check whether you can **identify** the persona in the client's data; otherwise you cannot target it directly. On inbound channels, the audience selects itself if you write well for it.
- Merge personas that end up on the same journey; complexity drops sharply.

## Example

At a company with a technical product set, the engineers wrote the user manuals themselves. Without personas, they ended up writing for themselves, not for the beginners who actually needed the manual.

## Questions

- What information about Ozzy and Charles would a strategist have to add to tell them apart?
- When is a persona only an attractive stereotype, with no data behind it?
