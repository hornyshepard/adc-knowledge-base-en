# The connected customer lives with three paradoxes: online vs. offline, informed vs. distracted, negative vs. positive advocacy

> **The idea in one sentence:** connectivity makes customers not only more powerful but also more contradictory: they want high-touch in a high-tech world, they know a lot but decide by what others say, and a brand's critics often wake up its defenders.

## Explanation

The new typical customer, the authors say, is young, urban and middle-class, with strong mobility and connectivity, and trusts their own network more than companies. Connectivity has three levels: **mobile** (the internet as communications infrastructure), **experiential** (the internet delivering a superior experience at touchpoints) and **social** (the strength of connection in communities). It produces three paradoxes:

1. **Online interaction versus offline interaction.** Online will not replace offline. In a high-tech world, high-touch interaction becomes the new differentiation: Birchbox opened a brick-and-mortar store, Zappos wins through its call center, Bank of America lets customers at the ATM video-chat with a personal teller. Conversely, technology enriches the physical experience (Macy's beacons, the John Lewis sofa studio with 3-D-printed miniatures).
2. **Informed customer versus distracted customer.** Customers research a lot, but they are not in control of what they buy. The decision comes from three sources (marketing communications, the opinions of friends and family, their own experience), and under the pressure of screens the opinion of others wins. The book cites the drop in attention span from 12 seconds (2000) to 8 seconds (2013). The conclusion: only brands with WOW! factors are worth listening to, and the brand needs advocates who sway the decision.
3. **Negative advocacy versus positive advocacy.** The Net Promoter Score subtracts detractors from promoters, but a brand with a strong DNA polarizes. The book cites YouGov BrandIndex: McDonald's has 33% lovers and 29% haters, Starbucks 30% and 23%. Spontaneous advocacy is rare; the most common kind is prompted advocacy, which stays dormant and is activated when someone asks or when someone criticizes the brand.

## Application

- In research, do not ask only "what do you know about the brand", but "whom would you ask and what would they tell you".
- Do not run from polarization: a brand without haters risks a dull conversation. Build instead an "army of lovers" who defend it.
- Design the experience as a mix: where do you win through technology, and where through people?

## Example

Amazon, a brand born online, needed a "physical channel": the Dash Button, which automatically replenishes coffee or detergent. The online/offline paradox works both ways.

## Questions

- The 8-second figure (attention "shorter than a goldfish's") is disputed by researchers. How do you use the idea without passing on a myth?
- When does polarization become a real risk for the brand rather than an asset?
