# Percy and Rosenbaum-Elliott argue that unconscious or subliminal processing of an ad cannot change attitudes or behavior

> **The idea in one sentence:** the unconscious only filters and proposes what reaches consciousness; without conscious processing, the message does not reach declarative memory, so it cannot form attitudes, and the subliminal myth was made up.

## Explanation

Chapter 12 takes a firm stand against an idea that is popular both in agencies and in some academic circles: that advertising influences us through unconscious routes. The authors' arguments:

1. **Neurological.** Unconscious processing goes on all the time, as a necessary step *before* consciousness (the filter of selective attention, the preconscious "buffer"). But the neural systems involved do not communicate with each other. During unconscious processing, brain activity stays within the narrow boundaries of the left temporal lobe. Consciousness involves much larger neural networks in the frontal lobe. Only consciousness allows lasting thoughts. The authors' formula, after Dehaene: "the unconscious mind can only propose. It is the conscious mind that selects."
2. **Preconscious ≠ subliminal.** A preconscious stimulus can become conscious if you attend to it (you have now noticed your hands). A subliminal one will never become conscious, no matter how hard you try.
3. **Historical.** The 1950s story of "Drink Coca-Cola" and "Eat popcorn" flashed subliminally in a movie theater never happened. In the 1970s, James Vicary admitted that he made it up to promote his consultancy business. Moore's studies found no effects even when the cues were included on purpose. A review in the early 1990s found over 200 studies that failed to find any effect on behavior.
4. **Priming.** The mere exposure effect (Zajonc) produces a slight familiarity. It could matter only in a choice between two unfamiliar brands. *Priming* is a non-declarative memory, separate from declarative memory, and it cannot inform higher cognitive processes.

The only exception the authors accept is **emotion**: non-declarative emotional memory enters working memory and frames conscious processing. But it, too, acts *during* conscious processing, not in its place.

**The tension.** Many researchers (those who work on implicit processing, implicit attitudes or neuromarketing) and practitioners who talk about "System 1" argue that much of advertising's effect is implicit, through associations formed during low-attention exposures. The authors themselves cite in their bibliography an article (Williams & Poehlman) that proposes a narrower view of consciousness in persuasion. The difference is partly one of definitions: where does "brief reflexive attention" end and where does "the unconscious" begin?

In the base, the tension has a concrete counterpart: Fennis and Stroebe ([[INS-017-subliminal-advertising-works-if-it-works-at-all-only-under|INS-017]], [[INS-006-incidental-exposure-to-ads-influences-preference-even|INS-006]], [[INS-082-recall-and-recognition-are-weak-measures-of-an-ad-s|INS-082]]) document small implicit and subliminal effects, conditional on goals, which Percy and Rosenbaum-Elliott reject.

## Application

- Do not build a strategy on subliminal effects or on "passive exposure" without attention. Design the ad to win at least reflexive attention and to link the brand to a benefit.
- Be skeptical of methodologies that promise influence "under the radar". Ask for evidence of an effect on attitudes or sales.
- Use emotion consciously: it shapes processing, but it does not replace the message.

## Example

A client proposes that the logo appear for a fraction of a second, undetectable, in videos. According to the authors, the gain would be zero, while the ethical and reputational risk is real.

## Questions

- How does the thesis fit with the observation that people often choose brands without remembering the ads?
- Is "low-attention processing" (Heath) a form of unconscious processing or a weak form of conscious processing?
