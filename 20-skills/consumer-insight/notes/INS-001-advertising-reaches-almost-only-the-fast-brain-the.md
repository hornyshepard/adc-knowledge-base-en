# Advertising reaches almost only the fast brain, the gatekeeper of attention: the message has to get past it first

> **The idea in one sentence:** people live on autopilot, with the fast, intuitive part of the mind; the slow, logical part costs energy and rarely switches on, so an ad has to be understood at a glance, or it gets filtered out.

## Explanation

Barry Milavsky, a psychologist by training and an agency man (Young & Rubicam), translates Daniel Kahneman's distinction (*Thinking, Fast and Slow*) into the language of advertising:

| The fast (operational) brain | The slow (logical) brain |
|---|---|
| always on, intuitive, pragmatic | passive, switches on only when needed or in a context that calls for it (a lecture hall, a hard problem) |
| uses shortcuts, stereotypes, first impressions | builds the frameworks (grammar, rules) that the fast brain then uses |
| decides: opportunity or threat? fight or flee? | costs energy, so we avoid it |
| is "leaky": it conflates information and misattributes it | stores long-term memory |

Consequences for the ad:

- **The gatekeeper.** Most advertising reaches only the fast brain. Only after the message passes through its filters can it reach the slow brain and enter the decision framework.
- **The small cards.** In the consumer's mind, the brand has, if you are lucky, an index card with its name and a few words. You can talk about the product for an hour; the consumer keeps a few words.
- **The limit of three.** We recognize at a glance three things at most (George A. Miller gives seven as the upper limit). Brand names, arguments and headlines have to respect the limit.
- **Picture before word.** The visual is processed immediately; the word has to be decoded.
- **No complex logic.** No heavy grammatical constructions, no calculations. The fast brain does not work out 17 × 23.

## Application

- Test every headline or key visual with the question: "Is it understood in two seconds, without the agency's explanation?"
- Take out of the message everything that takes effort: jargon, acronyms, double negatives, conditions.
- Repeat: repetition is the way a message gets from the fast brain into long-term memory.

## Example

Milavsky describes an experiment he took part in as a researcher: people physically excited by exercise reacted more aggressively to a later challenge. Their fast brain mislabeled the excitement as anger (excitation transfer, Zillmann, Katcher and Milavsky, 1972).

## Questions

- If the ad speaks only to the fast brain, how do you explain the success of long, informative advertising (B2B, real estate, cars)?
- Where does "talking to the fast brain" turn into manipulation?
