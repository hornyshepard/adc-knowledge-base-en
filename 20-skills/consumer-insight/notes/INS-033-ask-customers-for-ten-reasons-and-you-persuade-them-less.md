# Ask customers for ten reasons and you persuade them less than with one (ease of retrieval)

> **The idea in one sentence:** people judge not only by what comes to mind, but also by how easily it comes, and the difficulty of finding arguments is read as proof that the arguments do not exist.

## Explanation

Meta-cognition means thoughts about one's own thoughts. Fennis and Stroebe present ease of retrieval (Schwarz) as a form of fluency: "what I can easily remember should be correct".

The studies by Wänke and colleagues:

- Participants who had to generate **seven** reasons for public transport were less favorable than those who generated **three**. Those who generated seven reasons *against* were less negative. When they were merely exposed to the arguments, the effect reversed: seven arguments persuaded more than three.
- An ad that suggested naming ten reasons to buy a BMW produced less favorable attitudes than one that suggested a single reason, and even less favorable than one that suggested ten reasons *against*.

Another form of meta-cognition is **self-validation** (Briñol & Petty): confidence in one's own thoughts amplifies their effect. Credible sources increase confidence in the thoughts the message generates. Tormala, Briñol and Petty (2006, ch. 5, pp. 221–223): when the source (a consumer organization vs. a detergent manufacturer) was revealed **after** the message, the credible source increased persuasion for strong arguments but **reduced** it for weak arguments, because it strengthened confidence in the negative thoughts. Meta-cognition can also work against the ad: when consumers infer the marketer's ulterior motives, skepticism and resistance follow.

## Application

- Avoid copy that asks the audience for generating effort ("How many reasons do you have to…?") if the answer does not come easily.
- If you ask for participation, ask for little: "Give us one reason why…" works better than "ten".
- Turn the tactic against the competition: "Try to find one reason to pay more."
- When you supply the arguments (not the audience), more good arguments can help.

## Example

The BMW test ad (Wänke et al., 1997): "ten reasons to buy" worked worse than "one reason".

## Questions

- How does the effect apply to UGC campaigns that ask users for stories?
- Where does the effect stop: are experts in a field affected just as much?
