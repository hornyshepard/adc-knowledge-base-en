# The mere presence of a smartphone reduces attentional resources and memory, so ads on the phone are processed more superficially

> **The idea in one sentence:** the phone is a "supernormal stimulus" that hijacks attention even when it is turned off on the table; fewer cognitive resources mean less elaboration of the message, weaker memory and greater susceptibility to deception.

## Explanation

Toward the end of the chapter on digital media, the authors add a topic that other textbooks ignore: the **cognitive consequences of smartphones**. They matter for advertising because the phone is now the main source of digital advertising revenue.

Cognitive capability depends on limited-capacity attentional resources associated with **working memory** and **fluid intelligence** (the ability to select, store and manipulate information in a goal-directed manner). The psychologist Adrian Ward calls the smartphone a **supernormal stimulus** that can hijack attention whenever it is part of our surroundings, which it almost always is. The evidence cited:

- With the phone in view, even turned off, people make more errors on tests of attention than those whose phones are out of sight. The effect occurs without the person being aware of it.
- Students who did not bring their phones to class scored a full letter grade higher on tests, whether or not the others used their phones.
- In British schools that banned phones, exam scores went up, and weaker students benefited the most.
- A beep or a buzz during a difficult task makes attention wander, even if you do not check the phone. A phone that rings and that you cannot answer raises blood pressure and pulse and lowers problem-solving ability.
- **Memory**: if you take a photo of something, you remember less of what you saw. People forget information they think they will find online anyway (the **Google effect**). "Transactive" memory, relying on what others know, can erase one's own memories.

The implication for advertising: fewer attentional resources mean **less elaboration** of the ad and **greater susceptibility to deception**. In addition, information communicated digitally is less likely to find its way into memory, a serious problem for building awareness and attitude.

## Application

- On mobile, assume fragmented attention: a single benefit, the brand early, the picture carrying the message.
- Do not rely on mobile for high-involvement informational strategies, which need time and acceptance. Move the argument to media that allow processing time.
- Think ethically: if the audience processes superficially, ambiguous messages become deceptive more easily.

## Example

A bank explains its new mortgage through a 6-second video on the phone, with the interest rate in small print. Many people will retain only "low interest", not the conditions. The message is effective in the short term and ethically risky.

## Questions

- If the effect is real, what does it mean for the theory of minimum effective frequency on mobile?
- What research should be done to check the effect in advertising, not only in school tests?
