# The celebrity in an ad is a peripheral cue at low involvement and becomes an argument only when relevant to the product

> **The idea in one sentence:** the source's beauty or fame helps any product when people are not thinking, but with an attentive audience it helps only the products for which that quality is evidence.

## Explanation

Fennis and Stroebe distinguish **direct sources** (the spokesperson who delivers the message) from **indirect sources** (Cristiano Ronaldo associated with Tag Heuer, without speaking about the watch). Two classic dimensions:

- **Credibility** = expertise + trustworthiness. It matters mainly when the audience is not motivated to process the message. Trustworthiness increases when the source seems to have no vested interest: hence the ads with hidden cameras or with a conversation the camera seems to have accidentally overheard. The viewer is a bystander, not a target, and lets their guard down (Walster & Festinger, 1962).
- **Attractiveness** works as a halo effect: "what is beautiful is good" (Eagly et al., 1991).

The key result is **source congruity** (Kirmani & Shiv). Petty and Cacioppo observed that a model's attractiveness also persuaded the involved audience in a shampoo ad, because her attractive hair was visual evidence. Kang and Herr (2006): an attractive couple improved attitudes toward both a razor and a computer processor when the audience was distracted, but **only** toward the razor when the audience was attentive. Clint Eastwood, associated with ruggedness, would be a congruent endorser for mountain boots, not for Gucci loafers.

On the **sleeper effect** (the message of a low-credibility source gains influence over time, as the source is forgotten), the authors warn that its conditions are rarely realized in advertising: the arguments must first be accepted, and the information about the source must come after the message, not before.

**Convergence.** Milavsky ([[INS-035-source-credibility-is-judged-on-three-criteria-in-order|INS-035]]) and Percy's VisCAP model ([[INS-037-the-viscap-model-chooses-the-presenter-by-the-communication|INS-037]]) break credibility down the same way, into expertise plus trustworthiness or objectivity, and set the same condition: the source has to be relevant to what it proves.

## Application

- Choose the endorser for what they prove, not only for how well known they are: the athlete for sports equipment, the chef for knives.
- For low-involvement products, popularity and likability may be enough; for high-involvement ones, check relevance.
- Use bystander formats (apparently spontaneous testimonials), but without deceiving the audience.

## Example

Lindsey Vonn holding up her Head skis after a win (p. 198): a cue at a quick glance, an argument for whoever thinks about it (if she wins races on them, they must be excellent skis).

## Questions

- Which Romanian influencer would be a congruent source for a bank? And for an energy drink?
- When does irrelevant attractiveness become a distraction that costs the brand?
