# Choice architecture influences the decision without forbidding any option: through nudges and defaults

> **The idea in one sentence:** the way the choice environment is designed (the shelf, the screen, the form) changes behavior predictably, even when all options remain available and economic incentives do not change.

## Explanation

Grewal and Levy define **choice architecture** as an effort to influence consumers through the design of the environments in which they make their choices. It has two tools:

- The **nudge**: an element that alters behavior in a predictable way, without forbidding other options or significantly changing economic incentives. Impulse products (fragrances, magazines, in-game upgrades) are placed in heavily trafficked areas: about 10 feet beyond the entrance on the right side of the store, near escalators and cash wraps. Customers can walk past them, so the choice remains theirs.
- The **default**: it deals with the situation in which a person does not decide, by imposing a choice if they take no action. In the United States, personal information is generally viewed as public, and consumers must explicitly opt out. In the EU, consumers own their personal information, so firms must get them to opt in explicitly. The American convention nudges people into receiving messages.

The authors raise the ethical problem explicitly: game designers place in-app purchases at critical stages of the game, and children spend their parents' money (Dilemma 6.3, Fortnite, an estimated $3.9 billion a year from in-game purchases). The colorful, toy-like game currency makes real money look like pretend money.

## Application

- In activations, retail and website design, think about the architecture: where the product sits, what is preselected, which button comes first.
- For social campaigns (recycling, health), a well-placed nudge can do more than an ad. The CVS example: candy moved to the back of the store.
- The ethical test: could you explain publicly, without embarrassment, why you set the default where it is?

## Example

CVS (Dilemma 6.1) removed tobacco from its stores (a loss of $2 billion in annual sales) and tested moving candy to the back, to support its health positioning. This is choice architecture put at the service of a brand.

## Questions

- Where is the line between a legitimate nudge and a manipulative *dark pattern*?
- If the European opt-in reduces audiences, is it a cost or a trust advantage for brands?
