# An exaggerated promise sells once and disappoints afterwards (the expectancy disconfirmation model)

> **The idea in one sentence:** satisfaction does not depend on how good the product is, but on the gap between the performance experienced and the expectation created, by advertising among other things.

## Explanation

Fennis and Stroebe link novelty and surprise to Oliver's **expectancy disconfirmation model**. Before buying, consumers form expectations about product performance, in large part shaped by advertising. After buying, they compare the actual performance with the expectation:

- performance > expectation → a positive surprise, satisfaction;
- performance < expectation → disappointment, even if the product is objectively good.

Advertising tends to use **puffery** and exaggerate, so expectations are often higher than the "real deal". The paradoxical result: surprise does occur in the marketplace, but it is often negative. The authors call this a real conflict for advertisers: if they promise too little, nobody buys; if they promise too much, customers are disappointed.

The authors also note that novelty can be maintained through changes in execution, not only in the product: the theme of the repetition-variation hypothesis, discussed in the chapter on persuasion.

**Convergence.** Grewal and Levy ([[INS-043-the-purchase-decision-has-five-stages-and-does-not-end-at|INS-043]]) describe the same dilemma in the post-purchase stage and recommend realistic expectations, demonstrations of use and guarantees.

## Application

- When writing the central promise, ask: "Can the product deliver this every time it is used?" If not, move the promise towards a benefit it delivers for sure.
- For repeat-purchase products, satisfaction (and so the second purchase) matters more than the first sale.
- Leave room for a positive surprise: an unpromised detail discovered in use.

## Example

A cream that promises "wrinkles erased in 7 days" can generate trial, but the customer who sees only good hydration feels cheated and does not buy again; the same cream promoted for intense hydration would have produced satisfaction.

## Questions

- Are there categories where exaggeration is accepted as a game (perfumes, drinks) without affecting satisfaction?
- How does this model relate to the distortion of the memory of the experience by advertising?
