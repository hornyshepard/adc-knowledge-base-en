# Assimilation and contrast decide whether a new product lifts or lowers the parent brand

> **The idea in one sentence:** a product seen as a member of the category is "pulled" toward it (assimilation), while one seen as an exception becomes a standard of comparison and pushes the rest in the opposite direction (contrast).

## Explanation

Fennis and Stroebe treat brand extensions as a categorization problem: the brand is a category, and consumers use what they know about it to judge the new member. The fit between brand and extension helps acceptance, working as the representativeness heuristic. Still, **moderate incongruity** can be even better, because resolving it gives pleasure (the Pringles example: a different package, the same essence of potato chips).

The underlying mechanism:

- **Assimilation**: information included in the category colors it. Someone who has just thought about Nixon rates "American politicians" lower.
- **Contrast**: an exemplar seen as separate becomes a standard. Next to Nixon, a particular politician can look better.

The study by Wänke, Bless and Igou (2001): an inferior toaster (the Logan L 500) was downgraded next to the superior Logan T 5000, but not when the ads emphasized the shared brand identity (similar colors, slogans and logo). Bless and Wänke (2000) show the reverse risk: a member that is too atypical becomes a standard and lowers the rest.

The case of the VW Phaeton: a luxury car praised by the press and a commercial flop, because it was a Volkswagen and looked like one. The upward extension was assimilated to the "VW" category.

The same principle appears in priming (Herr, 1989): ambiguous cars were rated in the direction of the activated category (assimilation), clearly identifiable ones in the opposite direction (contrast). A Mini looks cheap among BMWs and expensive next to a VW Golf or Polo.

## Application

- For an upward extension, decide explicitly: do you want assimilation (the same family) or separation (a distinct sub-brand)?
- To protect the basic models from the "star" of the range, emphasize the shared identity in communication.
- Use the presentation context (shelf, showroom, page) as a tool: what sits next to the product changes how it is perceived.

## Example

The VW Phaeton (ch. 2, p. 75): top quality, but the brand pulled perception down.
