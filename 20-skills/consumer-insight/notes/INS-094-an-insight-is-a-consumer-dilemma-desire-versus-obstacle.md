# An insight is a consumer dilemma (desire versus obstacle) that the brand can resolve

> **The idea in one sentence:** finding an insight means discovering a deep motivation, not yet satisfied or not yet clearly expressed, usually in the form of an "I would like… but…" tension, and seeing how the brand can resolve it.

## Explanation

*La boîte à outils de la publicité* gives two useful meanings of the word:

1. **The insight tied to a hidden motivation or need**: a consumer dilemma (*dilemme consommateur*). The book's example: "j'aimerais consommer des légumes biologiques mais manger bio coûte cher" [I would like to eat organic vegetables, but eating organic is expensive]. It is explored through qualitative studies and brainstorming.
2. **The insight as a forerunner sign** (*signe avant-coureur*): a weak signal (*signal faible*) that, once spotted and brought to light, announces an underlying trend. Whoever puts it into words first gets ahead of the competitors.

The five-step process the authors propose:

| Step | What you do |
|---|---|
| Observation | studies, monitoring, analysis of raw data |
| Finding (*constat*) | you identify a consumer dilemma or a weak signal |
| Analysis | you decode the dilemma: wish versus tension |
| Interpretation | how the dilemma can be resolved, how the insight can be used |
| Use in communication | how you use the finding in communication, as a weighty argument |

The raw material is **motivations and brakes** (*freins et motivations*; Joannis, Haas), **needs** (Maslow's pyramid: physiological, safety, belonging, esteem, self-actualization), **expectations** (the evaluation criteria before purchase and the satisfaction criteria after it) and **perceptions**. The authors also stress the role of emotions: enthusiasm, joy or tenderness are immediate and push people to act.

**Convergence.** McTigue and Rucker reach the same structure in the Stark case ([[BRF-015-the-stark-cloud-solutions-case-how-a-good-brief-becomes-a|BRF-015]]): maintaining their own servers is expensive, *but* the cloud seems less secure; the insight shows the obstacle to overcome. Fennis and Stroebe treat the second half as the consumer's reluctance, and the strategies that reduce it as omega strategies ([[INS-039-you-can-persuade-by-increasing-the-attractiveness-of-the|INS-039]]).

## Application

- Write the insight in the first person, in the consumer's words, with the structure "I want… but…". If there is no "but", you have an observation, not an insight.
- Check whether the product can actually resolve the tension; otherwise the insight is attractive but unusable.
- Test the insight with three or four people from the target: if the reaction is "that's exactly it", you are on the right track.

## Example

"I would like to eat organic, but it is expensive" opens several brand answers: an organic range at a discounter's price, a promise such as "organic without waste", or communication about the real cost of cheap food.

## Questions

- Not every insight is a dilemma. What other forms can it take (cultural truth, ritual, contradiction)?
- Who has the right to call a finding an "insight": the planner, the creative or the consumer who recognizes themselves in it?
