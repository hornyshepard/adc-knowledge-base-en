# The postmodern consumer is fragmented and paradoxical and buys cultural meanings, not products

> **The idea in one sentence:** in postmodern consumer culture, the image and meaning of a product matter more than the product, and people build multiple identities by playing with signs, styles and eras, which marketing based on the rational consumer cannot read.

## Explanation

**Modern vs. postmodern** (Firat & Venkatesh, as presented by Batat). Modern society puts the product first, separates production from consumption, believes in progress and rationality and sees the consumer as a "destroyer" of value. Postmodernism answers that reality is socially constructed (aesthetics, language, narratives), that symbols are part of consumption and that consumers create value too.

**The characteristics**, from two lists that largely overlap (Stephen Brown, 1995, in Parsons, Maclaran and Chatzidakis; Batat):

| Characteristic | What it means | Examples from the two books |
|---|---|---|
| Fragmentation | markets and identities broken into pieces | mass customization; the "multiphrenic selves" who buy a luxury watch, rent one, buy a second-hand one and a counterfeit one |
| De-differentiation / juxtaposition | the boundaries between high and low culture blur | advertising displayed at the Tate, brand museums (Guinness) |
| Hyperreality | the simulation seems "more real" than the original | themed Irish pubs, Las Vegas, Disney World |
| Chronology / anachronism | retro and nostalgia, even for eras never lived | the new MINI, the VW Beetle, "vicarious nostalgia" |
| Pastiche / intertextuality | an ironic mix of styles and quotations | the Energizer Bunny as a parody of Duracell; Tarantino |
| Anti-foundationalism | the "rebel sell" | FCUK, "You know when you've been Tango'd" |
| Pluralism / tolerance | "anything goes", openness to diversity | a "vernacular religion" built from fragments |

Consequences for the consumer: paradoxical behavior (loving and hating the same brand), buying the **cultural meanings** attached to brands, seeking **linking value** (Cova), products and events that connect them with others (Innocent's "Fruitstock", the VW Autostadt), and moving from **solid** consumption (ownership) to **liquid** consumption (access, ephemeral; Bardhi & Eckhardt).

**Where the authors converge and where they differ.** Both books derive from this Holbrook and Hirschman's experiential consumption ([[INS-048-the-experiential-view-consumption-also-means-fantasies|INS-048]]). Batat treats postmodernism as a natural transition towards experiential marketing. Parsons and colleagues are more critical: identity play is a luxury of those with money, it does not apply to subsistence economies, and rebellion in the marketplace has weakened collective critique; some already speak of **post-postmodernism**, a "new sincerity" in place of irony.

## Application

- Use the table as a grid for analyzing the category: which codes does the competition exploit, and what space is left free?
- Do not ask segments for consistent behavior; design for the "several selves" of the same person.
- Pastiche needs an audience that recognizes the reference; check whether the target has it. Irony can wear out: sincerity can be the new differentiation.

## Example

"Homemade" food has different meanings across generations (Batat): closeness of family for the young, "made from scratch" for middle-aged consumers, tradition for seniors. Powerscourt Townhouse in Dublin (Parsons et al.) puts a piano, antiques and cheap jewelry under the same Georgian roof.

## Questions

- Is "postmodernism" a useful description or a label that explains everything and nothing?
- Which Romanian brand uses nostalgia successfully? Does irony still work with Generation Z?
