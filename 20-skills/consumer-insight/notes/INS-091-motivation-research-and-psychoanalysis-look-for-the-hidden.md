# Motivation research and psychoanalysis look for the hidden "why" of buying: good for insight hypotheses, unproven as science

> **The idea in one sentence:** from the 1950s on, a school inspired by Freud moved advertising from the product to people's hidden desires; it left useful methods (the depth interview, projective techniques) and fertile concepts, but also myths, and its ideas do not pass empirical tests.

## Explanation

**What it is.** Motivation research answers the question "why?": why people behave as they do toward a product. Louis Cheskin defined it by techniques designed to reach the unconscious, because preference is generally determined by factors of which the individual is not conscious. The central figures: Ernest Dichter (the "Freud of Madison Avenue", over 2,500 studies), Pierre Martineau, Gardner and Levy, Herta Herzog (Rorschach tests to verify "brand personalities"). Before them, Edward Bernays, Freud's nephew, had repositioned cigarettes as "torches of freedom" for women (1929).

**What it brought.** Dichter looked for three layers of motives: those consumers are consciously aware of, those they are aware of but unwilling to reveal, and those they are unaware of. Men said they chose a car for reliability and price, but in his reading the convertible was the "mistress" and the sedan the "wife". His formula: the advertiser's job is "to give moral permission to have fun without guilt". Martineau (*Motivation in Advertising*, 1957) attacks the logic of "salesmanship in print": you do not persuade anybody by winning an argument with the consumer through logic, and competitors can match ingredients and prices but not the nonrational psychological overtones of the product image.

**The critique.** Packard (*The Hidden Persuaders*, 1957) popularized the idea of hidden manipulation; Vicary's subliminal experiment proved to be a fraud; from the 1970s on, psychoanalysis was seen as reductivist, empirically unsubstantiated and sexist.

**Where the authors disagree.** Fernández Gómez (SRC-018) stresses the commercial side: the industry funded the school because it sold, and Reeves mocked the "hidden persuaders". Parsons, Maclaran and Chatzidakis (SRC-013) rehabilitate it as a vocabulary of sensitizing concepts, useful precisely where it is counterintuitive: Judith Williamson (drawing on Lacan) sees in the ad an "Ego-Ideal", Klein explains ambivalence (objects loved and hated at once), and unconscious guilt sheds light on the gap between declared ethics and purchase. For Fennis and Stroebe, without an experiment there is no proof ([[INS-081-the-effect-of-an-ad-is-proved-by-experiment-not-by-the|INS-081]]).

## Application

- Do not stop at the declared motives ("it is cheaper", "it is practical"): ask what the choice says about the person who makes it and what guilt or tension the product can "release".
- Use the depth interview and projective techniques ([[INS-089-projective-techniques-bring-to-the-surface-what-consumers|INS-089]]) to generate insight **hypotheses**; then check them in behavior and quantitatively.
- When someone promises "guaranteed unconscious manipulation", remember Vicary.

## Example

The convertible as "mistress" (Dichter): an interpretation impossible to verify, but one that opened a memorable direction for communication.

## Questions

- What has remained valid from motivation research in today's neuromarketing and behavioral economics?
- When does a psychoanalytic interpretation become a convincing but false story sold to the client?
