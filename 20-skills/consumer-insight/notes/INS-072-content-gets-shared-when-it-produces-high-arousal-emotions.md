# Content gets shared when it produces high-arousal emotions (awe, anger, anxiety), not only positive ones

> **The idea in one sentence:** for sharing, the level of physiological arousal of an emotion matters more than its valence; sadness, although strong, reduces sharing.

## Explanation

Fennis and Stroebe discuss interpersonal communication online as the third source of persuasion, after advertising and decision tools. The main findings:

- **Berger and Milkman (2012)**, all *New York Times* articles over a three-month period: positive content was on average more viral than negative content, but the decisive factor was **arousal**. High-arousal emotions, positive (awe, excitement) or negative (anger, anxiety), increased virality; deactivating emotions (sadness) decreased it. Sharing is an act, and acts start from emotions that mobilize.
- **The Kony case (2012):** a video about child soldiers was viewed more than 100 million times in less than six days; the indignation and outrage (an activating emotion) mattered more than valence.
- **Tirunillai and Tellis (2012)**, almost 350,000 reviews, 15 companies, four and a half years: the **volume** of chatter had the largest positive effect on the company's stock performance; positive reviews were inconsequential, **negative** ones had a negative effect that came quickly and went slowly. An increase in offline advertising dampened the volume of negative chatter.
- **Emotional contagion** (Kramer, Guillory & Hancock, 2014, almost 690,000 Facebook users): reducing negative posts in the news feed made people post more positively, and reducing positive ones made them post more negatively. Small effects, but shown online, and an ethically controversial study.
- Facebook profiles are generally fairly faithful to the real person; online friendships follow offline ones.

The authors temper the enthusiasm: it is hard to generate a high-arousal emotion, and non-commercial content (news, entertainment) does it far more easily than advertising, which is why true viral campaigns remain rare.

## Application

- When you write for sharing, ask: which activating emotion does this produce? Awe, amusement, useful indignation; not melancholy.
- Monitor negative conversations and respond quickly: they hit fast and persist.
- Do not promise the client "viral"; promise content designed for arousal and sharing.

## Example

The *New York Times* study (Berger & Milkman, 2012): articles that evoked awe were passed on more often than sad ones.

## Questions

- Is it ethical to use anger as the engine of sharing for a brand?
- Which recent Romanian campaign evoked awe and was shared organically?
