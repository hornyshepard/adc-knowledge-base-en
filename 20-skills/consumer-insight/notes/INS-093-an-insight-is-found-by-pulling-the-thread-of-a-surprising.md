# An insight is found by pulling the thread of a surprising "what" (a friction, a contradiction, an odd gesture) until you reach the "why"

> **The idea in one sentence:** people cannot explain their own motives, but they show their frustrations; the insight comes from observing a surprising behavior in its context and from asking "why?" again and again, past the literal answer.

## Explanation

**"What" and "why".** McTigue and Rucker start from the fact that surveys give rationalized answers: asked why they bought the Tesla, people talk about the environment and safety, not about the fast car they have wanted since they were five. The "what" is an observation, a fact; the "why" is the motive. Their case: a fully cooked breakfast sausage, ready in 60 seconds in the microwave, with benefits that scored well in surveys and lagging sales. At 6:45 in the morning, in a kitchen, Mom heats the sausages in the microwave, then puts them in a frying pan: "warming" is a functional task, "cooking" is an expression of love. The insight changed both the communication and the innovation. Jim Stengel: to understand how a lion hunts, go to the jungle, not the zoo.

**Friction.** Bridgette Braig (Kellogg) reaches the same method through friction points. Watching women clean the bathroom, a company found two: fumes so intense that they sent their kids to the other side of the house, and the toilet brush that disgusted them even with gloves on. KABOOM made a foaming cleaner with no brush, Clorox a toilet wand with disposable one-use scrubbers. The insight does not contain the solution, but it gives a clear problem. Then "why does it matter?", asked again and again: for a cheese with crystals, "The crystals set them apart from other cheeses" is only a start. Texture, surprise, energy for a break? Each answer opens another positioning.

**Other routes** (Braun and Landa): facts plus observations, and disconnects in the data (Gareth Price); pain points (Lysol "Teddy Repair": the toys kids love the most are actually the germiest); misconceptions (Tide #TurnToCold); the discovery of unexpected users.

**Convergence and tension.** All three sources put observation before the direct question, as Struhl does ([[INS-078-asked-directly-what-matters-people-say-everything|INS-078]]). They differ on the focus group: Braig recalls that the iPhone started from a session in which the Apple team criticized their own phones, while McTigue shows how a focus group produced a false insight about transparent packaging for meat.

## Application

- Go where the product is used; note the hesitations, the improvisations and the moments of disgust.
- For every surprising "what", ask "why?" at least three times, until you reach an emotional or social motive.
- Combine the sources (quantitative, observation, social listening, secondary data), then validate quantitatively how widespread the insight is.

## Example

Canadian Club had been in decline for 16–17 years. The creatives went out to bars and learned that young drinkers avoided the brand because it was a "dad drink". The barrier became the solution: "Damn Right Your Dad Drank It". The decline reversed in four months, with no changes in pricing or distribution.

## Questions

- How does an insight differ from a mere observation? What can big data replace in the search for the "why", and what can it not?
- How representative is a single ethnographic visit?
