# Repetition improves attitude up to a peak, then wears it out: substantive variation delays wearout better than cosmetic variation

> **The idea in one sentence:** recall rises linearly with repetition, but attitude follows an inverted U-shaped curve; when tedium sets in, the audience starts to counterargue.

## Explanation

Fennis and Stroebe present the two-stage model of Cacioppo and Petty (1979): with the first repetitions, the audience has the opportunity to elaborate the (strong) arguments and generates favorable thoughts; past a certain point, tedium sets in, and boredom motivates people to attack the argumentation. In their study, the peak came at three exposures. With weak arguments, repetition lowered attitude immediately.

Important refinements:

- **The Schmidt and Eisend meta-analysis (2015, 19 studies):** the peak is closer to **ten** repetitions, and earlier for high-involvement products. Recall increases linearly and does not explain the attitude curve.
- **Nordhielm (2002):** with shallow processing (the audience only "scans" the layout), there is no downturn: the effect is one of perceptual fluency, as with mere exposure.
- **Variation (Schumann et al., 1991):** **cosmetic** variation (layout, font, color, the same arguments) helps slightly and only under low involvement; **substantive** variation (different arguments) helps under high involvement.
- **Haugtvedt et al. (1994):** after an attack with negative arguments, the attitudes of those exposed to substantive variation stayed the most positive. Variation prevents tedium and also increases resistance to counterarguments.

## Application

- Plan a **pool of executions** with different arguments for long campaigns, not a single repeated spot.
- For low-involvement products seen in passing (OOH, banners), repeating the same execution can stay effective for a long time.
- Monitor wearout signals (falling liking, irritated comments), especially among involved audiences, where the peak comes earlier.

## Example

The "Omega 3" pen (Schumann et al.): eight exposures to the same ad lowered liking for the product; versions with different arguments maintained it among the involved audience.

## Questions

- What does "substantive variation" mean for a campaign platform built on a single idea?
- How do these data translate into the optimal frequency on social media?
