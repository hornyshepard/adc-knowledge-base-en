# Changing the campaign too often destroys penetration: the principle of perseverance

> **The idea in one sentence:** a good campaign does not wear out as long as the product does not become outmoded; the ones who get bored with it are the client and the agency, not the public.

## Explanation

Fernández Gómez considers perseverance the trait that makes the USP unique and one of its major achievements. Reeves called **penetration** the proportion of people who remember the message. His argument: manufacturers change the campaign because the story is "worn out", but if 90% do not remember it, the story is not worn out; and if 90% are not even aware of it, they can hardly be bored with it.

Three principles of Reeves's:

1. As far as penetration is concerned, changing a story has the same effect as stopping the money.
2. If you run a brilliant campaign every year but change it every year, a competitor can pass you with a less brilliant campaign that he does not change.
3. Unless a product becomes outmoded, a great campaign will not wear itself out.

Ted Bates said the same: a good idea is good for an indefinite length of time; at most you might put a new dress on it. Reeves's anecdote: a client asks what his seven hundred people do if they have been running the same ad for him for eleven years. The answer: "They're keeping your advertising department from changing your ad."

The idea runs across paradigms: Ogilvy recommended repeating a good ad until it stops pulling, and Trout and Ries make permanence a principle of positioning. Rom and Sabaté add a practical nuance: the USP can stay for years, but the way it is communicated has to change.

Barry Milavsky reaches the same conclusion from agency practice: campaigns wear out faster within marketing groups and agencies than with consumers. The agency always wants new ideas, and a new manager wants to leave a mark. The audience needs at least three exposures to understand a new idea. Real wear-out is managed through pools of 2–4 commercials from the same campaign, rotated like tires; as a rough threshold, Milavsky targeted an average of about 12 exposures in the heaviest viewing quartile.

Laboratory studies qualify the principle: attitude wears out after a certain number of exposures, and substantive variation in the execution delays wear-out ([[ADV-060-repetition-improves-attitude-up-to-a-peak-then-wears-it-out|ADV-060]]). The idea stays; the executions rotate.

## Application

- Separate what stays fixed (the proposition, the brand idea) from what is renewed (executions, formats, channels).
- Before proposing a new campaign, ask for data on awareness and on correct attribution of the current message. Without them, "fatigue" is only an internal impression.
- When you take over a brand, ask what is worth keeping from what your predecessor built.

## Example

Bates ran the same ad for the same client for eleven years; the agency boasted that in seventeen years it had never lost an account and that its clients' appropriations had grown steadily.

## Questions

- On social platforms, where an execution wears out in a few days, does the principle still hold?
- Holt (iconic brands) calls for reinventing the myth at every cultural disruption. How does that fit with perseverance?
