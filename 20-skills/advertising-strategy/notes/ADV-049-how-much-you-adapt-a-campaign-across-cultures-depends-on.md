# How much you adapt a campaign across cultures depends on the category's involvement and emotion and on cultural distance, along a gradient from global to local

> **The idea in one sentence:** standardization works for categories with low involvement and emotion in similar cultures; the more emotion and cultural distance grow, the more the strategy must be creolized or made local.

## Explanation

Chapter 3 starts from the dispute between Levitt (1983, global convergence) and de Mooij (divergence). The authors' conclusion: ownership of certain goods converges (televisions, cars, computers), but the way they are used differs, and as wealth grows, tastes **diverge**. Coca-Cola's CEO admitted that the company kept standardizing its practices just when local sensitivity had become essential to success.

The practical tool is a **gradient of adaptation** (Fig. 3.5). It combines the axes of the Rossiter-Percy grid (the category's involvement and its motivation or emotional arousal) with the **degree of cultural differentiation** between the source culture and the target culture:

1. low involvement and emotion, little cultural difference → **global campaign**;
2. higher up the gradient → a global strategy with **local adaptations**;
3. higher still → a **creolized** version: local cultural interpretations are built in and emotion is used to the full. Creolization goes further than "glocal", which adapts only some elements of the marketing mix.
4. the head of the gradient → **local brand and strategy**.

The message guidelines (Zandpour and colleagues, Fig. 3.6) give the direction:

| Dominant cultural factor | More likely | Less likely |
|---|---|---|
| Individualism | information | psychological appeals, symbolism |
| Uncertainty avoidance | argument, imitation | drama, symbolism |
| Power distance | psychological appeals | argument, symbolism |
| Polychronism | information, symbolism | argument, imitation |

Other findings: high-context cultures (Japan, Korea, China) use more symbolic images and celebrities, low-context ones (the US, the UK, Germany) more literal images. Comparative advertising persuades better in low-context cultures. Loss-framed messages work better where uncertainty avoidance is high. In the West, luxury goods are bought by the self for the self, while in Confucian Asia they are often bought as gifts, to place the person in the hierarchy (Wong & Ahuvia).

## Application

- Place the client's category on the gradient before discussing "creative adaptation". Detergent and perfume do not sit in the same place.
- Use the cultural dimensions (Hofstede, high or low context, Trompenaars) as hypotheses to test, not as stereotypes.
- For multinational campaigns with large cultural differences, rely on universal biological schemas.

## Example

For decades Carlsberg used "Probably the best beer in the world" in its export markets. The slogan was invented by a UK agency for the British market, while in Denmark the beer was advertised as a blue-collar beer for the people. The modest irony of "probably" did not work in Russia and Asia.

## Questions

- Where on the gradient would you place a dating app? And a beer brand?
- Are global teenagers a single cultural segment? The book cites studies that say they are not.
