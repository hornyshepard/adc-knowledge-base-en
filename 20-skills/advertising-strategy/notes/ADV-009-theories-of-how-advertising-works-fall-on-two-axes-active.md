# Theories of how advertising works fall on two axes, active or passive audience and individual or cultural explanation

> **The idea in one sentence:** the cognitive model used in the book is the most useful for management, but it is only one of four sectors; audiences actively construct meanings, and the social context of viewing matters.

## Explanation

In chapter 2, Rosenbaum-Elliott and Percy organize dozens of perspectives on advertising on a map with two axes:

| | Individual level | Social or cultural level |
|---|---|---|
| **Passive audience** | **Sector 1:** learning theories (Pavlov, Skinner), information processing (McGuire, the book's approach), psychodynamic theories (Dichter, Packard), psycholinguistics, emotions | **Sector 2:** critical theory (the Frankfurt School, Marcuse, Gramsci), advertising as ideology (Galbraith, Goldman), structuralism and semiotics (Williamson, Barthes), postmodernism, anthropology (de Mooij, Hofstede) |
| **Active audience** | **Sector 3:** information economics (rejected by the authors), uses and gratifications (O'Donohoe), narrative transportation, skepticism (the persuasion knowledge model) | **Sector 4:** sociolinguistics, feminism, social semiotics and visual persuasion (Messaris), post-structuralism, polysemy and interpretive communities (Ritson & Elliott), meaning-based models |

A few clear positions:

- **Information economics** says advertising only signals commitment to the market through the money spent, so whoever spends more wins. The authors reject it: there is effective and ineffective advertising, and the manager's job is to tell them apart.
- **Emotion frames rational thinking**: emotional memories enter working memory before the cognitive components.
- **Narrative transportation**: people absorbed in a story accept beliefs consistent with it more easily, with fewer counterarguments. Transportation is stronger when the story shows product usage rather than an end benefit.
- **Polysemy** threatens campaigns, but it is limited by the text (more open or more closed) and by **interpretive communities**, groups that read an ad the same way.

The authors' conclusion: the cognitive approach is "pragmatically the most useful" for strategy, but it ignores the way people interact with advertising in their day-to-day lives.

## Application

- Use the map as a checklist at pretest: what would a semiotician, a feminist critic or a fan community say about the execution?
- On social media, treat the audience as active (sectors 3–4): people reinterpret, parody and redistribute.
- For story-based campaigns, show the product *in use*, to increase narrative transportation.

## Example

Monki (H&M group) launched #Monkifesto, a feminist manifesto with ten slogans ("Periods are cool, period"). It was applauded in progressive communities and accused of *femwashing* in others, and the guide "Go Please Yourself" was pulled from some platforms. The same campaign was read in radically different ways by different interpretive communities.

## Questions

- In which sector would you place Byron Sharp's theory of "mental availability"?
- If the audience is active, can a brand still control its positioning?
