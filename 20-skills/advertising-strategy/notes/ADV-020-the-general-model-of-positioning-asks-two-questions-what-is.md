# The general model of positioning asks two questions, "what is it?" for brand awareness and "what does it offer?" for brand attitude

> **The idea in one sentence:** positioning in communication links the brand in memory to a category need (so that it comes to mind) and to a benefit (so that it is preferred), which are exactly the two universal objectives.

## Explanation

The authors start from the near-consensus definition of marketing textbooks (Jobber & Ellis-Chadwick, Hooley et al.): positioning means identifying the market where the brand will compete and establishing a **differential advantage**. For communication, they turn this into a **general model of positioning** with two questions:

| Question | The link created in memory | The communication effect |
|---|---|---|
| **What is it?** | brand ↔ category need, as the consumer defines it | brand awareness |
| **What does it offer?** | brand ↔ benefit, weighted by how important the benefit is | brand attitude |

The first question is not trivial. A Kia and a Ferrari are both automobiles, but they are unlikely to satisfy the same category need. The answer must be given in the consumer's terms, not the industry's. Coffee, for example, may compete on the need for stimulation or for relaxation, not on the need for refreshment.

The model is the first of the four steps to effective brand positioning (Table 8.8):

1. address the general model of positioning: "what is it?" and "what does it offer?";
2. make the initial positioning decisions: central or differentiated positioning, user or product orientation;
3. select the benefits: important to the target audience, deliverable by the brand, delivered better than by other brands;
4. use the correct benefit focus, according to the motivation.

Positioning is revisited with every communication plan, because the market, the competitors and what consumers look for keep changing.

## Application

- Write the answer to "what is it?" in the words consumers would use spontaneously if you asked them "what's this?".
- Check that the creative execution clearly answers both questions. Many "beautiful" ads say neither what the product is nor what it offers.
- Link the result to the positioning statement in the brief.

## Example

The Linda McCartney's ad answers both questions at once. "What is it?": a range of vegetarian food (the link to the category need, for brand awareness). "What does it offer?": deliciously vegetarian food, a benefit reinforced by the visual.

## Questions

- What happens to brand awareness if the brand is linked to the wrong category need?
- How does an umbrella brand (Samsung, Virgin) answer "what is it?"?
