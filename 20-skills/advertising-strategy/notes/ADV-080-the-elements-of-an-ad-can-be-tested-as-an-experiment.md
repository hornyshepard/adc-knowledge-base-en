# The elements of an ad can be tested as an experiment: message conjoint beats the A/B test

> **The idea in one sentence:** instead of comparing two whole versions, you break the ad down into elements (headline, image, color, structure) and test thousands of possible combinations with 20–24 planned profiles.

## Explanation

Struhl argues that, although conjoint is not the best choice for forecasting market share, it remains excellent for **optimizing communication**: when you are picking the best combination, how utilities become marketplace behavior no longer matters. The procedure:

1. list the elements to vary and their levels;
2. put them into an experimental design (uncorrelated variations);
3. produce the profiles the design requires;
4. show them to respondents (a rating of "how likely you would be to read and consider") or, on a website, serve them at random to visitors and measure clicks and time spent;
5. estimate the effect of each element and look for the optimal combination, possibly in a simulator.

The comparison with the A/B test is harsh: a recently reported A/B test involved over 1 million visitors for **two** alternatives. A design with 20 alternatives for 1,024 combinations needs about 2,500 visitors (125 "complete respondents"). The A/B test recalls the old direct-mail tests with 700,000 pieces sent out.

## Application

- In pretesting, offer the client a grid of elements instead of "concept A vs. concept B". The idea remains the creative team's; the test optimizes the execution.
- The elements must be independent variables: the same layout with interchangeable headlines, images and CTAs.
- Clean web data of extreme values (someone leaves the page open while putting out a kitchen fire).

## Example

A direct mail insurance offer (response rate under 1%) had six varied elements, envelope and letter, that is, 3,840 combinations tested with 24 profiles. The winners: the headline "Time is flying. Are you ready?", Calibri font and green ink on the envelope, and a letter ordered **warning first, promise next, message last**. Response rose by about 25%, just past the 1% mark. In a print ad (4,374 combinations, 24 profiles), the boss's favorite, the **HIPPO** (*highest paid person's opinion*), was only about one-third as well received as the best combination.

## Questions

- What does a strong creative idea risk if it is "optimized" element by element?
- Which elements of an ad cannot be broken down into independent variables?
