# Promises have a hierarchy of strength (sensory delivery, body effect, convenience, price), and the lower ones are the easiest to copy

> **The idea in one sentence:** the promise is the most important element of the strategy; choose the one competitors will find hardest to match, which most of the time means the sensory and psychological experience, not price or convenience.

## Explanation

For consumer food products, Milavsky finds only four types of promises, from strongest to weakest:

| Type | Examples | Why it is strong or weak |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Sensory delivery** | taste, odor, look | no other brand tastes exactly like yours; the context created by advertising (the label, the expectation) does change how the taste is perceived |
| **2. Body effect** | caffeine, vitamins, no sugar, omega 3, keto | can be matched by reformulating (iodized salt became the standard) |
| **3. Convenience** | product form, delivery, the drive-through | gives a temporary window of opportunity; McDonald's and Wendy's did not rest on it, and it became a parity promise |
| **4. Price** | price reductions, a fixed price | is matched almost immediately; a price reduction works only with a plan to return to the regular price |

The hierarchy also carries over outside food: in cars, sensation appeal (the thrill of the ride, the look, the status) ranks above safety and warranties, which rank above convenience, which ranks above price. His observation: the more immediately the benefit is evident, the more persuasive it is. "The here and now is fast brain territory."

Sometimes the real benefit should not even be stated. **Pampers**, launched in a foreign market: the real purchase motivator was convenience, but mothers felt they were cheating. The advertising talked only about drier bottoms, so fewer rashes and a happier baby. **Betty Crocker** added an egg to the cake mix recipe, so that it would feel like real baking, and the water-only version was sold as a snack (Snackin' Cake). His conclusion: when technology and psychology compete, psychology wins in marketing.

The promise is written as **a single declarative sentence**, not compound (no "and" or "but"), without "advertising" words (those are the copywriter's job). It usually does not change for years.

## Application

- List the possible promises on the four levels. Start from the top: is there something sensory or psychological that only you can claim?
- If your promise is about price or convenience, ask how long until the competition copies it and what will be left after that.
- Check that the "obvious" benefit does not cause guilt or suspicion; sometimes it must be shown, not said.

## Example

As a sensory test, Milavsky points out that nothing tastes like a Coke, except in a blindfolded test: the label changes the experience. The same wine seems better in a bottle with an expensive-looking label.

## Questions

- Does the hierarchy still hold for digital services, where convenience (one click less) seems to be the central benefit?
- Which promise would a mineral water brand choose, according to this grid?
