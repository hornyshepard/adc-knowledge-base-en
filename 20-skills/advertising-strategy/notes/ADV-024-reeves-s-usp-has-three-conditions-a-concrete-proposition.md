# Reeves's USP has three conditions (a concrete proposition, unique, strong enough to move the mass millions) and is not a slogan

> **The idea in one sentence:** the Unique Selling Proposition is a promise of a specific benefit that the competition does not make, strong enough to pull over new customers; textbooks often reduce it to a slogan, which is exactly what Reeves said it is not.

## Explanation

Rosser Reeves (Ted Bates & Company) defines it in *Reality in Advertising* (1961) through three rules:

1. **Proposition.** Each advertisement must say to each reader: "Buy this product, and you will get this specific benefit." Not just words, not product puffery, not show-window advertising.
2. **Uniqueness.** The competition cannot or does not offer it: either a uniqueness of the brand or a claim not otherwise made in that field.
3. **Selling power.** Strong enough to move the mass millions, that is, to pull over new customers.

The psychological premise: the consumer tends to remember just one thing from an advertisement. Reeves compares a good campaign to a burning glass that focuses the rays of the sun into one point. In 1996, 69% of North American advertising agencies still named the USP among their basic strategic mechanisms (*Advertising Age*).

**The textbook version.** Grewal and Levy start from the value proposition: people may not be looking for merino wool apparel as such, but for apparel that lasts and has anti-odor properties. For them the USP is an abbreviated value proposition, the common theme of a campaign, with three conditions: **unique** to the brand, **meaningful** to the consumer, **sustainable** over time. Their examples, however, are slogans ("A Diamond Is Forever", "Built Ford Tough"). Fernández Gómez notes that Reeves complained about exactly this: the acronym had come to be applied to almost any slogan slightly different from the competition's. The two versions converge on uniqueness and differ in emphasis: Reeves demands selling power, the textbook demands meaning and sustainability.

## Application

- Write the USP as a full sentence: Buy X and you will get Y. If you cannot, you have a slogan, not a proposition.
- The uniqueness test: look for the same promise in competitors' ads. If a competitor could sign it without lying, it is not unique.
- The strength test: would the promise change the behavior of a non-buyer, or only flatter an existing customer?

## Example

Ollé, quoted by Fernández Gómez, describes the logic of the 1960s: if a shampoo was improved by adding conditioner, the campaign's USP was "with conditioner"; if a toothpaste gained an ingredient against tartar, the campaign said it reduces plaque and tartar build-up. Reeves boasted of his results for Anacin, M&M's and Viceroy.

## Questions

- In parity categories (water, banks, telecom), are there still real USPs or only "claimed" ones?
- Is uniqueness enough if the promise does not matter to the consumer?
