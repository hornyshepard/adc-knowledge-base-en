# Femvertising sells empowerment: it wins awards and shares, but is suspected of selling the same old ideals back to women

> **The idea in one sentence:** ads that challenge gender stereotypes can be creatively exceptional and go viral, but the feminist critique asks whether a company can sell emancipation without turning it into just another product.

## Explanation

Parsons, Maclaran and Chatzidakis start from the idea that the marketplace plays a central role in perpetuating norms of masculinity and femininity: washing powder ads with the "ideal selfless mother figure", retouched bodies, "Mansize Tissues". The chapter goes through the four waves of feminism; the fourth (from around 2008) uses the internet for "calling out" sexism (#FBrape, Everyday Sexism, #freethenipple). Brands have taken up the wave: **femvertising**, or pro-female advertising, with the annual #femvertising award (and, since 2016, the "Dadvertising" category).

**The Always #LikeAGirl case (2014, Leo Burnett, lead creative: Judy John).** Building on Iris Marion Young's 1980 essay "Throwing Like a Girl", the team asked adults and children to "run/throw/fight like a girl". The adults mimed half-hearted, ineffective moves; the young girls gave it their best shot. Final line: "Let's make #likeagirl mean amazing things". More than 50 million YouTube views in a year, airing at the 2015 Super Bowl, an Emmy, the Grand Clio, a Cannes Grand Prix, D&AD Pencils. P&G backed the campaign with education programs on puberty and confidence.

**The two positions:**

- *For*: questioning stereotypes is needed; brands have enormous reach; some (Dove, Always) add real programs.
- *Against*: it is a diluted, individualized feminism (self-confidence instead of structural change); a beauty brand that talks about "real beauty" still reinforces the message that beauty defines women (the critique of Dove Real Beauty); the success of these ads shows how little has actually changed (Laura Bates, Everyday Sexism).

The chapter also shows how ads construct motherhood: archetypes of the mother in *Good Housekeeping* from 1950 to 2010 (nurturer, expert, then focused on health and well-being) and the Danonino ads, which played on five kinds of maternal anxiety (growth, intellectual development, exclusion from peer groups, family bonding, lack of parental presence), then offered the product as the resolution.

## Application

- Before a "purpose" campaign, check whether the brand has the right to speak (product, history, real actions) and what it does beyond the ad.
- Base the idea on a documented cultural insight (here, an essay in philosophy) and on an "experiment" that makes it visible.
- Avoid creating the anxiety that you then cure (the Danonino model).

## Example

Always #LikeAGirl: a pejorative phrase turned into a source of pride.

## Questions

- Which Romanian brand could run a credible campaign about gender stereotypes? What would it have to do outside the ad?
- Where is the line between authentic purpose and "purpose-washing"?
