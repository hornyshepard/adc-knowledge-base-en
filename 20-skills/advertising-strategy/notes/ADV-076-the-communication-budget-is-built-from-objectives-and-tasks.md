# The communication budget is built from objectives and tasks (from the sales goal back to exposures and cost), and rule-of-thumb methods only give the ceiling

> **The idea in one sentence:** percentage of sales or parity with competitors say how much you can afford; how much you need follows from what you want to achieve, how many people must be reached, how many times and at what cost.

## Explanation

**Rule-of-thumb methods** (Grewal & Levy, Exhibit 18.6; Schwarz-Musch et al.):

| Method | Logic | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Percentage-of-sales | a fixed percentage of forecasted sales | assumes the past percentage is still appropriate; when sales fall, the budget falls too |
| Competitive parity (*share of voice*) | share of communication expenses = share of the market | ignores the firm's own opportunities; if all competitors use it, market shares stay frozen |
| Available budget | what is left after operating costs and profit | assumes communication does not stimulate sales |
| Extrapolating past budgets | last year, plus a correction | perpetuates mistakes |

All the authors recommend the **objective-and-task method**. Percy and Rosenbaum-Elliott describe it as the communication response sequence run backward (Table 16.6): the sales goal in units → the number of purchasers needed → how many people must process the message through to action → the effective reach required → the number of insertions, based on the reach pattern → the cost, plus production and research. They add **advertising elasticity**: the expected increase in sales from a 10% increase in advertising spending, weighed against the cost and against competitors' spending. For promotions, a 10% price cut brings on average a 16% increase in unit sales for FMCG and 21% for durable goods (almost double if the price-off is advertised), but the margin can make it unprofitable.

Schwarz-Musch et al. give the version for online campaigns: **reach × advertising pressure (frequency) × media cost (CPM)**, while the rules of thumb serve only as an upper limit. With a fixed budget, higher reach means lower frequency, and the reverse. An opportunity to see the ad (*OTS*) is not the same as an actual contact.

**Evaluation** uses each medium's metrics: GRP for traditional media (comparable only within the same medium), impressions, CTR and ROMI for digital. ROMI = (gross margin − marketing expenditure) / marketing expenditure × 100. In Grewal and Levy, one keyword brought more sales than another, but a much lower ROMI.

## Application

- Build a table: target group (or persona) × reach × frequency × CPM = required budget. Compare the total with the rule-of-thumb ceiling.
- When the client sets a fixed budget, reverse the logic: show which objectives can be reached with it and what the desired ones would cost.
- When the budget is not enough, drop a target or a stage; do not cut frequency below the threshold at which the message is learned.

## Example

Sachdev does the calculation for search: at an average CPC of $4, $100,000 on Google Ads means up to 25,000 clicks. RXBAR (Kellogg) had an awareness goal, so it was judged on awareness (from 8% to 15.3%), not on sales.

## Questions

- Why would a finance director use percentage of sales, even though all the authors criticize it?
- What do you gain and what do you lose by moving from share of voice to objective-based budgeting?
