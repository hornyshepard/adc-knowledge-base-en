# Subjective exaggeration (puffery) is legal, but any quantifiable claim must be backed up

> **The idea in one sentence:** exaggerated praise that no ordinary consumer takes literally ("the best pizza") is tolerated, but a specific, measurable claim ("50 percent more pepperoni and sausage") must be backed up with evidence.

## Explanation

Grewal and Levy describe advertising regulation in the US as a mix of formal laws and self-regulation. The main agencies: the FTC (truth in advertising, deceptive practices), the FCC (radio and TV), the FDA (labels, terms such as *light* or *organic*), plus the ATF and the USPS (Exhibit 19.5). Self-regulation covers, for example, advertising to children. New data laws (California, the GDPR) increase the number of regimes that must be respected.

The gray zone is **puffery**: the legal exaggeration of praise, stopping just short of deception. The authors' test, with Papa John's:

- "Better Ingredients. Better Pizza." is puffery, because "better" is a subjective measure;
- "50 percent more pepperoni and sausage" would be a numerical, quantitative assertion and would have to be backed up.

The FTC's position, as the authors summarize it: it does not pursue obviously exaggerated or puffing representations, "those that ordinary consumers do not take seriously". The practical rule: the less specific the claim, the less likely it is to be considered deceptive.

The Chevrolet case (Ethical & Societal Dilemma 19.4) shows the conflict zone of comparative advertising: Chevy declared itself the "most reliable" brand on the basis of an Ipsos study (confirmed by J.D. Power). Ford, Toyota and Honda pointed to Consumer Reports, which ranked Chevy low, and accused it of false advertising. The truth of a claim can depend on the source chosen. A clear FTC case of deception: FleetCor promoted a fuel card with no fees, then charged undisclosed "administrative" fees.

Policarpio (SRC-002) gives the foundation from the consumer's side: **consumer rights** (the right to safety, to be informed, to choose, to be heard, to consumer education) and consumer responsibilities (to be critically informed, to keep the receipt, to use the product properly). False advertising is named explicitly among the abuses consumers must be protected from.

## Application

- For every claim in the copy, ask: is it an obvious opinion (puffery) or a verifiable fact? If it is a fact, ask the client for the proof before production.
- Superlatives of the "the best" type are safer than numbers, but also weaker as persuasion. The balance is a strategy decision.
- In comparative advertising, cite the source and the period. Be ready for a competitor to bring a different source.

## Example

Wrigley's direct-mail piece from around 1938 (Ethical & Societal Dilemma 19.2) sent gum to two-year-olds and claimed that gum was good for children's teeth and helped with teething. Today such claims, without evidence, would not pass the bar for truth in advertising.

## Questions

- In Romania and the EU, where is the line between exaggeration and a misleading commercial practice? (to be checked in local law)
- Is puffery ethical, even if it is legal?
