# In crisis communication, denial is the worst option: acknowledge it first, measure the impact, choose the channel, tell the truth, learn

> **The idea in one sentence:** a crisis is managed through transparency: if you are the first to talk about it, you control part of the story; if you deny it, you lose it completely.

## Explanation

*La bible de la communication* defines crisis communication (*communication de crise*) as all the actions through which the company fights the effects of an event, to keep its brand image even in the most difficult situations. The seven rules it proposes:

1. **Acknowledge the crisis**, with transparency; ideally, talk about it before someone else does it for you.
2. **Investigate** the causes, so that the scenario does not happen again.
3. **Measure the impact** before reacting.
4. **Choose the right channel**: blog, press release, social networks.
5. **Share the right message**, transparently.
6. **Learn the lessons** once the crisis has passed.
7. **Avoid denial** (*déni*): it is better to take responsibility and act than to deny the obvious.

*La boîte à outils de la publicité* adds the earned media perspective: a *bad buzz* is hard to contain without a **fast, effective and prepared** response from the brand. That is why monitoring conversations and a response protocol are part of any social media setup.

The institutional part of the book also shows the self-inflicted crisis: communication that promises more than the company does. McDonald's drew mocking tweets online when its "eco-friendly" cardboard straws turned out to be non-recyclable. In the digital era, the author says, everything must be transparent, and actions must come before their display.

## Application

- Prepare in advance, for each client, a list of 3–5 plausible crisis scenarios: who speaks, on which channel and with which approvals.
- In the first hours: acknowledge the known facts, say what you do not know yet and when you will come back. Do not speculate.
- Check any "green" or "purpose" campaign with the question: do we have proof we could show tomorrow, if asked?

## Example

The McDonald's straws case, cited in the book: the image initiative itself became the source of the crisis, because the environmental promise was not backed by the product.

## Questions

- Are there situations in which silence is better than fast acknowledgment (for example, for legal reasons)?
- How does a product crisis differ from a crisis caused by an ad (misjudged humor, a stereotype)?
