# The Rossiter-Percy grid combines involvement (risk) with motivation (negative or positive) into four brand attitude strategies, each with its own creative tactics

> **The idea in one sentence:** if you know how much the target audience risks and whether it wants to get rid of a problem or give itself a pleasure, you know what the ad must do: inform or transform, convince or just arouse curiosity.

## Explanation

The grid, which Rossiter and Percy built on the psychology of learning and persuasion, is the core of communication strategy for Percy and Rosenbaum-Elliott. It starts from the type of brand awareness (recognition or recall), then divides brand attitude strategy along two axes:

- **Involvement**, meaning perceived risk, financial or psychological. With low involvement, the target audience only needs to pay attention and learn something positive; trial builds the attitude. With high involvement, it must also **accept** the message as true.
- **Motivation**. Negative motives (problem removal, problem avoidance, incomplete satisfaction) call for **informational** strategies: information that solves the problem. Positive motives (sensory gratification, social approval) call for **transformational** strategies: a state that transforms the person.

| | Informational (negative motive) | Transformational (positive motive) |
|---|---|---|
| **Low involvement** | problem-solution, extreme benefit; the ad need not be liked ([[ADV-035-the-low-involvement-informational-strategy-calls-for\|ADV-035]]) | emotional authenticity; the ad must be liked ([[ADV-036-the-low-involvement-transformational-strategy-calls-for\|ADV-036]]) |
| **High involvement** | enough information to convince, within the latitude of acceptance ([[ADV-037-the-high-involvement-informational-strategy-must-convince\|ADV-037]]) | personal identification with the brand ([[ADV-038-the-high-involvement-transformational-strategy-requires-the\|ADV-038]]) |

The quadrant determines the benefit focus, the type of presenter (the ethos, chosen with VisCAP), whether the ad must be liked and which media fit. Even the structure of a commercial differs: in informational executions, immediate interest in the problem, the brand, then a new peak with the solution; in transformational ones, interest builds throughout and peaks at the end, with the brand.

**Compared with other classifications.** General textbooks (Grewal and Levy) divide appeals into informational and emotional, by the tone of the ad. The grid divides them by the target audience's motivation: an ad for a negative motive can be very emotional (anxiety, then relief), and a transformational one can contain information. So "emotional" is not a synonym for "transformational". The authors' criticism of the FCB grid comes from the same point: its think-feel axis assumes that only "feel" decisions involve emotions.

## Application

- Place the product in a quadrant *for the specific target audience*, not for the category. The same beer is sensory gratification after a game and social approval at a business lunch.
- Write the quadrant into the brief, before any creative idea.
- When the creatives propose an idea that breaks the quadrant's rules, test it before rejecting it: the authors say the principles are starting points, not laws.

## Example

A detergent (low involvement, negative motive) can have an irritating ad that demonstrates the benefit in the extreme. A perfume (positive motive) fails with the same approach: if the ad is not liked, the brand will not be either.

## Questions

- Fennis and Stroebe object that involvement and motivation belong to the person, not to the product. How does the grid answer this if it is applied to the specific target audience?
- Where would you place a streaming subscription? And an electric car for a buyer concerned about the environment?
