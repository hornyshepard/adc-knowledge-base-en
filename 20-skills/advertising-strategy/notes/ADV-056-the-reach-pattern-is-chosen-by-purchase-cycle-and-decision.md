# The reach pattern is chosen by purchase cycle and decision time, and a new product can hardly be over-advertised

> **The idea in one sentence:** media scheduling is worked out from the viewpoint of a typical member of the target audience: when and how often they must be reached, given how they buy and how long their decision takes.

## Explanation

Rossiter and Percy describe eight **reach patterns**, which the authors adopt. Four are for established products and four for a new product's first year.

**Established products**

| Pattern | When | How |
|---|---|---|
| **Impact schedule** | FMCG | 3+ OTS concentrated in a single day within each four-week period. Data show enormous sales effects. Because consumers do their major shopping on the same day each week, you run an impact schedule for each weekday, or seven impact schedules at four-day intervals. Rotate them so that competitors cannot detect and counter them. |
| **Awareness** | long purchase cycle and long decision time (durables, industrial products) | very high reach, relatively low frequency, widely spaced cycles. The timing of the decision is unpredictable for each person, so the brand must be kept in mind, but still above the MEF. |
| **Shifting recall** | long purchase cycle, but a quick decision | you concentrate one cycle on one or two vehicles, then shift to another, non-overlapping set. You gradually scan the whole target audience to catch the people who happen to be in the market. |
| **Seasonal priming** | sales clustered in seasonal peaks | presence just before or early into the peak, plus a few short flights a month or two ahead, which move the target audience toward the effective frequency level before all the competitors come in (Strong's simulations) |

**New products** (first year): "it is virtually impossible to over-advertise a new product."

| Pattern | Description |
|---|---|
| **Blitz** | 100% reach every week, with as much frequency as you can afford, all year. It needs many executions to prevent wearout. |
| **Wedge** | starts as a blitz, then frequency tapers off while reach stays high. The most common option. Suits low involvement, services and a repeat purchase objective. |
| **Reverse wedge** | exposure builds over the introduction. Suits social approval as the purchase motive. |
| **Fad** | a mini-blitz: get in early, with broad reach and high frequency, for the short life of the fad. |

After the first year, the product moves to the appropriate pattern for established products.

## Application

- Use the BSM to find the purchase cycle and the decision time, then choose the pattern.
- For launches, plan several creative executions from the start. A blitz quickly wears out a single ad.
- For seasonal categories (ice cream, toys, gardening), set aside budget for priming before the peak.

## Example

A windshield replacement company has a very long purchase cycle (you rarely need one) but a quick decision (a crack today, a repair tomorrow). The right pattern is shifting recall: this month radio during the morning news, next month search and posters at gas stations, then another set of vehicles.

## Questions

- How does the impact schedule translate into digital, where you can concentrate impressions on a single day and a single user?
- Which pattern would a luxury brand choose at launch (reverse wedge)? Why?
