# Hierarchy-of-effects models (AIDA, Lavidge-Steiner, DAGMAR) order think-feel-do objectives, but their fixed order has no empirical support

> **The idea in one sentence:** step models (awareness → interest → desire → action) are a good list of possible objectives, but people do not climb the steps in a fixed order, and the effect of advertising often comes with a delay.

## Explanation

Hierarchy-of-effects models assume that advertising moves people through learning stages: cognitive (think), affective (feel), conative (do).

| Model | Sequence |
|---|---|
| AIDA (E. St. Elmo Lewis, c. 1898; first developed for personal selling) | attention → interest → desire → action |
| Lavidge & Steiner (1961) | awareness → knowledge → liking → preference → conviction → purchase |
| DAGMAR (Colley, 1961) | category need → brand awareness → brand knowledge/comprehension → brand attitude → brand purchase intention → purchase facilitation → purchase → satisfaction → brand loyalty |

**What the authors accept.** Grewal and Levy present AIDA as the most widely used model and give it metrics: aided recall, and top-of-mind awareness (the brand mentioned first), which usually brings the brand into the evoked set. They also note two nuances: in an impulse purchase a consumer may feel and do before thinking, and the response is delayed (the **lagged effect**): De Beers does not know which ad sold a particular diamond. Policarpio uses the same scheme for promotion objectives. Fennis and Stroebe credit DAGMAR with one merit: it ties each step to a measurable objective.

**Where they disagree.** Fennis and Stroebe (following Vakratsas & Ambler) reject the hierarchy as a description of the process. There is no evidence that people climb the steps in order. The sequence presupposes high involvement, which is the exception (Krugman). The consumer is seen as passive and advertising as the only stimulus. Because the steps can be measured, the sequence looks real, although recall correlates only weakly with attitude. Percy and Rosenbaum-Elliott keep the idea of a sequence but move it to the level of processing a single message (McGuire), with compounding probabilities. History explains the rigidity: AIDA started as a copywriter's writing rule, not as a psychological theory.

## Application

- Use the hierarchy as a checklist of objectives (do we want awareness? attitude? trial?) and measure the step you chose. Do not judge an awareness campaign by next week's sales.
- Do not make every effect depend on awareness: with impulse products, action can come first.
- When a client asks to "climb all the AIDA steps", turn the request into measurable communication objectives.

## Example

Aflac had awareness (the duck), but people did not know what the company sold. The "Aflac Isn't" campaign defined the offering by what it is not (not car insurance, not home insurance), to unblock the interest step.

## Questions

- Why does AIDA survive in textbooks and agencies when it has been contested for more than 20 years?
- On social media, can "action" (a share, a like) come before awareness?
