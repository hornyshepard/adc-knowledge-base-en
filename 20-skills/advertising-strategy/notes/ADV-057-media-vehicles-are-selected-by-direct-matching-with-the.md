# Media vehicles are selected by direct matching with the audience's brand behavior, not by demographic matching

> **The idea in one sentence:** "women 18–49 with incomes over X" read both Elle and Country Life; what counts is which vehicle your buyers are in, not which demographic the vehicle has.

## Explanation

Media strategy is implemented through **vehicles**: programs, magazines, user segments. Most planners still use **demographic matching**: they define the target demographically, then look for vehicles with the same audience profile. The authors put the word "match" in inverted commas. If brand usage is 10% in the overall population and much more concentrated among women 18 to 49 with incomes over €15,000 a year, nothing guarantees that brand usage is evenly distributed among the magazines with this profile. Some of these women read Elle for high fashion, others read Country Life. For country casual clothes, the first choice is probably wrong. Besides, people with the same demographic profile hold very different attitudes toward the brand. Psychographic matching has the same problem.

The solution is **direct matching** (Wells and Tigert): you match the target buyer groups directly with the brand behavior of each vehicle's audience. Ideally, through research on the target's media behavior. Any indirect matching through a third set of variables lowers the success rate. **Programmatic** buying has a structural limit: at best it can only reach current category or brand users, not potential ones.

Three rules for selecting vehicles:

| Objective | Rule |
|---|---|
| High reach | many competing vehicles, one ad in each, with minimal between-vehicle duplication |
| High frequency | multiple insertions in a few vehicles that occur repeatedly on a regular basis (daily or weekly programs), with high duplication |
| Both | multiple insertions in several vehicles with large competing audiences, an expensive option |

"Vehicle quality effects" are small, if they exist at all, and are far outweighed by differences in the size of the target audience reached.

An extra layer is **brand ecology**: the emotional, social and cultural relationship of the active consumer with their media (must-see appointments, trust in a newspaper, solitary or social consumption). The same person can move in one evening from low-involvement comedies to a high-involvement match. Once researched, this relationship also informs the creative brief.

## Application

- Ask for TGI-type data (brand behavior × media consumption), not only demographic profiles of the vehicles.
- In digital, add to the targeting of current users some vehicles that reach potential customers (for example those who switch between other brands).
- Write in the brief the "mode" in which the target consumes the chosen vehicle: relaxed or attentive, alone or in a group.

## Example

A fishing equipment brand does not look for "men 35–55" on TV. It looks for the programs, YouTube channels and social media groups followed by people who already buy equipment in the category.

## Questions

- How do you apply direct matching when first-party data is limited by regulations such as GDPR?
- Does "vehicle quality" still make sense for brand safety in digital?
