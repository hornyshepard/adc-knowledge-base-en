# P&G's copy strategy fixes in writing the objective, the benefit with its proof, and the tone, not the execution

> **The idea in one sentence:** the copy strategy is the short document that states the basis on which we expect our product to be preferred over the competition's; how to dramatize that basis is the creative team's job.

## Explanation

Procter & Gamble, the pioneer of brand management (Neil McElroy's 1931 memo for Camay soap set out the duties of the "brand men"), also created one of the first rationalist strategic advertising mechanisms: the **copy strategy**, also known as the *copy platform* or *creative strategy*. For P&G it is the part of marketing strategy that refers to advertising: it derives from the product and the need it is meant to satisfy, and it expresses the fundamental benefit, the primary basis of purchase. The strategy belongs to the client, the execution to the agency. The document became a model for the creative work plan (Y&R), the T-Plan (JWT) and the creative strategy (Ogilvy & Mather).

Three versions of the same document, from the three sources:

| Arens et al. (in Fernández Gómez) | *La boîte à outils* (French school) | Milavsky (*Overcome AD-versity*) |
|---|---|---|
| objective statement (with the brand and the target) | promise (*promesse*, drawn from the positioning) | the target audience's current belief |
| support statement: the evidence behind the promise | proof (*preuve*) or reason why | target |
| tone or brand character statement | tone (*ton*) | goal (a single one) |
| | constraints (*contraintes*: budget, brand charter, law) | promise: one sentence, no "ands" or "buts" |
| | | support: at most three points |
| | | tonality and character; legal requirements |

Fernández Gómez links it to the USP: both are product-centric and built on a tangible benefit, and both assume a rational consumer. Milavsky adds two practical rules: read the strategy out loud before any creative presentation; if sales are not going well, change the commercials first, then the campaign idea, and only last the strategy.

## Application

- Write the copy strategy before any idea: one sentence of objective, one promise, one proof, one tone. If it does not fit on half a page, it is not yet a strategy.
- Check the link between promise and proof: every benefit needs a credible "because". A promise that piles up many arguments loses its force.
- Use it as a filter when judging ideas: the execution is free, but it must deliver the promise and respect the tone.
- The evaluation grid in *La boîte à outils* (p. 87): does the promise answer the problem, is it consistent with the positioning, original, right for the target and the brand, adaptable across every medium and acceptable internally?

## Example

Former P&G chairman Ed Harness sums up the philosophy: if the consumer does not perceive any real benefit in the brand, no amount of ingenious advertising can save it. Hence the creative agencies' criticism: the P&G style, tested obsessively with Burke's Day-After Recall, produced commercials that repeated the brand name five or six times in 30 seconds.

## Questions

- When does the rigidity of the copy strategy become a brake (for example, for emotional or lifestyle brands)?
- What would a copy strategy need today to work on conversational channels?
