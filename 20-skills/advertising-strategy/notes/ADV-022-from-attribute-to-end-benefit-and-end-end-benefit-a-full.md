# From attribute to end benefit and end-end benefit: a full inventory, the "so what" test and dropping category benefits

> **The idea in one sentence:** most of a product's attributes do not interest the buyer; the strategic work is to sift them until a single end benefit remains, motivating and the brand's own, ideally with a psychological or social reward on top of it.

## Explanation

Milavsky asks that all advertising development start with the product, seen **from the buyer's point of view**. The steps:

1. **The inventory of attributes**: name, form, process, function, place in the person's life, social setting, price, history. Make the complete list, without stopping at the first good idea.
2. **The "so what" test**: if challenging an attribute with "so what" does not get a compelling "really important" response, you throw it out. What brings no WIIFM (What's In It For Me) does not go into the ad.
3. **Drop the parity benefits.** Benefits common to the category (all soaps clean) create **primary demand**, which helps each brand in proportion to its share. With 20% of the market, 80% of the money spent on a category benefit works for the competitors. You need **secondary demand**: the reason to choose your brand.
4. **Look for the end benefit**, the result after use, and the **end-end benefit**, the personal or social gain on top of it.
5. **Reduce it to one sentence** of the form Our product X is better at Y, with no caveats or conditions.

The manufacturer's process (ingredients, logistics, the plant) matters to the customer only if it translates into a benefit: telling people that your job is easy and mine is hard impresses no one.

## Application

- Talk to real buyers: they, not the manufacturer, are the experts in how the product is used.
- Group buyers by their relationship with the product. For a dog food, Milavsky ranged owners from "surrogate child" to "livestock"; the type of food they chose followed the scale.
- For a retailer selling someone else's product: let the manufacturer praise the product and communicate what makes the store unique (secondary demand for the dealer, not for the brand).

## Example

In a lecture at Wharton, the students chose a "dull" product: Joy dishwashing liquid. The attributes: a clear, golden liquid, a yellow squeeze bottle, a lemon scent. The end benefit: a detergent leaves no film, unlike a fat-based soap; the plate shines like a mirror. The end-end benefit, added by the voice-over announcer: "isn't that a nice reflection on you?" Cleaning becomes a personal and social evaluation. For Jell-O pudding the chain was pudding → milk → calcium → strong bones for kids.

## Questions

- When is a category benefit worth communicating (for example, by the market leader or at the launch of a new category)?
- Can the end-end benefit become manipulative (the guilt of a homemaker judged by her dishes)?
