# The pretest is built on the communication objectives, with questions in a fixed order, and is never done through focus groups or advertising recall

> **The idea in one sentence:** the pretest checks whether the execution follows the brief and produces the intended brand effects; the manager's experience does not replace it, and even gets in the way, because the manager "sees" links the target does not see.

## Explanation

The authors insist: "it is foolish not to pre-test", however experienced you are. Someone who knows the brand processes the execution top-down and automatically makes the brand-benefit connection, which the target may not make. The pretest covers only the creative content; media placement and the competition are evaluated through tracking.

**Three methods to avoid:**

1. **Focus groups**: they overexpose the execution (long discussions against a few seconds of attention in real life) and replace the individual reaction with group dynamics. They are useful for strategy, not for execution.
2. **Advertising recall** (*day-after recall*): at most a rough measure of attention, not of brand effects. No pretest measure based on it has been shown to predict effectiveness.
3. **Physiological measures on their own**: EEG shows that "something is going on", but the interpretation is unclear; eye tracking relates only to brand recognition.

**The order of measures** (Table 14.2), chosen so that one question does not influence the ones after it:

| # | Measure | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Attention | only for print and banners, through a timed portfolio test |
| 2 | Learning (low involvement) | "What do you think the advertiser is trying to tell you?", plus "Which brand was it?" |
| 3 | Acceptance (high involvement) | open cognitive responses, coded afterwards |
| 4 | Purchase intention | exact wording, conditional on category need; not measured for low-involvement transformational ads |
| 5 | Brand attitude | specific to the situation, relative to competitors |
| 6 | Benefit delivery | diagnostic; the only attitude measure for low-involvement transformational ads |
| 7 | Brand awareness | at the end and on a delayed basis, compared with a control sample |

If there is no pretest, the manager at least carries out a **disciplined evaluation**: does it resonate with the target? is brand awareness executed correctly? is the benefit focused correctly? do the tactics fit the quadrant?

**Promotions are pretested too.** In 1992, Hoover offered in the UK two free flights to the US with any vacuum cleaner costing more than £100. The budget was £500,000. Consumers redefined the offer as "two tickets to the US for £100", and the cost reached £48 million, plus bad publicity.

## Application

- Build the pretest questionnaire from the brief: the quadrant decides whether you measure learning or acceptance and whether you measure intention.
- Ask for research adapted to the strategy, not only a standardized test from a research institute.
- Test concepts on the real target, not on colleagues or "consumers in general".

## Example

An agency wants to validate a spot through two focus groups. An alternative built on the authors' method: 150 people from the target buyer group, a single exposure, an open question about the message, attitude relative to competitors, and a phone call three days later for brand recall.

## Questions

- How would the order of measures adapt to online A/B tests, where you only have clicks and conversions?
- Does the criticism of focus groups also apply to asynchronous online groups?
