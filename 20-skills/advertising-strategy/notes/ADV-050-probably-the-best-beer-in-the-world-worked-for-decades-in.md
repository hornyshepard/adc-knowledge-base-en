# "Probably the best beer in the world" worked for decades in some markets, but the modest irony of "probably" did not translate in Russia and Asia

> **The idea in one sentence:** a slogan built on cultural values (here, Nordic modesty) is an asset in the markets that read them and a problem where they are not read; Carlsberg has swung between globalization and its roots.

## Explanation

Carlsberg is the fourth largest brewery in the world. Unlike China Resources Snow, it has a small home market, but it sells its premium lager in 150 countries. So it has to understand the strategically relevant cultural differences between those markets. The case study (Lars Pynt Andersen) follows its successive strategies:

| Period | Strategy |
|---|---|
| For many years, in Denmark and Sweden | the lager was branded under the name **Hof**, as a blue-collar beer for the people, with no claim to be "the best" |
| From the 1970s to 2011, export markets | **"Probably the best beer in the world"**, invented by a UK agency for the British market. Carlsberg was an imported premium brand, but never exclusive like Stella Artois ("Reassuringly expensive"). |
| 2011 | The growing markets in Russia and Asia did not understand the humble understatement in "probably", so the slogan was replaced with "That calls for a Carlsberg", which did not resonate anywhere |
| 2018 | global rebranding, "The Danish Way": new logo, bottle and packaging, with the actor Mads Mikkelsen riding a bicycle impossibly over Copenhagen rooftops and dining tables, asking why the Danes are so happy (hygge?). The final answer: "Probably." |
| 2019, UK | "Probably not": Carlsberg admits it is probably *not* the best beer in the world, that it focused on quantity rather than quality, and launches a new brew (#newbrew) |

The cultural link is the **law of Jante**, the core of the Nordic cultures described in the chapter: you should not think of yourself as special or better than anyone else. Modesty, equality, humility and skepticism. "Probably" is self-praise softened by Jante. In Denmark, "The Danish Way" tested as one of the best campaigns of 2018. The radical honesty of "Probably not" was well received even in beer connoisseur circles. The authors note that it was still too early to judge the effect on sales.

## Application

- Before exporting a slogan, identify the cultural value it rests on (modesty, irony, status) and check whether the target market reads it the same way.
- When a category fragments (craft beer, IPA), honesty can become a credible benefit. But it must be backed by a changed product.
- Use the gradient of cultural adaptation to decide what stays global (the irony, the character) and what is localized.

## Example

The book's discussion questions: how do the slogans reflect the law of Jante? Why would "probably the best" be a problem for the segments in Asia and Russia? Should Carlsberg localize or globalize its campaigns?

## Questions

- Is "Probably not" an attitude change (removing a negative belief) or a risk of undermining the brand?
- What brand personality, transferable across cultures, can "The Danish Way" build?
