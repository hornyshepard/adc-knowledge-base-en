# Jell-O Pudding with Bill Cosby: the campaign with a weak recall score that raised sales and ran for more than 20 years

> **The idea in one sentence:** the experts at General Foods and Young & Rubicam put the campaign on air only as a stop-gap, because it failed the recall test; sales rose by as much as 25 percent, and only focus groups showed that the moms loved the commercials.

## Explanation

In 1973, Jell-O Pudding was a tired old brand, with sales declining since the early 1950s, a small budget and the tag line "Kids Love Pudding". Milavsky, then a junior trainee at Y&R, lived through the whole cycle:

1. **A rejected commercial.** The "Sheldon" commercial, which the agency thought was hilarious, was turned down by the client: the mother's strong New York accent was read as too "ethnic". TV advertising stayed on hold for a year and a half.
2. **Three campaigns tested.** All had average to below average Burke Day After Recall (DAR) scores: less than 20 percent remembered the commercial the next day. Cosby, a very popular comedian who had never done a product endorsement, scored only marginally better.
3. **The stop-gap.** The Cosby commercials (a simple set, kids about six years old, bowls of pudding, copy that sounded spontaneous) went on air because the brand had been off air for so long and his fee was already paid. The agency kept looking for a "better" campaign.
4. **The anomaly.** Nielsen reports showed sales up by as much as 25 percent in some markets. The team put it down to statistical errors and promotions.
5. **The Atlanta focus group.** One participant asked to see the commercial again, then again. The moms did not just like it: "Not liked. Loved." The media budget was doubled, and the campaign ran for more than 20 years.

The author's lessons: the campaign followed the same strategy as the previous ones; Cosby's credibility came from his relationship with kids; warmth and humor relaxed the "fast brain"; and the client, for lack of alternatives, was **patient**. Coda: Cosby was later disgraced, and a celebrity can turn from an asset into a liability at any time.

## Application

- Do not let a single pretest measure (next-day recall) decide the fate of an idea. Recall measures how provocative a commercial is, not how much it is loved.
- Keep tracking sales after you launch a "temporary" solution: the audience may see what the experts did not.
- When you choose a celebrity, look for relevance to the promise and include a moral turpitude clause.

## Example

Cosby's contract (1974): 100,000 dollars a year, with 10 percent increases, dessert category exclusivity only, a limited number of shooting days, so three commercials were shot in two days.
