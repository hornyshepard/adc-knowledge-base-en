# The low-involvement informational strategy calls for problem-solution and a single benefit presented in the extreme, and the ad need not be liked

> **The idea in one sentence:** when the risk is low and people want to solve a problem, it is enough to make them curious (could it really be that soft?); trial does the rest.

## Explanation

The quadrant covers low-risk, negatively motivated decisions: detergents, toilet tissue, everyday pain relievers, cleaning products. The target audience does not need to believe the message literally. **Curious disbelief** (Maloney) is enough: if it does not work, you simply do not buy it again.

**The principles** (ch. 9, Table 9.4):

1. Information that solves a problem through use of the brand, usually in a **problem-solution** format ("Look at that dirty shirt!" → the brand cleans it).
2. **Simplicity**: one benefit or a group of mutually reinforcing benefits.
3. **Extreme presentation** of the benefit ("irresistibly clean").
4. The ad **need not be liked**.

**The creative tactics** (ch. 13, Box 13.1):

- the problem comes first and is resolved by the brand;
- one, at most two, benefit claims;
- an E⁻ → C benefit focus (a negative emotion resolved by a characteristic), A → C or, only here, a simple C. For example, the Philips Body Groomer: you understand the benefit without reading the copy;
- the emotional sequence that fits the motive: mild anxiety → relief (problem removal), fear → relaxation (problem avoidance), disappointment → hope (incomplete satisfaction);
- humor, if used, must be product-relevant and must not distract from the benefit;
- **source**: a presenter with perceived **expertise**. Objectivity is not needed.

**Media**: almost any medium, because the benefit must be learned in one or two exposures. The exception is a benefit that needs demonstrating.

## Application

- Write the script in three beats: visible problem → the brand → the exaggerated but plausible result.
- Cut every secondary benefit from the copy.
- Do not reject an execution just because the focus group did not "like" it. Ask whether people took away the benefit and the brand.

## Example

For years P&G's Charmin used the character of Mr. Whipple, an obnoxious store clerk who scolded shoppers: "Ladies, please don't squeeze the Charmin." Everyone hated the commercials, but the brand became the category leader. At the shelf, people did not think about the annoying ad; they wondered whether the tissue was really that soft, and tried it. Surf ("irresistibly clean" clothes, pulled off people) and Fairy (a baby's scratchy clothes, resolved by softness) show the same logic.

## Questions

- Doesn't the extreme benefit risk becoming deceptive advertising? Where is the line with *puffery*?
- Why don't the same rules work when the risk is high (see the high-involvement informational quadrant)?
