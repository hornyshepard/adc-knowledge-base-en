# Joannis's psychological axis: the campaign chooses either to stimulate a motivation or to lessen a restraint

> **The idea in one sentence:** every purchase is a conflict between forces that push (motivations) and forces that hold back (restraints); the campaign's axis is the element that, under advertising pressure, tips the balance in favor of the product to the greatest degree.

## Explanation

Henri Joannis (Rochas, McCann Erickson) was the first theorist who came from advertising, not from psychology or marketing, to apply motivational research systematically to the creation of ads (*De l'étude de motivation à la création publicitaire*, 1965; *Le processus de création publicitaire*, 1978). His central concept, the **axis** (also called the axis of communication, strategic axis or psychological axis), became part of agency jargon in France and Spain.

The logic:

- **Motivations** are positive forces that push toward purchase: hedonistic (pleasure), self-giving (the desire to do good for others, above all the family) and self-expression (self-fulfillment).
- **Restraints** are negative forces: **inhibitions** (motivations seen as frivolous or shameful) and **concerns**, associated with people's fears (perceived risks).
- *La boîte à outils de la publicité* keeps Joannis's classification in today's textbook and adds Claude Raymond Haas's **tendencies** (*tendances*): egoistic (self-preservation, domination), altruistic (closeness, sympathy), ego-altruistic (self-esteem, ambition), idealistic. Under restraints (*freins*) it names **fears** (*peurs*: real or imagined difficulties), **risks** (*risques*, financial for example) and **inhibitions**. Communication objectives explicitly aim to "lever les freins" [lift the restraints].
- The axis chooses a single pressure point: the strongest motivation or one that no product satisfies; or the most vulnerable restraint or one that our product lessens more than competitors' products do.

The process has distinct stages: the **axis** ("what to say", a strategic decision based on research) → the **concept** (the concrete, credible representation of the chosen satisfaction, "how" to say it; this is where creation begins) → the execution. The same "what" can become humor or drama.

Joannis insists that a product moves not in an economic context but in a moral, family and human one, and that some products serve to show who you are or who you want to be. Fernández Gómez sees the axis as an improved version of the USP: still few reasons and repetition, but centered on psychological satisfaction, not on the product. Unlike the star strategy or Lovemarks, it is a mechanism for creating ads, not for building brands.

## Application

- Make two columns for your category: motivations (by the three types) and restraints (inhibitions, concerns). Note what the consumer would have to believe for each one to change.
- Choose a single axis and write it as a strategic sentence; only then look for the creative concept.

## Example

A message about the health risks of alcohol can use the same axis (fear of the consequences) in opposite executions, humorous or dramatic: the author's example for separating axis and concept.

## Questions

- Joannis warned that a single axis puts you in a position of weakness: if you choose wrongly, you have nothing in reserve. How do you reduce the risk?
- When is it more effective to lift a restraint than to amplify a motivation?
