# The high-involvement informational strategy must convince, with claims kept within the target audience's latitude of acceptance or with a refutational approach

> **The idea in one sentence:** when the risk is high and people want to solve a problem, they integrate the message with what they already know; if you claim more than they can believe, they reject the whole message.

## Explanation

The quadrant covers risky, negatively motivated decisions: household appliances, televisions, computers, financial services, many B2B decisions. The target audience must be **convinced**. It will actively process the information and compare it with declarative memory, so the result depends on existing attitudes toward the brand, the product and the category.

**The principles** (Table 9.6):

1. Provide **sufficient information** to solve the problem.
2. The target audience must be convinced.
3. **Limit the message to acceptable levels** of the target audience's attitudes.
4. As long as the message is believed, the execution need not be liked.

The theoretical tool is Sherif and Hovland's **assimilation-contrast theory**. Between what a person readily accepts and what they reject outright lies an area of indifference. The strategist must know, from research, where the **latitude of rejection** begins, and must not cross the line. You can "stretch" attitudes, but carefully.

**The creative tactics** (Box 13.1):

- claims at the upper level of believability, without over-claiming. The book's example: a washing machine with a load capacity 50% bigger than most, an extra-large door, a tilted drum and a specialized duvet cycle that "will cut out those trips to local dry cleaners." All of them must seem basically true;
- if the brand can actually deliver a benefit that the target audience does not believe, use a **refutational approach**: first acknowledge the disbelief ("we know you don't believe that…"), provide evidence, then restate the claim firmly. You start from the audience's agreement, not from its rejection;
- an A → C benefit focus (the attribute supports the subjective claim) or E⁻ → C;
- the number of claims must be limited even here: irrelevant information dilutes the benefits;
- **source**: credibility, that is, **expertise and objectivity** (sincerity);
- a strong visual focused on a single point, plus a short headline that draws the reader into the copy.

**Media**: processing time, so print and digital. Broadcast media (TV, radio) do not suit as the primary medium for the full argument. Frequency is not an issue: the argument must be accepted after one or two exposures.

## Application

- Before writing copy, research what people already believe about the category and the brand. Without that, you do not know where the line is.
- Write each claim with its proof (A → C). Drop the claims you have no credible proof for.
- For deeply negative beliefs, consider a fear appeal only if the proposed solution is easy to put into practice.

## Example

Whirlpool ("the biggest door opening in the market", so it is easier to unload) is an A → C for high involvement. Panasonic Viera combines a vivid, believable visual (picture clarity), copy that reinforces the benefit and a need → brand structure for recall.

## Questions

- What happens to a true claim that falls within the latitude of rejection? What alternative does the book propose?
- How does assimilation-contrast theory apply in B2B, where the decision maker is an expert and skeptical?
