# An effective ad does three things (gains attention, amplifies the benefit, links it to the brand), and without the third the audience remembers the story, not the brand

> **The idea in one sentence:** an effective ad does three things: it gains attention, it gets people to relate the message to what they know and what they want, and it clearly links the benefit to the brand name; if the third step is missing, people remember the story but not the brand.

## Explanation

Brian Sternthal starts from a creative brief derived from the positioning. The Burger King example: objective: grow the frequency of visits; target: men aged 18–34; insight: they feel they have little control over their lives; position: the most satisfying food, because large portions and tasty options let you "have it your way"; personality: fun, irreverent; mandatories: food visuals with appetite appeal; equities: "Have it your way".

**1. Attention.** Novelty, surprise and humor (Le Trèfle toilet paper: 25 million YouTube views in 10 days, sales up 130%). A threat appeal works only if it also offers an effective way to overcome the danger; otherwise people engage in fear control (the smoker who lights a cigarette to calm the fear).

**2. Amplification (elaboration).** People must relate the message to their knowledge and their goals. The techniques, depending on the situation:
- announcing the brand benefit (*hard-sell* advertising, Ted Bates: M&M's "Melt in your mouth, not in your hand");
- amplifying the benefit (framed for a promotion or a prevention orientation);
- proof through an attribute (Grainger);
- comparison (Surface Pro 3 vs. MacBook Air; a new product vs. its predecessor);
- analogy (the kangaroo protecting her young in her pouch, for Hanes boxer briefs);
- the spokesperson (with its risks: dilution, defection, scandal, death);
- *story grammar*: a problem the brand overcomes (Mercedes-Benz through a snowstorm).

**3. Brand linkage.** Late identification of the brand (*late ID*) invites other thoughts: the Genworth ad with Agassi and Graf was remembered, the brand almost not at all. An execution too similar to the rest of the category is just as dangerous (upscale hotel ads with lobbies and rooms with views sell the category, not the brand). The internet softens the problem: anyone who remembered an element can look the ad up.

Cindy Halvorsen (Google, ch. 22) adds the mobile context: the old three-step story arc (the accepted consumer belief, the brand benefit, the reason to believe) has given way to **starting with the peak of the story arc in the first five seconds**, with the brand established early to earn the next seconds. Her tips: talent speaking directly to the camera, tight framing, quick pacing with multiple peaks, high contrast, a clear call to action; review the cut on a phone first.

## Application

- Evaluate every script with three questions: what gains attention? how do people elaborate? where, and how early, does the brand appear?
- Choose the amplification technique by the strength of the benefit: a clear and important benefit is simply announced; one that is hard to demonstrate calls for an analogy or a story.

## Example

Allstate's "Mayhem" commercials have a late ID of the brand, but they drew over 20 million YouTube views in the first year, so the link was made online.
