# Liking for the ad transfers to the brand along two paths (the dual mediation hypothesis)

> **The idea in one sentence:** a liked ad improves brand attitude directly, as the affect carries over to the brand, and indirectly, by making the product claims easier to accept.

## Explanation

Fishbein held that advertising changes brand attitude only through beliefs about product attributes. Mitchell and Olson (1981) challenged this. They showed mock ads for a fictitious brand of facial tissues: some with explicit verbal claims about softness, others with only the brand name and a photograph of a fluffy kitten. The picture, too, created the softness belief. More importantly, **attitude toward the ad** (Aad) influenced brand attitude even when beliefs were statistically controlled.

MacKenzie, Lutz and Belch (1986) formulated the **dual mediation hypothesis**:

Aad → brand beliefs → brand attitude → purchase intention
Aad → brand attitude (directly, through evaluative conditioning)

The indirect path: the positive affect created by a liked ad increases the willingness to accept its claims. The direct path: the liking for the ad will "rub off" on the product.

An important limit, noted by Fennis and Stroebe: the effects are stronger for **novel brands**; prior brand attitudes reduce the impact of attitude toward the ad (Brown & Stayman, 1992).

## Application

- For launches and little-known brands, liking for the ad (likeability) is a legitimate objective: the execution itself builds the attitude.
- For mature brands, a likable ad is not enough; you need new beliefs or stronger existing ones.
- In pretests, measure Aad and brand attitude separately: an ad can be liked without helping the brand.

## Example

The tissues with the fluffy kitten (Mitchell & Olson): without a single word about softness, the image created both the belief and the liking that transferred to the brand.

## Questions

- Can an annoying (irritating) ad still be effective? Under what conditions?
- In a test, how do you separate the effect of liking the ad from the effect of its arguments?
