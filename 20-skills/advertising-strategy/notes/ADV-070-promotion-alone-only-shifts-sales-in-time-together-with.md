# Promotion alone only shifts sales in time; together with advertising it produces a ratchet effect, and their weighting is chosen by life cycle, differentiation and loyalty

> **The idea in one sentence:** a promotion without advertising brings a sales peak followed by a dip; advertising keeps the people it attracted, and each promotion + advertising cycle raises the customer base like a ratchet that does not slip back.

## Explanation

**The shared warning.** Grewal and Levy show that price cuts on products that can be stockpiled (household cleaning supplies) only shift purchases from the future to now, while on perishable ones (Dannon yogurt) they can take share from a competitor. Percy and Rosenbaum-Elliott describe the same phenomenon through the **ratchet effect** (Bill Moran, Fig. 16.3):

- promotion only: a sales peak, then a dip below the normal level and a return to equilibrium. Nothing gained, unless the promotion brings new loyal customers;
- advertising only: steady but slow growth;
- together: the promotion speeds up purchase, advertising keeps the people it attracted, and the base grows in steps.

The mechanism: a strong brand attitude makes your own promotions seem better value and competitors' promotions less effective. Moran speaks of **upside elasticity** (sales go up with a price cut) and **downside elasticity** (sales decline with a price increase). The best defense against competitors' price cuts is not to copy them but a strong advertising presence, plus tactical promotions. Brand attitude is advertising's strength, purchase intention is promotion's, and brand awareness belongs to both (Fig. 16.2). That is why traditional advertising "will almost always be more important", although it receives only about one-third of communication budgets.

**The weighting** (Table 16.1–16.2):

| Situation | Emphasis |
|---|---|
| Launch | both, at a high level: awareness and trial |
| Growth, differentiated brand | advertising; promotion is unnecessary |
| Growth, undifferentiated brand | promotion to induce switching, taking care not to get people used to the lower price |
| Maturity, high loyalty | advertising; promotion only lowers the price |
| Maturity, low loyalty | advertising maintains awareness, promotion attracts and retains |
| Decline | only trade promotions, to keep distribution |

## Application

- Plan promotions *within* or *after* periods of advertising, not instead of them.
- Ask whether the promotion attracted new customers or only rewarded those who would have bought anyway, and what it does to price perception in the long term.
- When a competitor comes in with price cuts, first check whether attitude toward your brand is strong enough.

## Example

Old Spice (Grewal and Levy): the "Old Spice Guy" ads raised sales, but the "buy one, get one free" deals run at the same time essentially eliminated the potential profit impact. It is the textbook case of the promotion that sabotages the campaign.

## Questions

- What would a retail media specialist say, where advertising and promotion are almost the same format?
- How would the ratchet effect look in a digital subscription with a free first month?
