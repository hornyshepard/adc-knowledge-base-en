# The FCB grid (Vaughn) orders "think-feel-do" by involvement and type of decision, but ties involvement to the product category

> **The idea in one sentence:** the FCB grid corrected the fixed hierarchies by showing that the order of effects differs by involvement and by type of decision, but it placed whole categories in quadrants, although the same category can be high involvement for one person and low for another.

## Explanation

To correct the classic hierarchies, the agency Foote, Cone & Belding created the **FCB grid** (Richard Vaughn, 1980), with two axes: thinking versus feeling, and low versus high involvement.

| | Thinking | Feeling |
|---|---|---|
| High involvement | houses, loans, cars: think → feel → do | jewelry, perfume, fashion: feel → think → do |
| Low involvement | detergent, toilet paper: do → think → feel | candy, soft drinks: do → feel → think |

The grid is still cited in textbooks and agencies, but it is criticized from two directions.

**Fennis and Stroebe's critique.** The grid ties a level of involvement and a type of motivation to product categories. Milk bought because you have run out reflects an informational motive; milk craved for its taste, a transformational one. Involvement and motivation are **person variables**, and variables of the moment, not attributes of the product. The grid also keeps the assumption that all three responses always occur, in a sequence.

**Percy and Rosenbaum-Elliott's critique.** The grid does not distinguish category choice from brand choice. Its think-feel dimension is superficially conceptualized, because it assumes that only "feel" decisions involve emotions; negative motives involve emotions too (anxiety, then relief). The Rossiter-Percy grid replaces this axis with the type of motivation (negative or positive) and defines involvement as the risk perceived by the target audience.

**The tension.** Fennis and Stroebe apply the same critique to the Rossiter-Percy grid. Percy answers from inside the model: the product is placed in a quadrant for the specific target audience, not for the category. The critique still holds for the way the grids are usually used, by category.

## Application

- Use the grid as a starting point in the brief, then check through research where the product sits for your target audience, at its particular moment.
- Segment by motivation: the same coffee can be "morning fuel" or a "pleasure ritual", with different messages.
- With low involvement, do not write long ads full of arguments: behavior comes first.

## Example

FCB puts cars in "thinking + high involvement". Fennis and Stroebe note that for many people the car is a feeling choice (p. 37), so the same category can sit in two quadrants.

## Questions

- Which quadrant would you put a smartphone in? A streaming subscription? Would your colleagues agree?
- What would a grid built on segments of people, not on categories, look like?
