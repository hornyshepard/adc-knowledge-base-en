# Social marketing communication must change attitude and behavior, so it adds the social norm (Fishbein-Ajzen), the stages of change and a moderate fear that is then resolved

> **The idea in one sentence:** to stop smoking, speeding or drinking, a better attitude is not enough: you need a motivation as strong as the one that sustains the undesirable behavior, and a change in what "others" believe.

## Explanation

All the principles of the strategic process still apply, with four adaptations.

**1. The target audience by stage.** The transtheoretical model (Prochaska & DiClemente), in Andreasen's version: *pre-contemplation* (raise awareness), *contemplation* (persuade and motivate, the hardest task), *action* (initiate the behavior, perhaps with an incentive) and *maintenance* (promise favorable consequences or remind people of the consequences of slipping back). The population divides into **conformers** (the equivalent of brand loyals), **vacillators** and **offenders** (those loyal to the undesirable behavior).

**2. Positioning** is almost always repositioning: the desired behavior becomes more attractive, or the undesirable one less attractive. Failing to understand the motivations, especially among vacillators, is the main reason social programs fail.

**3. The social norm.** The expectancy-value model is extended into the **theory of reasoned action** (Fishbein & Ajzen): intention = personal attitude toward the behavior + perceived social norm, weighted by the motivation to comply. Socially visible behaviors (speeding, smoking in public) are more sensitive to the norm than private ones. Hornik observes that a social campaign changes personal attitudes little but can shift the norm a great deal over time, as happened with smoking.

**4. The appeal and the strategy.** Extreme fear fails: following McGuire, anxiety increases the motivation to change but triggers avoidance of the message. The optimum lies at the top of an **inverted U**: moderate fear, necessarily followed by **its reduction** through the desired behavior. The right strategy is high-involvement informational, often refutational.

**Charity** works differently: people give for the *warm glow* (Andreoni), and they give more readily to identifiable individuals than to large tragedies. **Ethics**: the exaggeration in social campaigns is, the authors say, a form of lying, which no appeal to a greater good justifies.

## Application

- Segment the campaign's audience by stage of change and write a different message for each.
- Measure personal attitude and the perceived norm separately. For visible behaviors, work on the norm.
- Calibrate the fear through pretesting, and never end without the concrete solution.

## Example

The ad for fighting motor neurone disease: "Lucy will lose her gran to motor neurone disease" arouses a measured anxiety for others, which the final headline, "Help her fight back", resolves. A little girl and her gran, identifiable people, not statistics.

## Questions

- How would you use the social norm in a campaign against drink-driving in Romania?
- Is exaggeration ethically acceptable in a road safety campaign?
