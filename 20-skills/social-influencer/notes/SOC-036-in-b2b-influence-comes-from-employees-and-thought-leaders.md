# In B2B, influence comes from employees and thought leaders

> **The idea in one sentence:** in business-to-business communication, the most credible influencers are not stars but recognized experts in the field, and even the company's own employees, who reach audiences the corporate channels do not touch.

## Explanation

Glenister describes two sources of B2B influence:

**Employees as micro-influencers (employee advocacy).** The cost is a fraction of that of external influencers, and employees are seen as more authentic. Data cited (Social Media Today): as few as 2 percent of employees regularly share employer content, although many would if they knew what to post; a brand message shared by employees reaches an audience 561 percent larger than through a corporate channel. IBM created a three-level program: content for the roughly 15 senior leaders, support for around 200 subject matter experts and an intranet with ready-to-share posts for the wider workforce. Siemens combines employees as "multipliers" with an external community of influencers.

**Thought leaders.** On LinkedIn, posts from personal profiles do much better virally than company page posts (AJ Wilcox); a viral post does not bring leads in the comments but direct messages from people who remember the expertise. Sarah-Jayne Gratton sums up the relationship with B2B influencers in the acronym **VVP**: Vision, Value and Passion, and in five principles: authentic alignment, content personalized to the audience's pain points, creative freedom for the storytellers, data for selection, relationships instead of transactions.

Phil Szomszor observes that negative practices (fake followers, lack of disclosure) are rarer in B2B, where collaboration more often means co-creating content.

## Application

- Before a B2B campaign with external influencers, audit which experts you already have in the company.
- Give employees themes, examples and freedom, not just links to share.
- On LinkedIn, use the voices of people, not only that of the brand.

## Example

During the pandemic, SAP moved its annual conference online, with only about 10 days to get the word out. It enlisted about a dozen thought leaders from tech and marketing (Mark Schaefer among them). The live event normally attracted about 25,000 people, and the optimistic estimate was 60,000 online registrations; 120,000 people signed up, and the servers crashed at the start.

## Questions

- What legal and reputational risks arise when employees speak publicly about the company?
- Must the employment relationship be disclosed when an employee recommends the product? (The FTC rules say yes.)
