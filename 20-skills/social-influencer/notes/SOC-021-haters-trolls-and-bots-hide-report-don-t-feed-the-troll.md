# Haters, trolls and bots: hide, report, don't feed the troll

> **The idea in one sentence:** the negative noise on social media comes from a very active minority, amplified by algorithms that measure the quantity of interaction, not its quality; the right response is calm, hiding comments and reporting them, not deleting or arguing.

## Explanation

In her contribution, journalist Deana Mrkaja starts from the 90-9-1 rule: only 1% of users are very active (and very loud), and this 1% includes trolls, haters and bots interested in a destructive discourse. Current algorithms do not conduct a qualitative analysis of posts; a shitstorm is evaluated as positive, because there is a lot of interaction, and is played out further. Hence the feeling that "everyone on social media has gone crazy".

Her checklist:

- **Prevention**: check the privacy settings (public or friends only, private profile), do not publish your address, phone number or email, hide your friend list, use two-step authentication, require permission for tags.
- **Calm**: every shitstorm has an end.
- **Hide, don't delete**: the author can see a deletion, and it can start a chain reaction of new comments; a hidden comment stays visible only to its author, and with no reactions the haters fall silent over time.
- **Don't feed the troll**: respond briefly and factually, with your line of argument, then ignore.
- **Report** comments and profiles to the platform; **block** repeat offenders.
- **Don't switch off comments** as a reflex: it could lead to an even bigger shitstorm.
- **Draw the legal line**: "stupid" is not a criminal offense, a death threat is; insult, defamation and incitement can be reported to the police.

## Application

- Write a moderation guide before launching a sensitive campaign: what you hide, what you report, what you escalate, who responds.
- Separate legitimate critics (who deserve an answer) from trolls (who want the reaction).
- Monitor more closely during livestreams and in the first hours after posting.

## Example

On Twitter, a mistake in a published tweet is corrected transparently by retweeting it with the correction in the comment, possibly with humor. According to the authors, this way of handling criticism even adds credibility.

## Questions

- When does "hiding" comments become censorship in the community's eyes?
- How does handling a troll differ from handling a real brand crisis?
