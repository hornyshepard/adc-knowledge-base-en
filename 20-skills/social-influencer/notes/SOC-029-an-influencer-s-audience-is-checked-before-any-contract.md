# An influencer's audience is checked before any contract: fraud signals, engagement authenticity and audience fit, manually or with AI

> **The idea in one sentence:** because an influencer's fee depends on followers and likes, some accounts inflate their numbers, so before paying, the brand checks three things: whether the audience is real, whether it is the brand's audience and whether the person fits the brand's values.

## Explanation

**The scale.** Glenister cites HypeAuditor: 63 percent of marketing specialists have had personal experience of influencer fraud; in 2022, 36 percent of the Instagram influencers analyzed were impacted by fraud, from about 32 percent among nano-influencers to about 60 percent among mega-influencers; on average, about 14 percent of followers are suspicious accounts. Grewal and Levy (SRC-001) put fraud first on the list of ethical concerns: the brand pays for reach that does not exist. The techniques: bought followers, likes and comments; automated follow/unfollow; *comment pods* (groups of often 30 to 40 bloggers who comment on each other's posts, hard to spot because they are real people).

**Glenister's checklist:** the followers-to-following ratio; sudden spikes in the growth chart; an engagement rate of 1 percent or less; a thin profile (no bio, links or contact); empty comments (wow, emojis only, the same commenters); a three-step manual check (the follower list, the likes on the last 12 posts, the comments and the accounts that wrote them); an audience quality score with a 70 percent cut-off (Mark Dandy).

**What AI adds.** Iroegbu (SRC-042) describes Influencer Marketing 2.0: automated analysis can assess at scale the fit between the audience and the target, the authenticity of engagement (follower growth patterns, the balance of comments, likes and shares), alignment with brand values, reputation (sentiment in mentions) and even the chances of success, based on past collaborations. Elle James (SRC-041) adds a source of candidates: image recognition finds the people who already show the product without being paid. The signals are the same as in Glenister, but he insists on real statistics and manual checks, while the AI books stay at the level of a list, with no cases backed by numbers.

## Application

- Make the check a standard step of selection; ask for access to the account's statistics or use platforms with authentication.
- Manually review the last 20–30 posts for values and tone: automated analysis of alignment is crude.
- Set out in the contract the consequences of falsified numbers; do not confuse the victims of bots with the fraudsters.

## Example

A fashion retailer told Sedge Beswick (Seen Connects) that influencer marketing doesn't work: it had worked with influencers who had bought their following, "fake-fluencers". Twelve months later, with vetted creators and clear KPIs, every £1 spent on influencers delivered a return of £17.

## Questions

- Is buying followers only the influencer's problem, or also that of the brands that pay by follower count?
- How does fraud change with generative AI?
