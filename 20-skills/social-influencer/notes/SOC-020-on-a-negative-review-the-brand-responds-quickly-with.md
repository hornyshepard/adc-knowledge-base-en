# On a negative review, the brand responds quickly, with empathy and professionally, moves the resolution to private and does not delete the criticism

> **The idea in one sentence:** both books agree: public criticism is a chance to show care in front of everyone, not a problem to hide.

## Explanation

Sachdev describes digital reputation management as a process with clear rules:

- **Do not ignore it.** Respond personally, acknowledge the frustration and provide a genuine solution; offer a number of ways to reach the brand.
- **Public or private, depending on the case.** If the issue and its resolution are relevant to all consumers, post the resolution. One-off gestures (a 10 percent discount for an unsatisfied customer) are not posted publicly, so they do not set off a slew of requests.
- **Neither too aggressive nor too slow.** An impulsive response makes things worse; a late one feeds the comments. A PR specialist can check the text.
- **Do not censor.** Deleting negative reviews destroys credibility; fake reviews are unethical. In the US, the law even prohibits penalizing customers for honest reviews.
- **Listen continuously.** Google Alerts, Brand24 and Hootsuite's sentiment analysis (an alert on negative spikes) help the brand find out quickly.

Nishad (SRC-033) adds the community management rules: do not argue with unsatisfied customers, reply in a professional manner and try to fix their problem, keep notifications on and reply within the first hour.

## Application

A five-step protocol for the social team: (1) acknowledge the problem and the emotion publicly; (2) ask for details in private; (3) resolve it; (4) if it is useful to everyone, come back publicly with the resolution; (5) record the cause as an insight for the product or service.

## Example

Best Buy responded on Facebook to a customer who had been told that a refrigerator would not fit, even after measuring the same model in a store: it acknowledged the frustration and asked for a private message. The oddity Sachdev notes: the post the customer complained under promoted the "Advice Delivered" campaign, and the problem was erroneous advice from a delivery driver. Taco Bell has a "Fish Bowl", a social listening room where the team reviews posts and mentions in real time.

- When is it better not to respond at all, for example to an obvious troll?
- How does the protocol change when the criticism goes viral?
