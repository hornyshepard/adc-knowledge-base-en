# Fyre Festival and other failures: what happens when the execution betrays the promise

> **The idea in one sentence:** influencers can sell a promise extremely fast, but if the product, the event or the execution does not deliver on it (or if the commercial relationship is hidden), the audience's backlash turns violently against the brand and the influencers alike.

## Explanation

The book's central case is the **Fyre Festival (2017)**: a luxury festival on a remote island, promoted by over 400 influencers, many of whom didn't disclose they were paid and gifted for the event. An ambiguous orange tile posted at the same time by the ambassadors reached over 300 million people in 24 hours; $250,000 was reportedly paid for a single post; 95 percent of the tickets sold within 48 hours. The reality: a production team with less than eight weeks to pull it off, a move to another island, promised villas that did not exist, teams that didn't know what they were supposed to be doing, investors who were misled. The social media backlash against the influencers was considerable and, the author notes, almost gleeful.

Other failures the book cites:

- **Scott Disick and Bootea**: instead of just posting the caption, he posted the entire instruction he had received from the brand, with the time to post and the caption to write.
- **Kim Kardashian and Diclegis**: the promotion of a morning sickness pill that had potential side effects.
- **Terrie McEvoy** rigged a competition so her friends won the prizes; she had to delete her Facebook account.
- **Scarlett Dixon for Listerine**: an image that looked overly staged drew bad comments and even some death threats.
- **Holly Willoughby**: 150 units per SKU sold in about 43 minutes, then over 500 customer care complaints after items sold out too fast.

The recommended crisis steps: (1) **respond immediately**, through a dedicated person, even if you only acknowledge the complainant and say you are investigating; (2) **be transparent**: say "We are sorry", preferably also directly to the affected individuals; (3) **legal support** you can reach quickly. As prevention: legal compliance, honesty with the audience, no jumping on trending hashtags, care around major national stories.

## Application

- Run a "pre-mortem" before any influencer campaign: what can go wrong with the product, the logistics, the communication, the disclosure?
- Prepare the customer care teams and the stock before celebrity partnerships.
- Write a crisis protocol before the launch, not after.

## Example

The book's positive counterexample: an influencer who says honestly "so sorry that the stock has run out. We've got more on the way…" and thanks followers for their patience protects trust better than any silence.

## Questions

- Who carries the main blame in the Fyre case: the organizer, the paid influencers or the platforms?
- How would you rebuild trust after a public failure like Bootea?
