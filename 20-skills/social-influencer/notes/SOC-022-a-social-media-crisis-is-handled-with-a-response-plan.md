# A social media crisis is handled with a response plan prepared in advance, not with improvisation

> **The idea in one sentence:** a social media crisis is not one customer complaining but a storm of negative responses; the brands that get through it have active monitoring, a decision tree for the response and a tone they own.

## Explanation

Customers increasingly reach out on social media first, before email or phone, and expect a quick response: about 50 percent expect a response in less than three hours, and the fastest airline responders on Twitter answer in under five minutes (JetBlue). Jeff Bezos, quoted by the authors: a customer you make unhappy in the physical world might tell six friends; on the internet, 6,000. The Customer Rage study shows that 14 percent of complainants share their experiences on social media an average of 16 times per year.

Storms start from questionable advertising (JCPenney's teapot ad), new product launches (Walmart's Juneteenth ice cream), bad employee behavior or the brand's own posts. Prevention is **social media listening** (Meltwater, Salesforce Social Studio, DataEQ), which also catches the problem outside the owned channels. The response is a **written plan**, like the comment triage flow chart of a community bank (Figure 9.12): is the post positive or negative? Is it true? Does it pose a legal risk? Will the online community police itself within 24 hours? Will taking action bring more attention to the problem than taking no action? The plan also sets the rules: a 24-hour response goal (one hour for urgent cases), documented sources, a friendly tone, no rushing, priority for active participants.

## Application

- Along with the social strategy, deliver a crisis protocol to the client: who approves, how fast, in what tone.
- Prepare standard wording for the likely scenarios in the category (delays, defects, communication blunders).
- Respond with empathy and specifics; a fast but defensive response feeds the storm.

## Example

On International Women's Day, **Burger King** tweeted "Women belong in the kitchen", and the wave of reactions forced it to come back with a public response (Figure 9.11). The brand produced this crisis itself; the authors leave students to judge the response. **KFC UK** (2018), described by Connors (SRC-030), did the opposite: out of chicken because of logistical issues, it ran, with the agency Mother London, a full-page newspaper ad showing the bucket with the letters rearranged to spell "FCK", plus a direct apology. Owning the mistake, self-deprecating humor and speed turned the crisis into a brand moment.

## Questions

- Would KFC's tone have worked for a bank or a hospital? Where does humor stop in a crisis?
- When is it better not to respond?
