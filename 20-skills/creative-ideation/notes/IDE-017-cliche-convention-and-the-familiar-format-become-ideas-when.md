# Cliché, convention and the familiar format become ideas when they are turned on their head

> **The idea in one sentence:** a visual language the audience recognizes from afar creates an expectation; when you fill it with an unexpected message, the expectation collides with reality and the viewer is caught.

## Explanation

Ingledew describes three variants of the same mechanism.

1. **Clichés seen afresh.** A cliché is an image that used to work but has become stale through overuse. You do not avoid it: you assemble the clichés and reinvent them. Jonathan Barnbrook took the illustration "The March of Progress" (the evolution of man) and turned the final figure, with a gun, back on the others. "A cliché seen afresh has twice its original potency": the viewer gets the message *and* recognizes the smartness of the reinvention.
2. **Creative collisions.** Many genres have a visual language that announces the message from afar: the boxing prize-fight poster, the holiday postcard, loveheart sweets, the pizza box, the aeroplane safety card. You use them for another message. A Warhol–Basquiat show was advertised as a boxing match, with the old champ and the young contender. Loveheart sweets with flirtatious messages carried sexual health messages. A student poster against the Iraq war hijacked the visual language of the easyJet campaign.
3. **Familiar but seen afresh.** You piggyback on a very familiar format or a long-running campaign. The "iRaq" flyposter used the iPod silhouettes with the image of the tortured prisoner at Abu Ghraib. London Transport reused the same silhouettes against pickpockets. Australian activists turned the Marlboro slogan into "New. Vile a Bore".

The mechanism ("Brainjack"): the viewer expects the next in a long line of similar messages and gets something else. The greater the collision, the bigger the crash in their mind. At the strategic level, BDDP's Disruption method follows the same logic: you identify the category convention, break it and propose a vision ([[ADV-046-disruption-dru-bddp-identify-the-category-s-convention|ADV-046]]).

## Application

- Make an inventory of the category's visual clichés (for film festivals: clapperboards, film spools, cameras). Pick one and ask: how can I make it new?
- Collect "well-behaved" visual languages and look for the least fitting message for each.
- Check the right to parody: another brand's format can raise legal or ethical problems.

## Example

Jonathan Barnbrook took "The March of Progress", the illustration of the evolution of man, and turned the final figure, with a gun, back on the others. The cliché is recognized instantly, and its twist says something about violence without a single word.

## Questions

- When does parodying a well-known campaign become free advertising for the parodied brand?
- Is it easier to subvert a format than to create one? Which brand wants to be merely the "commentary" on another?
