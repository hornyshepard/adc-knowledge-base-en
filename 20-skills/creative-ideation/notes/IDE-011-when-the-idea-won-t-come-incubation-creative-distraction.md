# When the idea won't come: incubation, creative distraction and random triggers

> **The idea in one sentence:** once you have filled your mind with the brief, ideas often connect when you stop looking at the problem; and when you are completely stuck, a stimulus picked at random breaks the pattern.

## Explanation

**Incubation.** Ingledew describes creativity as a 24-hour-a-day process, with active work and "unconscious" work: in the bath (Archimedes), with music (Sherlock Holmes, quoted by James Webb Young), at the wheel, on a walk, in sleep. That is why agencies kit out their studios with pool tables. Ogilvy's advice: "Stuff your conscious mind with information, then unhook your rational thought process." Dreams and daydreams rearrange things differently; night-time ideas are written down at once, because they vanish quickly. The classics collected by Watkins confirm it: Rubicam left the line in a drawer for a few days so he could judge it coolly ([[IDE-024-is-the-idea-born-alone-in-a-pair-or-in-a-meeting-the|IDE-024]]).

Braun and Landa put the same intuition into a historical frame: Graham Wallas's four-stage model (1926: preparation, incubation, illumination, verification) and James Webb Young's five-stage version, still used in advertising. They add **associative thinking**: you let the mind wander freely among associations, then consciously pick the thoughts relevant to the goal. The researcher Zachary Irving finds in the wandering mind a neural pattern similar to the one in creative thinking tasks.

**Random triggers** (Ingledew's "Stuck? Try these strategies"):

| Technique | How it works |
|---|---|
| The random word | Edward de Bono: a word picked at random from the dictionary as a catalyst for new connections |
| Cards with cryptic remarks | decks such as the one co-created by Brian Eno (one card tells you to do the opposite) |
| Chance creation | you cut up and assemble snippets of unrelated text |
| Fifth book along | the author's strategy: you borrow the book related to the brief and the fifth one along from it on the shelf, in both directions |
| Sketchbooks | you rethink and adapt old ideas |
| A random A–Z entry | you apply a technique picked blind to the brief |

Waiting until the last minute also creates intensity, but Ingledew does not recommend it as a first-choice strategy.

## Application

- Plan the incubation: finish the research a day before the ideas meeting.
- Keep your phone or a notebook at hand for the ideas that turn up "on the way".
- When the team goes round in circles, draw a stimulus at random (a word, a card, an A–Z entry) and work with it alone for 15 minutes.

## Example

Carl Spier (Hamilton) tried 40 or 50 approaches for the Christmas ad over several days, with no result. The first good line came when he wrote from his own experience, and he then wrote the ad in seven minutes: intense work, the pause and recombination, exactly the sequence Wallas and Webb Young describe.
