# Debate: is shock the most powerful weapon for attention or the biggest risk of a negative reaction?

> **The idea in one sentence:** Ingledew sees shock as the only shout that rises above the noise of the street; McTigue and Rucker see it as the main source of negative "amplification"; the difference lies between an offense taken on strategically and one produced through carelessness.

## Explanation

**For shock: Ingledew.** "Shock shouts louder than anything else in the visual cacophony of the street." It creates debate, challenges what can be shown publicly, and banned ads make the news and spread their message further. His examples: the Barnardo's ads about abused children, which a self-regulatory body advised newspapers not to run; the Sécurité Routière road safety poster; Susan Sontag, for whom photographs must shock in order to accuse. The related entry, "Surprise", cites George Lois: the element of surprise is what separates a brilliant idea from the pedestrian pack.

**Against: McTigue and Rucker.** What counts is what *stays* with the viewer (*amplification*): Quiznos wanted to say that the sandwich is so good you will fight to eat it even after a dog has chewed on it, and consumers were disgusted. The red flags in feedback include the risk of offending, and testing is recommended exactly when the audience's reaction is uncertain. Still, they do not ask you never to offend anyone either. Nike and Colin Kaepernick were a strategic choice; the problem is being called into the CEO's office because you accidentally offended a huge population.

**In between: Braun and Landa.** Their interviews defend discomfort: Luiz Sanches says you know you have a valuable insight when it makes you feel uncomfortable, and Yousuke Ozawa that you're better off being told "terrible" than being forgotten. The same book, however, offers a responsibility checklist: stereotypes, power, cultural appropriation.

**The synthesis.** All three sources accept that the risk of not being noticed is real. They differ on the price: shock as a *means* of bringing out a truth the target avoids (Barnardo's) is something other than shock as an *end*. History confirms both sides: Benetton and FCUK bought global awareness with small budgets, but moved the discussion from the product to taste and ethics ([[HIS-027-shockvertising-attention-won-through-scandal-has-a-price|HIS-027]]). And the research on fear appeals shows that strong fear does not "block" the message, as was once believed ([[INS-042-fear-appeals-work-better-the-more-severe-the-threat-the|INS-042]]).

## Application

- Ask: does the shock serve the insight and the benefit, or only attention?
- Separate the target from the secondary audience that will see the material anyway: who can be hurt?
- For shock ideas, testing is mandatory (see [[BRF-014-measurement-is-written-into-the-brief-before-the-campaign|BRF-014]]), and the decision to offend someone has to be written down and owned.

## Example

The Barnardo's ads about abused children, which a self-regulatory body advised newspapers not to run. Ingledew gives them as an example of shock that serves a truth the public avoids; the mechanism, he says, is that the stopped ad makes the news and spreads its message further.

## Questions

- Do social causes (NGOs, public health) have more "license to shock" than commercial brands? Why?
- Does the "banned ad becomes news" mechanism still work on social media?
