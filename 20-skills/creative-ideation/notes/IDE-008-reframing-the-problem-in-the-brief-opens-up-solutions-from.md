# Reframing the problem in the brief opens up solutions from another category

> **The idea in one sentence:** before you look for solutions, check whether the problem in the brief is defined correctly; changing the angle you look from changes the kind of answer that is possible.

## Explanation

Braun and Landa note a temptation: creatives start thinking about creative solutions right away, without examining the problem's depth or its connections to other issues. Reframing means stepping back: is there a larger or different issue at play? The first step is to question the problem in the brief:

- **Validity of the problem:** is the problem defined accurately? Are there factors that have not been addressed?
- **Misconceptions:** is there an assumption skewing the perception of the problem?
- **Layers:** which additional questions would reveal a broader or more intricate problem?
- **Category:** is it a problem of sales, awareness, a new audience, brand personality?

Three cases from the book:

| Case | The initial problem | The reframing |
|---|---|---|
| Suncorp (Leo Burnett Australia) | how do we communicate the protection insurance gives? | insurance is not about protection but about resilience: the prototype house "One House to Save Many" |
| Prego (told by Malcolm Gladwell) | what is the perfect spaghetti sauce? | Howard Moskowitz: there is no one perfect sauce, only different kinds of sauce that suit different kinds of people; the result was a whole product line |
| Coors Light "Made to Chill" | how do we associate ourselves with chilling out? | roofs turned into billboards: reflective white paint that cools down apartments in Miami |

One tool for reframing is Hal Gregersen's (MIT) **brainstorming for questions**. You describe the problem in two minutes, at a high level; the group has four minutes in which it may only ask questions, with no preambles or justifications, aiming for at least 15. Then you look for the questions that challenge your assumptions and commit to pursuing at least one of them. Unlike Osborn's rules for classic brainstorming (quantity, no criticism, wild ideas, combination), here you produce questions, not answers.

## Application

- Rewrite the problem in the brief in three versions: narrower, broader, from another angle. Which one does the client believe? Which one does the target live?
- Run a round of brainstorming for questions at the start of every team project.
- Check that the reframing stays within the brand's territory (see [[BRD-029-the-brand-construct-leads-to-archetype-personality-and|BRD-029]]).

## Example

Suncorp, "One House to Save Many" (Leo Burnett Australia): reframing insurance as resilience led to a prototype house, a campaign that, according to Braun and Landa, reached almost all of the target audience, and an influence on Australia's building codes. The problem in the brief ("how do we communicate protection?") would not have produced anything of the kind.
