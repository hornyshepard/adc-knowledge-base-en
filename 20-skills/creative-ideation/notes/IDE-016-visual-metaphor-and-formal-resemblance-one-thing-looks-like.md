# Visual metaphor and formal resemblance: one thing looks like another and borrows its qualities

> **The idea in one sentence:** when one object resembles another or fuses with it, the viewer transfers the qualities of one onto the other and closes the loop on their own.

## Explanation

Ingledew starts from etymology: in Greece, removal vans are called "metaphor", because metaphor *transports* meaning. A visual metaphor reveals that something familiar possesses the qualities of something else: it is removed from its original context and transferred to another. Ken Garland photographed a rope run over by a truck on a street in Galway. He first saw a Celtic knot, then the shape of the island of Ireland, then an unlit fuse. Nature offers ready-made metaphors: seeds for growth, tree rings for the passing of time, clouds for thoughts. Luke Sullivan, quoted by Ingledew, says metaphors involve the reader: they use images already in the reader's mind and "ask the reader to close the loop for us."

Three related techniques:

| Technique | Mechanism | Example from the book |
|---|---|---|
| One thing looks like another | the visual similarity between two objects | the foamy head of Boddingtons beer becomes a rocker's quiff (BBH); broccoli becomes a dumb-bell; Abram Games merged a spade and the front of a ship (grow your own food) |
| Fusion | merging two unrelated elements into a hybrid | Nick Park described *Chicken Run* as *The Great Escape* with chickens |
| Making faces | eyes and mouths appear in objects: headlights, sockets, intercoms | the face collections of Pentagram and Tibor Kalman |

In 1832 the caricaturist Charles Philipon was tried for drawing the king as a pear (French slang for a dullard). He defended himself by drawing, in front of the jury, a series of sketches in which the king's head turns step by step into a pear, and he was acquitted: the resemblance was undeniable.

## Application

- Describe the benefit as a quality ("tough", "light", "hot"), then look for objects that already have it. Try to make them look like the product.
- For fusion, write "X meets Y" with X and Y from different worlds, the way film directors pitch their films.
- Check that the resemblance is visible at first glance, without explanation; otherwise the metaphor becomes a rebus.

## Example

Priorin, a hair supplement, shows a flexed arm whose muscle is made of hair (the example from Percy and Rosenbaum-Elliott, [[IDE-004-the-creative-idea-must-first-dramatize-the-key-benefit|IDE-004]]). The metaphor lends the hair the strength of the muscle, and the viewer closes the loop alone: the hair becomes strong.
