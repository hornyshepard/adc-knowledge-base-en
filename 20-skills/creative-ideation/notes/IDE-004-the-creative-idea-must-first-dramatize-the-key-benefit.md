# The creative idea must first dramatize the key benefit claim, then get attention, speed up processing and support many executions

> **The idea in one sentence:** the most brilliant execution that is not consistent with the strategy loses to an average one that is; the idea is the catalyst that speeds up processing of the benefit, not an end in itself.

## Explanation

**The requirements for the creative idea** (Table 14.1):

1. **Dramatize the key benefit claim.** This is the main requirement.
2. **Attention-getting**, since without attention there is no processing, but without betraying the positioning. The authors give many Super Bowl commercials as a negative example: entertaining but not effective.
3. **Catalytically relevant**: it speeds up processing of the message, the way a catalyst speeds up a chemical reaction.
4. **Detailed enough to be easily executed** (the idea is not the ad, but its starting point).
5. **Lends itself to multiple executions**, as a platform for the campaign, against wearout (in most cases).
6. **Adaptable across media** (in most cases). The exceptions are one-off promotions and direct response.

**How many ideas?** The theory of "random creativity" says that the more ideas there are, the more likely you are to come up with a winner. Because only about 1 in 6 campaigns is successful, the authors recommend generating at least **six ideas** to be tested. The principle of volume appears in many other authors ([[IDE-009-volume-comes-before-quality-the-good-idea-comes-out-of-a|IDE-009]]).

**Four forms of the idea**, which are also the "conveyor" of the message:

| Form | When |
|---|---|
| **Key visual** (Kroeber-Riel) | the most common: one dominant image that illustrates the benefit |
| **Key benefit claim** | messages processed very briefly: posters, banners |
| **Auditory** | music, a jingle or a unique sound, with only minor variations |
| **Postmodern** | an unexpected, seemingly irrelevant approach with no obvious benefit. It gets attention, but a positive attitude is hard to build outside young targets (18–34). Testing is essential. |

**The remote conveyor model** (Kroeber-Riel): you choose an element that at first seems out of context in relation to the product. The incongruity triggers reflexive attention and curiosity. The brain searches the ad for the explanation, aided by a key benefit prompt. Resolving the incongruity brings a brief mental reward. The image is processed by the right hemisphere, then the verbal centers on the left look for the meaning.

## Application

- Write the key benefit from the brief above each idea and ask: does it dramatize it, or only get attention?
- For each idea, sketch three executions in different media. If you cannot, it is not a campaign idea.
- When you use a "remote conveyor", check in the pretest that the incongruity is resolved quickly and leads to the benefit, not only to amusement.

## Example

Priorin (a hair supplement) shows at first glance a flexed arm muscle, but the muscle is made of hair. The incongruity stops you, and resolving it delivers the benefit: strength for your hair. On 4 May 2010, BlackBerry tweeted "May the 4th be with you": it reached 150,000 people and drew a 15% increase in followers. The topical idea was consistent with the brand, which offered an app that turned the phone into a faux lightsaber.

## Questions

- Is the Old Spice "Smell like a man, man" campaign "postmodern"? And the 14-hour ad from Brazil?
- What is the difference between "shooting the brief" (translating it literally) and dramatizing the benefit?
