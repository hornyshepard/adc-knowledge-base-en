# The Big Idea: the concept that makes an ad different from competitors' ads combines strategy with surprise

> **The idea in one sentence:** the Big Idea is the story or central angle of a campaign that makes it different from, and better than, competitors' ads; it does not replace strategy, it delivers it in an unexpected form.

## Explanation

Richards notes that creatives commonly use the term for a campaign's creative concept: the story told in the ad and its tone (funny, serious or something else entirely). Its origin is disputed. Some say George Lois coined it (he wrote a book about the Big Idea with Bill Pitts, 1991), others claim it came from David Ogilvy. But the earliest printed mention the author found is by Daniel Starch, in 1914: such work is largely evolution, and a big idea cannot be created out of a single session of thinking.

The textbook example is "Lemon" (VW, Bernbach). The Big Idea was the ironic use of what appear to be critical remarks about the product. Richards stresses that the ad did have a USP, but it contained much more: humor and surprise, which delivered the claim.

Watkins, writing a decade before the creative revolution, says the same in other words: great advertising has in common "an idea, or concept, that can't be buried regardless of presentation". George Cecil, the author of the 1920s campaign that launched the coffee break, notes that there was nothing sensational about the words or the illustrations. The all-important thing, sometimes missing through lack of digging, was the copy idea.

In the final chapter, Keith Reinhard predicts that the industry will learn the difference between an algorithm and a true insight into human nature, and between big data and a big idea.

**What the other sources add.** Percy and Rosenbaum-Elliott require the idea to dramatize the key benefit and support many executions ([[IDE-004-the-creative-idea-must-first-dramatize-the-key-benefit|IDE-004]]); Braun and Landa talk about the North Star idea that keeps the whole campaign on course ([[IDE-005-the-north-star-idea-is-crystallized-with-the-three-gs-goal|IDE-005]]); Aly Richards and Scott McLean warn that a Big Idea without a Long Idea loses its audience after the first contact ([[ADV-047-a-big-idea-is-not-enough-without-a-long-idea-the-audience|ADV-047]]). All of them keep the core of the definition: strategy delivered in a surprising form.

## Application

- State the strategy first (what the person has to believe), then look for the surprising form in which that becomes memorable.
- Test the idea: can it be said in one sentence? Would it work in any medium? Could a competitor claim it?
- Look for irony, inversion or a "weakness" turned into an advantage, as with Lemon.

## Example

"Think Small" (VW, 1959) turned small size, seen as a flaw in the era of cars with tailfins, into the very reason to buy.

## Questions

- Does the Big Idea still make sense in campaigns split into hundreds of personalized variants?
- What is the difference between a Big Idea and a merely memorable execution?
