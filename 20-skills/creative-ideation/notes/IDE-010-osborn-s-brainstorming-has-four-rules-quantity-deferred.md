# Osborn's brainstorming has four rules (quantity, deferred judgment, wild ideas, combination) and keeps generating separate from evaluating

> **The idea in one sentence:** brainstorming is a group method in which everyone produces as many ideas as possible without judging them on the spot; evaluation comes only at the end, separately.

## Explanation

The method comes from advertising. Alex F. Osborn, the creative in the BBDO agency's name, introduced the term, and brainstorming became a hallmark of the agency. The sources do not give the same date: Richards points to the book *How to "Think Up"* (1942), while Braun and Landa place the launch in 1953, with *Applied Imagination*. Osborn, influenced by Wallas's stage model, saw it as group problem-solving, with four rules (according to Braun and Landa):

| Rule | What it means in the session |
|---|---|
| 1. Quantity | the target is the number of ideas, not the quality of each |
| 2. Deferred judgment | no one criticizes anyone else's ideas until the session ends |
| 3. Wild ideas | odd ideas are welcome, because they open new directions |
| 4. Combination | participants take up and improve on one another's ideas |

Marketing and problem-solving textbooks describe the same mechanics. Grewal and Levy, on new product development: no idea can be immediately accepted or rejected, the moderator may channel attention to specific attributes, and at the end the group votes on the best ideas or combinations of ideas. Ahmed adds practical details: a time limit, a neutral facilitator and the problem stated clearly before the start.

The core of the method is rule 2: the thinking that produces is kept separate from the thinking that judges. The same principle appears in the seven-step process ([[IDE-007-the-seven-step-problem-solving-process-from-defining-the|IDE-007]]) and in the distinction between the convergent strategist and the divergent creative ([[BRF-006-the-creative-brief-fits-on-one-page-in-three-blocks-task|BRF-006]]). Its limits are discussed too: Braun and Landa note that associative thinking happens in one person's mind, and many classic ads were written in solitude.

## Application

- Open the session with the brief reduced to one sentence, a time limit and an announced volume target.
- Someone writes everything down, unfiltered; evaluation happens after a break or in another session, with agreed criteria.
- Alternate individual and group work, so that dominant voices do not take the place of ideas.
- For vague briefs, replace the round of ideas with a round of questions (Gregersen).

## Example

"Soup on the Rocks" (Campbell's) was born, according to Watkins, in a meeting: one person asked "What would happen if you'd pour it over ice?", another thought it would be fun to find out, and somebody had the courage to try. That is the combination rule exactly: each one built on the other's idea.

- Why is the "no criticism" rule the one most often broken in agencies? Who usually breaks it?
