# Idea evaluation grids converge on three questions (does it serve the strategy, does it stand out, does it stay linked to the brand) but apply at different moments

> **The idea in one sentence:** each author has a grid, but all of them check whether the idea delivers the benefit in the brief, whether it stands out and whether the audience links it to the brand; what differs is the moment you apply it and what each one adds.

## Explanation

| Grid | Author | When you apply it | What it adds to the others |
|---|---|---|---|
| Requirements for the creative idea | Percy and Rosenbaum-Elliott | at the concept, before executions | dramatizing the key benefit claim is requirement no. 1; the idea must lend itself to multiple executions and be adaptable across media |
| A.L.T.E.R. | Braun and Landa | on the central campaign idea | being opportune (*Timely*), participation (*Engage*), fit with the company's values (*Lodestar*) |
| The idea on paper | Milavsky | before production | clarity, memorability, uniqueness and production cost |
| ADPLAN | McTigue and Rucker | on the execution the agency presents | what the audience actually feels (*amplification*) and fit with the brand's heritage (*net equity*) |
| Weighted matrix | Ahmed | when choosing between options | criteria with weights fixed in advance, scores multiplied and added up |

**Where they converge.** Three criteria appear, under other names, almost everywhere: (1) **strategy**: the idea dramatizes the benefit (Percy), the positioning comes through (ADPLAN), an idea that does not express the strategy is thrown out, however interesting (Milavsky); (2) **distinction**: attracting, attention, uniqueness, difference from the category; (3) **link to the brand**: *linkage* for McTigue and Rucker, brand identification for Milavsky.

**Where they diverge.** Percy and Rosenbaum-Elliott say that even the most brilliant execution that does not follow the strategy loses to an average one that does. Braun and Landa put more weight on appeal, timing and sharing. No grid replaces testing: McTigue and Rucker remind us that "We are not our customers", and Percy and Rosenbaum-Elliott ask for a pretest built on the communication objectives, never focus groups.

## Application

- Choose the grid by the moment: Percy's requirements or A.L.T.E.R. for concepts, Milavsky before production, ADPLAN for executions.
- Fix the criteria and the weights before the presentation, starting from the brief, so that you don't adjust them to your preferences.
- Keep a heading for strategic risk, so that a bold idea is not eliminated only because it is unusual.

## Example

The Quiznos spot in which a man pries his sandwich from his dog's mouth passes the strategy test, because it wanted to say "it's that good". It fails on amplification, though: consumers were disgusted. A grid that stops at "is it on strategy?" would not have stopped it.

## Questions

- Why does almost no grid have a separate criterion for emotion? Where does it go?
- Award juries judge by criteria other than effectiveness ([[HIS-007-advertising-awards-follow-two-philosophies-aesthetics-adc|HIS-007]]). What grid would you build for an ADC jury?
