# The influencer brief gives direction but leaves room for the creator's interpretation

> **The idea in one sentence:** a good influencer brief is precise about objectives, deliverables, deadlines, legal rules and approval, but flexible about execution, because the creator knows their audience better than the brand does.

## Explanation

Glenister proposes a brief in two parts:

- **Overview**: the company's products and services, the target audience, previous campaigns.
- **Campaign**: the main objective and what success looks like; the key message (hashtag, tagline); objectives and KPIs; channels; deliverables with dates and times (to sync with the rest of the campaign); the target audience; budget (including travel expenses); how results are reported; the content review process; payment and its terms.

Deliverables are described in concrete terms: the type of content (post, video, story), the deadline and the sign-off process, the guidelines for the content (possibly a **mood board** or a "look book" with the visual style wanted, for example the product with or without packaging), the caption messaging, the hashtags and the weblinks. The brief contains a list of **Dos and Don'ts** (for example, no profanity if the brand is averse to it) and the product's benefits, so the influencer can recraft the value proposition in their own tone of voice.

The counterpoint comes from Craig Knox (The Corner London): the key to influencer negotiations is **creative compromise**. The first question any influencer asks is what the creative brief is; they need to feel the brief is open to their own personal interpretation. A flexible brief secures partners better than a big budget. The creator Amber Doig-Thorne confirms it from the other side: what puts her off most is a brand that says "here's a script, you have no creative control, you do exactly as we want." It is the "fence in the yard" principle ([[BRF-002-the-brief-fences-in-the-yard-strategic-constraint-gives|BRF-002]]) applied to a partner who is both a channel and an author.

## Application

- Fix firmly: objective, KPIs, deliverables, dates, disclosure, approval, payment. Leave open: the idea, the tone, the exact format.
- Do not send press releases: give two or three key messages as bullet points.
- Do not ask the creator to repeat your message word for word; it will appear inauthentic.
- Keep the brief simple; brands often expect too much from a single campaign.

## Example

A model of deliverables from the book: four Instagram stories and two Instagram posts over two weeks; each story ends with the brand's call to action; the post includes the brand's hashtag and a brand mention; the campaign runs for five weeks; the post is legally marked (#ad); the content is approved before it goes live.
