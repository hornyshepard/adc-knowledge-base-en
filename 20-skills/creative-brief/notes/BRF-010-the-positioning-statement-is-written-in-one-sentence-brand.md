# The positioning statement is written in one sentence: "brand X is the brand for [target audience] in the market for [category need] that offers [benefit]"

> **The idea in one sentence:** a good positioning statement contains exactly three variables (the target audience, the category need, the key benefit) and so answers "what is it?" and "what does it offer?".

## Explanation

At the end of the chapter on positioning, Percy and Rosenbaum-Elliott ask that the strategy be summarized in a **positioning statement**. Its simplest form:

> *[Brand] is the brand for [target audience] in the market for [category need] that offers [benefit].*

Each variable has a step of work behind it:

| Variable | Where it comes from |
|---|---|
| Target audience | the buyer or loyalty group chosen in step 1, not a demographic profile |
| Category need | the definition of the market in the consumer's terms, at the right level of partitioning |
| Benefit | the one chosen with the expectancy-value model: important, deliverable, delivered better than by competitors |

The statement gives everyone who works on the brand (marketing, agency, media, promotions) a quick overview of the positioning. It is an internal tool, not a slogan.

**Compared with the Kellogg formula.** McTigue and Rucker ([[BRF-009-the-positioning-statement-in-the-brief-target-frame-of|BRF-009]]), like Tybout and Calkins, write the positioning in four pieces, with the reason to believe at the end. Percy and Rosenbaum-Elliott drop the proof and replace the frame of reference with the category need, defined in the consumer's terms. The difference is one of school: at Kellogg, the sentence answers why the target would choose the brand; for Percy, it answers "what is it?" (for brand awareness) and "what does it offer?" (for brand attitude) ([[ADV-020-the-general-model-of-positioning-asks-two-questions-what-is|ADV-020]]).

The authors give two examples:

- *Snickers*, if it chose to compete with Cadbury on indulgence: the brand for those who switch among several brands, including Snickers (*favorable brand switchers*), in the market for inexpensive chocolate bars, that you can indulge in any time.
- *Buffalo Trace*: the brand for whiskey drinkers looking for great bourbons and whiskeys, from the world's most award-winning distillery.

## Application

- Put the positioning statement on the first page of the creative brief, before the "proposition" or the insight.
- Test every creative idea: does the execution answer the category need and the benefit in the sentence?
- If you cannot fill in one of the variables without writing "everyone" or "quality", the strategy is not ready.

## Example

For Swapfiets (bikes on a subscription): the brand for urban commuters who want a bike with no hassle, in the market for daily transport by bike, that offers a bike that always works, repaired or replaced within 12 hours, at a fixed monthly price.

## Questions

- What do you gain and what do you lose compared with brief formats built on a "single-minded proposition"?
- What would the statement look like for a brand that addresses the trade and the consumer at the same time?
