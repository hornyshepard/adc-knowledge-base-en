# The creative brief is the blueprint for the execution: a road map for the creative work and a criterion for evaluating it

> **The idea in one sentence:** a creative brief does two things at once: it guides the team that creates the work, and it sets in advance the criteria by which the work will be judged.

## Explanation

McTigue and Rucker compare the brief to the blueprints of a house: you do not tell the builder to build you a house and that you will see them when it is finished; you agree with the architect on what you want, and when you see something amiss during construction, you go back to the blueprint. By *execution* the authors mean any creative output: an ad, a series of social media posts, an email, a logo, an experience. The brief is the client's document (sometimes cocreated with the agency) that sets out the objective, the target, the insight, the main message, the media and production details and how success will be measured.

For the client, the brief serves **two basic functions**: (1) an informative and ideally inspiring road map for the development team; (2) an aligned set of strategic criteria to evaluate the output. It also holds the client accountable: if you asked for the wrong thing, the creative can point out that this is exactly what you asked for.

For the agency, a sound brief reduces the churn or "swirl": the needless rounds that cost billable hours, burn people out and lead to tepid creative work of the kind the client will surely approve. Good agencies have two primary goals, to make money and to do great work, and a strong brief serves both. Percy and Rosenbaum-Elliott see the same role from the strategist's side: the brief is the bridge between the strategist's convergent thinking and the creative's divergent thinking, and work does not start until everyone has accepted it ([[BRF-006-the-creative-brief-fits-on-one-page-in-three-blocks-task|BRF-006]]).

The authors name two basic failures. The first: having no brief at all (a nonprofit at round four of logo development, with six lovely options and no written goal). The second: a brief slapped together in a rush, which gives a false sense of security and may do as much harm as no brief at all, or more.

## Application

- Before any project, even a small one, ask: is there a brief? For a single post, at least check the brief of the campaign it belongs to.
- In every feedback meeting, the question "what did we ask for?" comes before the question "do I like it?".
- Treat the brief as a road map that can be revised, not as a contract: if the agency finds a better road, change it explicitly and agree on the new version.

## Example

Marc Pritchard (P&G) observed that "advertising has a bad reputation as a content crap trap": thousands of posts that only add to the noise. The authors' conclusion: if a piece is not worth a brief, it may not be worth making at all.

## Questions

- "In digital we A/B test, we don't need a brief anymore": what do the authors answer? (A/B testing does not help if both options are bad.)
- Where does a brief that works as a road map end, and where does a brief that "writes the ad" begin?
