# The client brief asks the question, and the creative brief turns it into a problem for the creative team to solve

> **The idea in one sentence:** these are two different documents: the client brief (*brief annonceur*) is the starting point of the relationship and says what the client wants; the creative brief (*brief créatif*) is the agency's internal document that gives the creatives a short guiding thread (*fil conducteur*).

## Explanation

**The client brief**: the required sections according to *La boîte à outils de la publicité*:

1. the company, the product or service, its strengths and weaknesses, the brand's ecosystem;
2. the objectives: ideally both marketing and communication objectives;
3. the targets: the commercial target, but also the influence target (journalists, prescribers);
4. the competitive watch (*veille concurrentielle*), or benchmark: best practices and the competitors' choices;
5. the budget: a closed envelope (*enveloppe budgétaire fermée*) to be optimized;
6. the launch schedule;
7. the "expectations" (*attendus*): the client's constraints (no TV, a celebrity face, 30% of the budget on TV, etc.);
8. the KPIs: measurable indicators, for example +5 points on an image item in a post-test, or 50,000 units sold. They can be the basis of a variable fee for the agency.

As the authors put it: "le brief annonceur pose la question à laquelle l'agence va devoir répondre" [the client brief asks the question that the agency will have to answer].

The sections converge with Percy and Rosenbaum-Elliott's briefing document ([[BRF-004-the-briefing-document-comes-before-the-strategy-product|BRF-004]]): product, market, competition, objectives, budget. Barre and Gayrard-Carrera explicitly add the constraints and the KPIs, which is exactly what can later become a cause of dispute.

**The typical flow inside an agency** (the Publicis Conseil example): brief → 15 days → copy strat presented to the client → approval (immediately or within a week) → creative brief → 15 days of creative development → first creative feedback to the client and debrief → 8–15 days → approval of the overall plan (*dispositif global*). Between the stages: the *Plan's Board* (creative director, creatives, account people, planning) and creative check-ins (*points créatifs*).

**The creative brief** has these sections: brand, deadline, context, positioning, identity, values, competition, targets (profile, expectations, current and desired perception), copy strat (problem statement, objectives, promise, proof), planned plan of action (*dispositif*), constraints, budget, deliverables.

## Application

- When you receive a client brief, sort it: what you keep, what is missing, what you have to ask for. A 50-page brief can leave out the essential information. The full steps are in [[BRF-024-receiving-the-brief-you-reduce-it-to-its-essence-check-it|BRF-024]].
- A creative brief is good if the creatives do not have to restate the problem, the creative idea can be rolled out across all media, and the hierarchy has approved it.
- If the client does not know where it wants to go, the agency becomes the strategic pivot and has to go beyond the brief.

## Example

A client asks for "a 360° campaign, no press, with a strong presence on social media". That is a constraint on deliverables, not a strategy; the agency first has to find out the communication problem behind the request.

## Questions

- Who should write the creative brief: the account person, the planner or both?
- Is it healthy for the agency's fee to depend on KPIs? What risks appear?
