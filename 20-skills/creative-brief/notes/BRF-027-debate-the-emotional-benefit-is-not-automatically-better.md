# Debate: the emotional benefit is not automatically better than the functional one

> **The idea in one sentence:** Braun and Landa bring data showing that emotion drives attention, sharing and long-term growth; McTigue and Rucker warn that, for a given target, the functional benefit may be the most persuasive.

## Explanation

**The McTigue–Rucker position.** No rule says the emotional rungs of the benefit ladder are always better. What decides is what is most meaningful, different and authentic to the target. Google's "Loretta" spot (2020 Super Bowl) is very emotional, but it communicates very functional benefits. The owner of a premium-priced smoothie made from "ugly" produce wanted to talk about food waste, but the target wanted to know whether the smoothies tasted great and what varieties were available. Brands go up the ladder when they are forced to: when the target is already familiar with the features, or when competitors have copied them.

**The Braun–Landa position.** Emotions imprint stories in memory. The book cites WARC's *Health of Creativity* report (over half of the most award-winning campaigns of 2015–2018 focused on emotion), the 2019 analysis of Australian Effie winners (long-term market share growth comes more effectively from emotional advertising) and Jonah Berger's research: high-arousal emotions (amusement, anger, fear) drive sharing. Hyundai's "Operation Better" shows no car at all. Nike sneakers are made of the same materials as almost every other mainstream sneaker, but their value is emotional.

**Where they meet.** Landa's own examples (Lysol, Iceland) first fulfill a practical need (germ reduction, a vacation), then an emotional one (trust, reconnection). That is exactly climbing the ladder. The difference lies in the objective: Braun and Landa write about content that has to be shared and about brand building; McTigue and Rucker write about persuading a precise target, sometimes one that does not know the brand. And *purpose* is not automatically a valuable emotional benefit for the target (the smoothie case). The research cited by Fennis and Stroebe arrives at the McTigue and Rucker position: a rational or emotional appeal is a match with the product and the person, not an agency philosophy ([[CPY-030-a-rational-or-emotional-appeal-is-not-an-agency-philosophy|CPY-030]]). The dispute is old: Reeves and Ogilvy already fought it as the USP against brand image ([[ADV-027-the-reeves-ogilvy-dispute-usp-versus-brand-image-reason|ADV-027]]).

## Application

- Choose the rung by four variables: the brand's maturity, how well the target knows the product, the type of objective (launch or brand building) and how easily the competition copies.
- For new products, start functional and climb gradually; for mature brands in parity categories, the emotional benefit becomes the differentiator.
- Even in an emotional spot, check that the benefit stays linked to the brand (*linkage*, [[BRF-026-adplan-six-questions-for-an-execution-that-is-on-brief-but|BRF-026]]).

## Example

In "Loretta" (Google, 2020 Super Bowl), an older man dictates memories of his deceased wife to his Google Assistant. The spot is very emotional, but every scene shows a concrete function of the product; McTigue and Rucker cite it precisely as evidence that the rungs of the ladder do not exclude each other.

## Questions

- Do award databases (Cannes, Effie, WARC) favor emotional work? What does that mean for the Braun–Landa conclusions?
- Can a "purely functional" ad be shared widely? Which examples do you know?
