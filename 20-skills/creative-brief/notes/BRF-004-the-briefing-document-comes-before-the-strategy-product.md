# The briefing document comes before the strategy: product, market, source of business, competition and objectives, plus the persona and its journey in digital

> **The idea in one sentence:** before the creative brief, someone sums up in a briefing document what we sell, in which market, where sales will come from, who we compete with and what we want to achieve; in digital, the persona and its path to purchase are added.

## Explanation

Percy and Rosenbaum-Elliott ask that, before the five steps of strategic planning, the manager review the marketing plan and write a **briefing document** covering five areas (Table 5.1):

| Area | The question | What the authors ask for |
|---|---|---|
| Product description | What are you marketing? | a written description for someone totally unfamiliar with the product; what is obvious to the manager may not be obvious to the target market |
| Market assessment | What is your overall assessment of the market? | up-to-date information, only what affects performance: brand vs. category, innovations, new entrants |
| Source of business | Where do you expect business to come from? | new customers to the category, users of other brands (trial) or your own customers buying more (repeat purchase) |
| Competitive evaluation | What is your competition and how does it position itself? | the competitive set *in the minds of consumers*, with the competitors' ads and media tactics |
| Marketing objectives | What are the marketing objectives for the brand? | share or sales targets; the budget is calculated from them |

Schwarz-Musch, Tauchhammer and Guetz explicitly take over this structure for online campaigns and rearrange it into four blocks: the initial situation (the company, the market, its own and the competitors' communication), the advertising problem and objectives (economic and psychological, plus the central message and its justification), the target group, and the budget with the schedule. Their addition is digital: besides sociodemographic, psychographic and behavioral data, the target group is described as a **buyer persona** with its **customer journey**, and the objectives are written separately for each phase of the journey. They admit, however, that there is no single format and cite Davis: "There are as many methods of briefing as there are people."

The document anchors the strategy in business reality and gives the creatives the context that otherwise only the manager assumes.

## Application

- Write the "description for a stranger" even for ordinary products: it brings out the internal jargon and the assumed benefits.
- Attach the competitors' ads, so that the new execution can be different.
- Link the source of business to the action objective (trial or repeat purchase).
- In a digital brief, ask for the persona and the journey if they are missing, and for objectives by stage, not a single "we want sales".

## Example

A plant-based milk brand that writes "oat drink, lactose-free, with the texture of milk" finds that a stranger would understand "oat juice"; the corrected description also changes the benefit: it foams like milk in coffee. For an e-mountain bike, the same document would separate two personas, the first-time buyer and the user of a competing e-bike, with different objectives in the awareness phase: awareness of the category for the first, of the brand for the second.

## Questions

- Which part of the document is most often missing from the briefs agencies receive?
- What does this document not contain compared with a creative brief (insight, the central proposition, the tone)? Who should write the persona: the client or the agency?
