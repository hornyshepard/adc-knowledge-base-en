# The creative brief fits on one page, in three blocks (task definition, objectives and strategy, execution), and only the target audience and the benefit support are spelled out in detail

> **The idea in one sentence:** the strategist thinks convergently, the creative divergently; the brief is the bridge between them, and it has to be short precisely to show that the information has been thought through.

## Explanation

The authors start from O'Malley's distinction. The strategist does **convergent thinking**: from the data, they deduce the "hot button" most likely to influence behavior. The creative does **divergent thinking**: they move out from that point toward surprising and memorable ideas. The transfer happens in two steps.

1. **The briefing** (the presentation): the market, the consumer (including their dreams and desires, for transformational strategies), the product, the brand's positioning (radical departures erode *equity*), the competitors' advertising (so that the new execution can be different), the communication objectives and the media considerations (for example an explicit request for point-of-purchase material, direct mail, outdoor or radio). British agencies invest a lot in making the briefing engaging: the more unique facts it contains, the more likely it is to spark the *big idea*.
2. **The creative brief**, the one-page document (Fig. 14.1):

| Block | Sections |
|---|---|
| **Task definition** | key market observation · source of business (specific: current owners of competitors' luxury cars who are looking for something more exciting) · consumer barrier or insight · target audience (a vivid description) |
| **Objectives and strategy** | communication objectives and tasks · brand attitude strategy (the quadrant of the grid) · benefit claim and support (the most difficult and often contentious area: the benefit put in terms of the desired emotional response, the heart of the execution) · desired consumer response |
| **Execution** | creative guidelines (look and feel) · requirements and mandatory content (logo, legal identifications) |

As the authors put it: "The key to an effective creative brief is that it is brief." The brief must be **agreed upon by everyone** before creative work begins. It is needed for promotions too, not only for advertising. The authors are skeptical of overly restrictive *style books*, but ask that the tagline and the logo stay untouched once they are established.

## Application

- Do the page test: if the brief runs to three pages, the strategy has not been decided yet.
- Write the source of business and the insight as concrete images, not as market segments.
- Check that the grid quadrant in the brief is consistent with the benefit claim (informational → problem solved, transformational → emotion).

## Example

The book's brief for internet banking (Fig. 14.2). Observation: customers go into branches or use the telephone for business that could be done online. Source: current checking account customers. Insight: they are comfortable using the internet and are heavy users of ATMs. Target audience: loyal and vulnerable, young, middle-income, full-nest households with busy lives. Objective: reinforce the overall IMC convenience positioning. Strategy: informational, low involvement, incomplete satisfaction. Benefit: online banking is more convenient, you can pay bills almost any time. Desired response: recall, consider. Guideline: exaggeration in the execution.

## Questions

- Which section of this model is missing from "Get / To / By" briefs? And the other way around?
- Is it good for the brief to contain the grid quadrant, or does that fence in divergent thinking?
