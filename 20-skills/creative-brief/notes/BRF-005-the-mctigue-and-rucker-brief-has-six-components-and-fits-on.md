# The McTigue and Rucker brief has six components and fits on a single page

> **The idea in one sentence:** whatever the agency's form looks like, a solid brief answers six questions, in order, and does not go beyond one page.

## Explanation

The authors distilled the format from the hundreds of briefs they have seen at clients and agencies. Brands with good brief practices depart from this structure only in matters of taste, not of strategy.

| Component | The question it answers | Detailed note |
|---|---|---|
| Objective | What must the communication change, by how much and by when? | [[BRF-007-the-objective-in-the-brief-is-a-communication-objective\|BRF-007]] |
| Target | Who are we talking to, as a priority? | [[BRF-008-targeting-means-prioritization-three-typical-errors-and-the\|BRF-008]] |
| Insight | What human truth about the target makes the message relevant? | [[INS-092-an-insight-is-a-meaningful-human-truth-and-has-to-pass\|INS-092]] |
| Positioning | What main message must stay with them? | [[BRF-009-the-positioning-statement-in-the-brief-target-frame-of\|BRF-009]] |
| Execution | Personality, mandatories, timing and budget, media | [[BRF-013-the-execution-section-of-the-brief-has-four-pillars\|BRF-013]] |
| Measurement | How do we know whether it worked; what do we test and when? | [[BRF-014-measurement-is-written-into-the-brief-before-the-campaign\|BRF-014]] |

The components form a chain: the objective calls for a suitable target; insight is "the glue that binds the target and the brand position"; the execution delivers the positioning; measurement goes back to the objective.

The second principle is **parsimony**. The name even has "brief" in it, and a strong brief is almost always a single page. A ten-page document is usually a sign that the research has yet to be distilled. Strategy requires prioritization, and prioritization also helps the creatives focus. Still, the "less" has to contain all the critical ingredients. Percy and Rosenbaum-Elliott also arrive at one page, but in three blocks and with sections that McTigue and Rucker do not have, such as the source of business and the brand attitude strategy ([[BRF-006-the-creative-brief-fits-on-one-page-in-three-blocks-task|BRF-006]]).

## Application

- Use the six questions as a checklist for any agency form (see the comparison in [[BRF-021-agency-brief-models-share-the-same-core-but-each-one|BRF-021]]).
- In the first draft keep the labels (e.g., "Frame of Reference", "Reason to Believe"), so you can check that each element is there; you can remove them in the final version.
- If you do not have a real insight, leave the box empty: that is more honest than an invented one.
- The details (the wording of survey questions, the full media plan) go in attached documents, not in the brief.

## Example

The fictional company Stark Cloud Solutions goes through all six sections, from a "good" brief to a great one ([[BRF-015-the-stark-cloud-solutions-case-how-a-good-brief-becomes-a|BRF-015]]).

## Questions

- Many agency forms have no measurement section. Is measurement part of the creative brief or of the marketing plan?
- Which elements common in agencies (context, history, tone) are missing from this model, and where would you put them?
