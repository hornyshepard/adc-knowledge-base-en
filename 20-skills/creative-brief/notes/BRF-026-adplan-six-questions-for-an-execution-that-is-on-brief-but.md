# ADPLAN: six questions for an execution that is on brief but can still fail

> **The idea in one sentence:** being on brief does not mean the ad is effective; ADPLAN gives the client a vocabulary for the tactical failures of an execution that is strategically correct.

## Explanation

McTigue and Rucker found that many clients lack the words for what they feel is off, especially when an assistant brand manager has to give feedback to a prominent agency. Their framework has six elements, and most ad testing services map back to them.

| Letter | The question | The example in the book |
|---|---|---|
| **A**ttention | Does the ad draw and maintain interest to the end? (in testing: *breakthrough*) | Bernbach: if no one notices the ad, everything else is academic |
| **D**istinction | Is it different from the ads in the category? The von Restorff effect (1933): distinctiveness drives memorability | the dancing silhouettes for the iPod, identified as Apple before the logo |
| **P**ositioning | Does the benefit from the brief come through? (in testing: *main message takeaway*) | (none) |
| **L**inkage | Are both the brand and the benefit remembered, and linked to each other? | "I saw the Chobani advertisement; it had a bear.": the brand remembered, the benefit lost |
| **A**mplification | What does the audience actually feel and think? (in testing: *liking*) | Quiznos: the sandwich pried from the dog's mouth meant to say it is that good; consumers were disgusted |
| **N**et equity | Does it fit the brand's heritage? | BMW talked about safety through its handling, without diluting its equity as the ultimate driving machine |

The authors' test for *linkage*: if you can describe the ad to someone without mentioning the brand, you have a problem. Negative amplification often comes from sensitive content (sexism, racism, negative emotions, controversial spokespeople). The remedy they propose: diversity in your teams, in the agency's teams and in testing.

Three of the letters (attention, amplification, linkage) also appear in Sternthal, their Kellogg colleague, as a three-step creative strategy ([[ADV-043-an-effective-ad-does-three-things-gains-attention-amplifies|ADV-043]]). McTigue and Rucker add distinction, positioning and net equity, which is everything that ties the execution to the brief and to the brand's history.

## Application

- Turn vague comments into ADPLAN questions: ask whether the ad will be distinct versus the competitive ads in the category, instead of saying you do not like it.
- Use the framework to choose what to test: only the letters where doubt remains after discussion.
- For creatives: run your own idea through ADPLAN before the presentation.

## Example

The first Stark ad in the book is correct on P (the message from the brief) and on the brand color, but weak on A and D: it does not break through the clutter of cloud storage ads. The feedback is framed as a question on these letters, not as a list of changes ([[BRF-025-creative-feedback-is-organized-in-four-categories-things-i|BRF-025]]).

## Questions

- Does ADPLAN remove the need for testing? The authors say no: we are not our customers.
- In which order would you prioritize the letters for a launch spot? And for the fifth execution in a long campaign?
