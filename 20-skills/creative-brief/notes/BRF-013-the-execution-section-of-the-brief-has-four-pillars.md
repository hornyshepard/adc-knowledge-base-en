# The execution section of the brief has four pillars: personality, mandatories, timing and budget, media

> **The idea in one sentence:** the tactical details decided in the brief (tone, mandatories, deadline, money, channels) prevent needless rounds, but too many "mandatories" end up writing the ad instead of the creatives.

## Explanation

McTigue and Rucker start from an analogy: you are the executive behind a new *Star Wars* film. The script is ready, but you have to decide the tone, the fixed rules (the scrolling text prologue at the start, no swearing), the budget (250 million dollars, not 15) and the release windows. The execution section of the brief looks the same:

| Pillar | What it contains | Examples from the book |
|---|---|---|
| Personality | the way the brand comes across in its communications: serious, lighthearted, empathetic | Canada Goose sends serious emails, Bombas humorous ones; the tone adapts to the context (COVID, the Super Bowl) without the brand seeming "replaced" |
| Mandatories | real requirements: brand elements, legal issues, corporate policies | Jif always showed peanuts transforming into peanut butter; legal restrictions on supplements; policies on diverse casting or comparative advertising |
| Timing and budget | the launch date, from which the calendar is worked out backward, including testing and approvals; a reasonable budget shared with the agency | if you have 50,000 dollars, you do not want 5-million-dollar ideas |
| Media | the likely channels, and so the formats: portrait or landscape, fifteen seconds, podcast, poster | what looked good on a poster in the conference room may not work on a phone screen |

The authors name the common mistakes: not asking "where else might I use this?" (worldwide usage rights, format adaptations, translations); unrealistic timelines, in which creative development time is cut first; approvals that drag on; and **mandatories that write the ad**, as the sum of the creative visions of several people on the client team. In their words: "If you have a creative genius on staff, fire the agency and do it yourself." Percy and Rosenbaum-Elliott draw the same line: the logo and the tagline stay untouched once established, but overly restrictive style books stifle the execution ([[BRF-006-the-creative-brief-fits-on-one-page-in-three-blocks-task|BRF-006]]).

## Application

- For each mandatory, ask: is it actually mandatory, or is it a preference?
- Ask from the start for two or three sample formats (for example a fifteen-second video, a podcast, an in-store poster) to see how wide the idea is.
- Map from the start who approves what (does the CEO approve the casting too?).

## Example

"Got Milk?" was translated in Mexico, according to the authors, as "Are you lactating?". The lesson: think from the start about the markets and languages in which the material will circulate.

## Questions

- Media before the idea, or the idea before the media? The authors say it depends: if you know the target is on Instagram, you do not want a Super Bowl spot.
- Cutting the "nonworking" budget (research, production, agency) assumes that all ads are equal. How would you calculate the gain from creative work that is 5% better?
