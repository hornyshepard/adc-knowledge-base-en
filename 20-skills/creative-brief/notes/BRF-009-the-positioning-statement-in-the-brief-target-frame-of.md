# The positioning statement in the brief: target, frame of reference, point of difference, reason to believe

> **The idea in one sentence:** positioning is the main message that you want the target to take away, and its statement answers a single question: why would they choose me over the competition?

## Explanation

McTigue and Rucker use a sentence with four pieces: *To [target], [brand] is the [frame of reference] that [point of difference] because [reason to believe].* Their example, an invented coffee shop: to coffee patrons with discriminating tastes, Rutigue is the upscale coffee shop that offers a customized experience, because it has thirty unique beans ground right before your eyes.

| Piece | Role | Example from the book |
|---|---|---|
| Target | the relevant benefit differs from one target to another | none |
| Frame of reference | the competitive set: what does the target use if not us? It is **malleable** | Hyundai launched the Genesis as a "luxury sedan", not as a "family sedan" |
| Point of difference | the key benefit, derived from the insight | Dove "owns" moisturizing in people's minds |
| Reason to believe | the evidence for the benefit | Cheer: a side-by-side comparison of a shirt washed with and without color guard |

The frame of reference matters more than it seems. A single word ("luxury") gives the Genesis the associations of the category (a comfortable luxury interior, advanced driving capabilities), a path to growth without cannibalization, and a new advantage: a price 30 percent below similar models, which would not impress against mainstream cars. One benefit can have several reasons to believe, one per commercial (Green Giant: vegetables handpicked, vacuum sealed, frozen while fresh).

Once the positioning is written, everything in the campaign serves it: the celebrity spokesperson, the event, the new social media format. The authors reject manipulation: you do not claim a benefit you do not offer, but "we are owning some truth in consumers' minds" through consistency.

**Other formulas.** Tybout and Calkins, their colleagues at Kellogg, use the same four-piece sentence ([[BRD-006-the-positioning-statement-has-four-elements-target-frame-of|BRD-006]]). Percy and Rosenbaum-Elliott write a three-variable version, without the reason to believe, in which the frame of reference becomes the "category need" ([[BRF-010-the-positioning-statement-is-written-in-one-sentence-brand|BRF-010]]). All of them are internal tools, not slogans.

## Application

- Write the first draft with the labels in parentheses and check each piece.
- Test the frame of reference: what does the target actually compare us with? If the frame changes, the benefit that matters changes too.
- When you evaluate the creative work, ask what main message was taken away (see [[BRF-026-adplan-six-questions-for-an-execution-that-is-on-brief-but|BRF-026]]).

## Example

Stark no longer positions itself as "the most secure cloud" but against all data storage, including the client's own servers ([[BRF-015-the-stark-cloud-solutions-case-how-a-good-brief-becomes-a|BRF-015]]).

## Questions

- Differentiation or just awareness? The authors keep the need for a reason to choose; otherwise price becomes the tiebreaker.
- How long does a positioning last? The Masterbrand ideally eight or more years, the campaign three or more years, the executions until wear-out. The authors' rule: change the campaign when you need to, not when you have grown tired of it.
