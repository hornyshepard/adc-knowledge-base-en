# Measurement is written into the brief before the campaign, and testing is justified by uncertainty and stakes

> **The idea in one sentence:** if you decide what to measure only after the launch, you have no point of comparison and you will look for any figure that looks good.

## Explanation

McTigue and Rucker open the chapter with the warship *Vasa* (1628), which sank on its maiden voyage. One explanation: the shipbuilders used rulers calibrated in two different units (Swedish feet and Amsterdam feet). Measurement serves the objective: if you want to change the perception of quality and you measure likes, you learn nothing. The most egregious error is a measurement strategy put together after the campaign. There is then no baseline and no control group, and the team slides into *confirmation bias*: "Sales are not up? How about our YouTube likes?"

| Method | What it is good for | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| Focus group | uncovers what you did not know, explains the "why" of a quantitative test | not for "voting" on the ad; the moderator can lead |
| Copy test | a survey before and after exposure, or exposed vs. not exposed; verbatims explain the reactions | rough stimuli (storyboard, animatic) |
| Market test | A/B in the market, a regional test, budget variations | expensive, later in the process |
| Attribution / marketing mix modeling | advertising's contribution to sales | years of data; digital attribution overstates the last touch (for example a Google search) |

**When testing is worth it:** when you have creative concepts that seem equally viable (a celebrity or an everyday consumer for a bomber jacket), when you launch a new campaign or brand, when you do not know how the audience will respond (for example to an execution that pushes the envelope on traditional gender roles) and when the stakes are huge (the Super Bowl). A campaign that has run for years, like Absolut, needs testing less.

**Where the authors disagree.** Percy and Rosenbaum-Elliott are stricter: the pretest is built on the communication objectives and is never done through focus groups or ad recall ([[ADV-079-the-pretest-is-built-on-the-communication-objectives-with|ADV-079]]). McTigue and Rucker accept the focus group, but only to explain the "why", not to vote; the literature on predicting choice draws the same limit ([[INS-077-the-focus-group-is-good-for-language-and-for-detecting|INS-077]]).

## Application

- In the brief: which indicator, which threshold, which test before launch, which test in the market.
- Share the measurement strategy with the agency. One client measured cleaning power among male and female consumers and reported 44% instead of 50%, although the product and the ad were aimed at women; among female consumers, purchase intention had risen to almost 60%.
- Do not measure everything: many questions "bleed" into one another (soft → "good" → "high in quality").

## Example

Orbit had an animatic that tested terribly, yet the campaign took the brand to number one in the category. The results were weighed, not applied mechanically.

## Questions

- Does testing guarantee success? The authors: no. The stimuli are imperfect, and a moderator who "tells the story" of the idea can produce false applause.
- "It is too late to test, we have already produced it": why is that a myth?
