# Targeting means prioritization: three typical errors and the Own–Attract–Accept bull's-eye

> **The idea in one sentence:** the target in the brief has to be big enough for the objective and as narrow as possible for the message and the media; otherwise you have chosen nothing.

## Explanation

Segmentation is a map: it divides the population into groups that are mutually exclusive and, as far as possible, cover everyone, ideally by needs ("needs-based" segmentation). Because needs are not easily observable, we use proxies: geographic, demographic, behavioral, psychographic. Targeting means choosing the regions of the map you have the resources for, just as even with an entire year and a million dollars you cannot see the entire world. The desired target is the one that gets you most easily to the communication objective. The target guides both the message (it must be watched, understood and change something) and the media buying.

The authors name **three errors**:

| Error | What it looks like | Example from the book |
|---|---|---|
| The "everyone target" | no choice, or five groups with nothing in common | the B2B brand whose target was everyone in the industry |
| The "popular kid target" | the largest segment or the biggest spender, and so also the most contested | SpotHero chose business parkers, not occasional leisure parkers; Old Spice moved from "smell attractive to women" (Axe's territory) to "feel confident" |
| The "egocentric target" | you judge the ad through your own eyes, not the target's | for a confections product aimed at teen boys, the agency first showed the content the target was watching |

For multiple targets, the authors propose the **bull's-eye**: at the center **Own** (the primary target, with the most resources), then **Attract** (secondary targets, pursued tactically and at a lower cost), then **Accept** (those who buy anyway, without special attention). For any given ad, the greatest chance of success still comes from a singular target.

Percy and Rosenbaum-Elliott choose the target from another angle: not by needs or demographics but by the relationship with the brand, from five buyer groups, ranging from new category users to brand loyals ([[ADV-015-the-target-audience-is-chosen-from-five-buyer-groups-and|ADV-015]]). Demographics, they say, describe the market, not the communication's audience ([[MKT-016-demographics-define-the-target-market-but-the-target|MKT-016]]).

## Application

- Add to the target an observable "trigger" that makes it ready to buy (for Stark: servers that are five years old).
- Before you evaluate an idea, look at the target's media diet, not yours.
- Put micro-targeting to the cost test: a retailer tested twenty-some variations, and only two segments (male and female) proved worthwhile.

## Example

SpotHero (a parking app): the largest segment with the lowest cost per acquisition, occasional leisure parkers, was crowded with new entrants who all competed on price. The firm chose the second most attractive segment, the underserved business parker, and five years later it had a leading market position.

## Questions

- The authors say: "Don't be okay for everyone, be great for some." How does that square with the argument that brands grow through broad reach?
- When does it still make sense to go after the "popular kid"? (When you are the category leader or have a unique insight about them.)
