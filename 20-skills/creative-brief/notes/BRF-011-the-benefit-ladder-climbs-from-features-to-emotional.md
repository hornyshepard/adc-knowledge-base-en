# The benefit ladder climbs from features to emotional benefits, and the chosen benefit has to pass four tests

> **The idea in one sentence:** by asking "so what?" you climb from facts about the product to functional and emotional benefits; you choose the rung that is valuable, yours, authentic and lasting.

## Explanation

McTigue and Rucker ask that the point of difference pass **four tests**:

1. it is valued by the target, that is, tethered to an insight;
2. it is ownable by the brand, not yet strongly associated with any competitor;
3. it is authentic and accurate;
4. it has staying power, to build the brand for years to come.

Benefits are **functional** (what the product does for you: North Face makes cold weather outerwear, BMW provides power and exceptional handling) or **emotional** (what you feel: North Face allows outdoor enthusiasts to excel at their passion, BMW allows drivers to feel empowered). The **benefit ladder** links them: at the bottom are the features (true, observable facts), and with each "so what?" you climb one rung.

| Rung | The book's example: a childhood education nonprofit |
|---|---|
| Features | six locations, five PhD counselors on staff, bilingual |
| Functional benefit | children learn to read 30 percent faster |
| Emotional benefit | children have greater confidence in school |
| Higher-order benefit | a better chance at a happy, successful life: "from six locations to the promise of a better future" |

The ladder is useful because the brand needs content in many places: a commercial can work on a high rung, the website on the lower rungs, and the social feed can highlight each in turn. Brands climb the ladder for two reasons: the audience comes to know the features and functional benefits better and better, and the lower rungs are the easiest to copy, so they become part of the category's frame of reference.

Milavsky uses the same "so what?" question and adds a rule: you drop from the start the benefits that the whole category has ([[ADV-022-from-attribute-to-end-benefit-and-end-end-benefit-a-full|ADV-022]]). Percy and Rosenbaum-Elliott choose the benefit differently, with the expectancy-value model: what matters to the target and who delivers it better ([[BRD-011-the-positioning-benefit-is-chosen-with-the-expectancy-value|BRD-011]]).

## Application

- Build the ladder in a workshop, with the team: list the features, then ask "so what?" for each one.
- For each rung, score the brand's desired and actual performance; you test only where ambiguity remains.
- Choose the rung by the four tests, not by a preference for the "emotional".

## Example

Apple is known for highly emotional brand ads, but when it launches a new version of a device, its ads are functional, because the new features are not yet known.

## Questions

- Which rung do you choose for a new brand, in a category the target does not know?
- Can an emotional benefit be "owned" without real functional support?
