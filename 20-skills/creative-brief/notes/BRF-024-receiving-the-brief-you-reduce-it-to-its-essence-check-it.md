# Receiving the brief: you reduce it to its essence, check it component by component, ask the naive questions and agree any change in writing

> **The idea in one sentence:** a brief is not received passively but unpicked: the team reduces it to its essence, checks what is missing, asks the questions that seem foolish and turns any lack of clarity into a new, agreed version before the first idea.

## Explanation

The sources write from different angles (the client, the agency, the creative), but they describe the same move.

| Step | What you do | Who says it |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Clarify the deliverables | what exactly you are asked for, by when, in which formats | Ingledew, stage 1 |
| 2. Reduce to the essence | read, reread and rewrite the problem in a few words; an art director cut a ten-page brief down to 15 words | Ingledew, stage 2 |
| 3. Triage | what you keep, what is missing, what you ask back for; a fifty-page brief can leave out the key information, a three-page one forces you into research of your own | Barre and Gayrard-Carrera |
| 4. Ask the naive questions | any question, "however stupid or innocent they may seem"; the answer could turn the brief on its head | Ingledew, stage 1 |
| 5. Question the problem | the brief could be skimpy or poorly conceived; first seek the insight and check whether the problem is framed correctly | Braun and Landa |
| 6. Agree the changes | the brief is a road map, not a contract: if you find a better road, you rewrite it and approve it again | McTigue and Rucker |

The ritual continues in the presentation meeting: in McTigue and Rucker, the account person reads through the brief at the start, and the client listens for whether it is rendered faithfully. The Publicis process described by Barre and Gayrard-Carrera places internal creative check-ins (*points créatifs*) between the brief and the first presentation, then a creative debrief (*débrief créatif*) after the first proposals.

The sources differ in emphasis. For McTigue and Rucker, the brief is the client's responsibility, and the agency proposes changes. Ingledew and Braun & Landa start from the premise that the brief is often too long or too vague, so restating it is itself the first piece of creative work.

## Application

- Make a sheet: the problem in 15 words, the deliverables, the deadline, the six components ticked off ([[BRF-005-the-mctigue-and-rucker-brief-has-six-components-and-fits-on|BRF-005]]) and the questions for the client.
- Send the questions in a single message after the first reading, not piece by piece.
- If your restatement changes the objective or the target, ask for a new, approved version of the brief.

## Example

Kenneth Grange, asked to design British Rail's new intercity train, asked what the buffers on a locomotive were for. He learned they were an unnecessary hangover from a bygone age and could design a streamlined train: one naive question changed the brief.

## Questions

- Where does legitimate restating of the brief end and ignoring it begin?
- What do you do when the client refuses to revise a clearly weak brief?
