# Ten red flags in a brief: from an objective that is not falsifiable to measurement decided after the campaign

> **The idea in one sentence:** the most dangerous brief is one that "seems okay" on the surface; ten quick checks show where it will cause pointless rounds and tepid work.

## Explanation

McTigue and Rucker end each chapter with a section on why brands fail. Together they form a checklist for any brief form, before you send it or after you receive it.

| # | Red flag | Why it is serious |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | There is no brief, or it was slapped together in a rush | with no finish line you cannot judge any option; a weak brief gives a false sense of security |
| 2 | The objective is not falsifiable ("grow the brand image") | no one can be caught failing, and the creatives insert their own objective (awards, entertainment) |
| 3 | The objective asks advertising for something it cannot do | good ads kill a bad product faster |
| 4 | The target is "everyone", or five groups with nothing in common | the message and the media money are diluted |
| 5 | The "insight" is a fact or an opinion about the category | nothing links the target to the positioning; leaving the field blank is more honest |
| 6 | Five "equally important" benefits | the agency guesses with a 20 percent chance, or crams them all in |
| 7 | The frame of reference is missing | you do not know who the target compares you with, so you do not know which benefit matters |
| 8 | The mandatories "write the ad" | the sum of the preferences in the client team takes the place of the idea |
| 9 | A timeline with no time for creative work and no named approvers | creative development is cut first; approvals drag on |
| 10 | Measurement is decided after the campaign | there is no baseline, and the temptation is to look for any number that looks good |

Other sources add tests of form. Percy and Rosenbaum-Elliott: the brief fits on one page, and work starts only when everyone involved agrees to it. Barre and Gayrard-Carrera: length guarantees nothing: a fifty-page brief can still miss the key piece of information. McTigue and Rucker want the brief approved by the leaders who will judge the work; otherwise the execution may be rejected after the resources are spent.

## Application

- Score each row 0 or 1; at three flags or more, the brief goes back to its author before any creative work.
- In a seminar, students assess the same brief alone, then compare results.
- For short briefs (Get/To/By, social), check at least rows 2, 4, 5 and 6.

## Example

The first Stark brief in the book fails at least four checks: a vague goal, an "insight" that is only an opinion on the category, a positioning built on "the greatest functionality", and five benefits ([[BRF-015-the-stark-cloud-solutions-case-how-a-good-brief-becomes-a|BRF-015]]).

- Which of the ten flags is hardest for the brief's author to see? Why?
