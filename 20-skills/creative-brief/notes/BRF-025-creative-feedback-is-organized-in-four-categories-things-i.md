# Creative feedback is organized in four categories: things I love, things I like, questions, red flags

> **The idea in one sentence:** you lead with what works so you protect it, and you frame the problems as goals or questions, not as dictated solutions.

## Explanation

McTigue and Rucker describe a typical meeting. The agency has had a month, the idea is on brief, but the client kind of hates everything else and says what they feel: the picture does not work, the font is hard to read, they are disappointed the agency is not farther along. The reaction is not wrong, but the way it is shared leads to more development time and erodes the relationship. The client forgets that what they see has already survived the agency's internal filters and that the creatives are excited about the idea.

**Before the meeting**, the client team decides how it will give feedback: on the spot, or after a quick private recess? Who goes first? **In the meeting**, someone from the agency (usually the account person) reads through the brief; you listen to make sure it is represented faithfully. Then you listen through the ears of the target, with the insight in mind; a smile is not a final approval; you can ask to hear it again. Your notes are sorted into four categories:

| Category | What goes in |
|---|---|
| Things I love | what makes the idea exceptional: the insight demonstrated perfectly, the brand made unmistakable |
| Things I like | what is sound and on brief without wowing you |
| Questions | where you do not see how the execution meets a goal; choices you do not understand |
| Red flags | major deviations from the brief (the insight lost), legal or ethical concerns, the risk of offending |

**The delivery:** lead with the positives, not to be nice but so that what works is not lost when the changes are made. Frame the negatives as the concern, not as the solution. Do not dictate a new color scheme, better actors, bolder text and a puppy; name the goal the execution does not seem to serve. Be honest, and close with a recap and clear priorities: more emphasis on one thing means less emphasis on another.

Milavsky, from the agency side, gives almost identical rules: you hear all the options before commenting, you ask for the team's recommendation and the reason for it, and you phrase comments as questions, not rewrites ([[CPY-024-an-ad-idea-is-checked-on-paper-before-production-brand-id|CPY-024]]).

## Application

- Make a four-column table and fill it in during the presentation.
- If you are junior and speak first, say little: talk about the idea, not the location or the casting.
- Use the ADPLAN vocabulary ([[BRF-026-adplan-six-questions-for-an-execution-that-is-on-brief-but|BRF-026]]) to turn "I don't like it" into a question about effect.

## Example

The same reaction to the first Stark ad, in two forms. Spontaneous: "This picture doesn't seem appropriate. The font is also hard to read." followed by disappointment that the agency is not farther along. With the method: you say the brand is visible from the first second and you want to keep that (a like); then you say one of the goals is to break through the clutter of other cloud storage ads and ask how this imagery serves that goal (a question). The second form protects what works and leaves the solution to the creatives.

## Questions

- How do you adapt the method when you are the creative receiving the feedback?
- What do you do when the client's feedback contradicts the brief they signed off?
