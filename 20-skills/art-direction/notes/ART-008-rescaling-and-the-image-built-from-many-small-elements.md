# Rescaling and the image built from many small elements change the expected order of the world

> **The idea in one sentence:** enlarge or shrink the object that matters most to the message, or build an image from hundreds of pieces connected to it, and the viewer stops.

## Explanation

**Rescaling.** Ingledew starts from Gulliver and Alice: worlds in which familiar objects are huge or tiny grip our imagination. Claes Oldenburg enlarges tools and fast food (a 12.5-meter trowel). Jeff Koons made a 13-meter puppy out of flowers. Famous photographers seem to have used tiny cars and coffee cups. A tiny portfolio stood out at a student graduation show precisely because of its size. A UK billboard against gun crime enlarged the gun in proportion to a mobile phone, and the difference in scale creates an unsettling tension. The rule ("DIY"): accentuate the scale of the objects most vital to your message.

**Building a bigger picture.** An image built from a multitude of small elements is hugely arresting. Arthur S. Mole "drew" the Liberty Bell with thousands of soldiers. Ruth Adams built the all-seeing eye on a cover of *1984* from portraits of politicians. The caricaturist Voltz made Napoleon's face from the bodies of those killed in his wars. David Gentleman's placards with a single word, "NO", formed a "forest" at the 2003 march against the war.

The mechanism ("Brainjack"): the awe grows with the number of pieces, and the power comes from the tension between the pieces and the final image, especially when the relationship between them is revealing.

## Application

- Ingledew: find a relationship between two elements of the brief; build an image that shows one of them, using the other as building blocks.
- When rescaling, choose a single exaggerated object; if everything is distorted, there is no norm left to compare against.
- Check both reading levels: the big image from a distance, the pieces up close (and on a phone screen).

## Example

For *Penguin by Design*, a book about the publisher's iconic covers, David Pearson built the cover from shelfloads of Penguin books. From a distance you see a cover; up close you see the very subject of the book. The piece and the whole say the same thing, so the image becomes an argument.
