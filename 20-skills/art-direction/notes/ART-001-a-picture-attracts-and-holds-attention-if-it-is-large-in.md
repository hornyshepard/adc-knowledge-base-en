# A picture attracts and holds attention if it is large, in color, concrete, shows the product in use and is seen before the words

> **The idea in one sentence:** the average time spent looking at a magazine ad is about 1.65 seconds, and 70% of that time is spent with the picture; the picture decides whether the message gets processed.

## Explanation

**The creative unit** (the size of the ad, the length of the commercial, the screen area) accounts for as much as half of the variation in attention, independent of the message. The book's rough attention probabilities:

| Unit | Attention probability |
|---|---|
| 30-second TV commercial | ~0.65 (a 60-second one adds only about 20%; a 15-second one gets ~80% of a 30-second one) |
| Newspaper, one page black and white / color | 0.43 / 0.64 |
| Magazine, one page color, general / special interest | 0.49 / 0.64 |
| Banner / pop-up / skyscraper / leaderboard | 0.30 / 0.40 / 0.28 / 0.19 |

The visual tactics (Table 13.3–13.4):

- **Size**: recognition increases roughly with the square root of the size of the picture (four times larger, twice the attention). The exception is long-copy direct response.
- **Color**: color > two colors > black and white. Its main effect is motivational, so black and white is not used with positive motives.
- **Editing**: 20 or more cuts per 30-second commercial lower attention by about 17% (25% among 18- to 34-year-olds), but raise arousal, which is useful in transformational strategies. Pictures held for at least two seconds are remembered better.
- **The product in use or with the user** helps associative learning.
- **Concrete, high-imagery pictures**: they are coded twice, as an image and as a verbal label.
- **Viewer included or observer**: the picture invites you in, or leaves you outside wishing you were there; the choice has to be deliberate.
- **Pictures seen before words** increase learning: the dominant picture draws the eye first, wherever the headline is.
- **Subtle visual games** get lost, because top-down processing "corrects" the picture (you do not notice that the table in the Degas painting has no legs).

## Application

- In a layout, decide first on the single focal point, then on the short headline that pulls the eye toward the copy.
- In a storyboard, check the length of the key shots (at least two seconds) and the number of cuts against the strategy.
- Test the commercial with the sound off: what do the pictures alone communicate?

## Example

A print ad for a washing machine (informational, high involvement) can use a single large picture with the huge door open and a five-word headline. One for ice cream (transformational) has to be in color, large and show someone enjoying it with a real smile.

## Questions

- The book's attention probabilities come from older research. How would you update them for vertical mobile formats?
- Does the "fewer cuts" rule contradict the TikTok aesthetic? Where would it be acceptable?
