# The Tropicana case: the packaging redesign erased the recognition cues

> **The idea in one sentence:** in routine categories people do not read the package, they recognize it by a few visual cues; when Tropicana replaced them with a "more contemporary" design, sales fell about 20 percent within six weeks.

## Explanation

Brian Sternthal describes three ways in which consumers evaluate brands: on the basis of the message content, on the basis of their feelings about how easily they reached a judgment, and through **perceptual processing**. The last one dominates when brand choice is habitual or involvement is low: people rely on a few perceptual cues (color, visuals, shape) with minimal processing of the message and no retrieval of prior knowledge.

Tropicana was bought in exactly this way. The old package had stable visual cues, recognizable from a distance. The brand changed the packaging to make it more contemporary, and sales fell about 20 percent within six weeks. The mechanism was twofold: some consumers did not recognize the product in brand advertising or in-store; others saw it and felt it was not the product they were familiar with, which reduced their desire to buy it.

The case ties directly to Bobby Calder's argument (ch. 7): brand design works in System 1, and its value lies in the cues that trigger associations automatically. A redesign judged only on aesthetics ("it looks better") can destroy precisely the equity built up in those cues. Percy and Rosenbaum-Elliott (SRC-049) cite the same case, with the same drop of 20 percent in less than two months and a fast return to the old package, as proof that managers underestimate packaging as a communication medium.

## Application

- Before any redesign, identify which visual elements carry recognition (color, symbol, shape, image) and treat them as assets to keep or to evolve gradually.
- Test the new package in a realistic shelf context, with a short exposure time, not only in focus groups that analyze it at leisure.
- State the goal of the redesign in terms of associations, not "modernization".

## Example

The contrast in the same book: in 2016 Uber changed the way its name is rendered (blockier, more ordinary letters) to cue associations of everyday utility, but kept the name, which had too much identity equity to change.

## Questions

- When is a radical redesign justified, despite the Tropicana risk?
- How would you convince a client that "more beautiful" does not mean "more effective"?
