# The advertising layout was invented under constraints: Franklin's white space and the Age of Repetition

> **The idea in one sentence:** the basic elements of the advertising layout (the large headline, white space, the border, the small icon, repetition) were invented by advertisers and publishers working against technical limits and the rules newspapers imposed.

## Explanation

Richards describes the first newspaper ads (17th to 18th centuries) as blocks of text set in the same font as the news around them or smaller, with no headlines and no pictures. Every visual innovation is a small revolt:

- **Benjamin Franklin (Pennsylvania Gazette, 1729).** He separated the ads with several lines of white space, introduced a 14-point heading (first as the first line, later shortened and centered), ornamental borders and typographic icons: a horse for horses for sale, a book for booksellers.
- **Breaking the column.** In 1743, a half-page ad that cut across two columns was "well ahead of its time". In 1760, the *New York Gazette* ran advertisements across its entire front page.
- **Publishers' bans.** James Gordon Bennett (*New York Herald*, 1840s) banned illustrations and two-column ads, so that no advertiser had an advantage in drawing attention. Ads could not appear for longer than two weeks, and later not for more than one day.
- **The "Age of Repetition" (1850s to 1860s).** Advertisers worked around the rules by running the same ad multiple times on a page, creating large letters out of multiple small letters or printing the company name over and over on successive lines. 12-point type could have an effect similar to 60-point type.

Technology gradually freed the forms: lithography (1796), chromolithography (1837), the halftone process for photographs (1873), linotype (1886). By 1900 it was possible to print fast, in huge runs, in color and with photographs. Only then does art direction become a profession: art departments at Mather & Crowther and Bates (1890s), the first art director at Ayer (1898).

## Application

- Treat the constraint (small format, no image, text only, 6 seconds) as a creative brief, not as an excuse.
- White space is the oldest weapon of differentiation in a crowded medium.
- Visual repetition of one element (name, shape, color) can make up for a lack of size.

## Example

The 1856 ads for the *New York Ledger* were repeated so often that they filled entire pages in other newspapers, an early form of "dominating" the medium.

## Questions

- What rules do the platforms impose today (text-to-image ratio, formats, safe zones), and what "tricks" have appeared to work around them?
- How much of an era's style comes from aesthetics, and how much from print or screen technology?
