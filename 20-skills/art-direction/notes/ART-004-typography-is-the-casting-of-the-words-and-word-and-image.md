# Typography is the casting of the words, and word and image have to be in tension, not repeat each other

> **The idea in one sentence:** a typeface is not neutral packaging but a character that adds meaning; and copy and image communicate best when they collide like two live cables.

## Explanation

**Type.** Ingledew points out that letters are known as *characters*, as if they had personalities. Bo Bergstrom describes them as pompous, hesitant, cheeky, vulgar, but also open, lucid, elegant. Hence **typecasting**: you choose a typeface the way you cast an actor in a role. Some typefaces are almost autobiographical (Baskerville, Bodoni and Gill carry their creators' names). The author's advice: experiment with typography that complements the message and with typography that clashes with it. His remark: if type is "the clothing words wear", Futura is the Little Black Dress.

**Phototypography.** Letters formed from objects, people or space: the sky alphabet (Lisa Rienermann saw a Q between the houses of a Barcelona courtyard, then went on to find the whole alphabet), an alphabet of paper clips, the 1940 *Vogue* cover with letters formed by a model's body. The tension between what you say and what you write it with produces the meaning.

**The tension between words and images.** For Clive Challis, the picture and the words are two live electric cables: the viewer grasps both, and sparks fly in their head. Agencies reproduce the relationship in the creative team: a visual person coupled with a words person.

## Application

- The basic rule: do not say in the copy what the picture already shows. The copy twists, contradicts or completes.
- Set the same headline in three typefaces and ask: which "actor" delivers the line most convincingly?
- Classify the text–image relationship in the sketch: redundant (weak), complementary (good), in productive contradiction (often the best).
- Typefaces are part of the brand's signals: the choice has to pass the consistency test (see [[ART-019-the-one-wall-test-all-the-materials-of-a-brand-must-appear|ART-019]]).

## Example

The HSBC series shows the same photograph three times, each time overprinted with a single different word. The word completely changes how the image is read, and viewers are challenged to choose the interpretation themselves, as in a juxtaposition.

## Questions

- What happens to typecasting in identity systems where the brand has a single proprietary typeface?
- How does the word–image tension move into video, where the text is often a voice?
