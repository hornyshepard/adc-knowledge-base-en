# The geometric DNA of the brand mark extends into patterns, icons and letterforms

> **The idea in one sentence:** when the client asks to "make the logo bigger", the real problem is that the branding in the layout is not strong enough; the solution is to integrate the geometry of the brand mark into every other element, so the brand is recognized without a gigantic logo on each page.

## Explanation

Allan Peters says that since he began integrating the "DNA" of the brand mark into all the other design elements, he has not been asked to make the logo bigger. The mark contains a set of rules (line width, the relationship between strokes and negative space, the curves, the level of organic versus geometric structure) that can be applied to:

- **Patterns**: for the top of a Facebook page, tissue paper, the back of a business card; they can be the logo repeated, an element from it, a series of stripes from it or its outlined shape. Peters delivers a range from "subtle" to "can't look away".
- **Icons**: you determine the subject matter, the simplest way to illustrate it, then "skin" it with the rules of the mark. The PDCo squirrel has the same line width, the same negative space between strokes and the same curve as the brand mark; its eye is the size of the mark's center negative space. The test: if you saw the icon without the logo, would it make you think of the brand?
- **Flourishes and badge shapes**: the same system of positive and negative strokes; the elements must feel as if they came from the same artist. A custom logo paired with a random font and flourishes found online feels like "a group of strangers forced to live together".
- **Typography**: when there is a budget for a custom font, its line widths, curves and negative space match the mark. Still, the true purpose of typography is communication: no more than two or three unique characters that catch the eye, the rest in harmony with them (like the lowercase "a" in Helvetica).
- **Illustrations**: sketched freely, then built with the geometric rules of the mark; Peters prefers combining branded vector art with photography.

## Application

- Write down the geometric rules of the mark (line widths, angles, radii, spacing) and use them as the "grammar" for the rest of the identity.
- Build the client a library of patterns and icons, not just a logo.
- When you get the request "make the logo bigger", check whether the layout uses enough brand elements.

## Example

For Landmark Church, the brand mark (a cityscape, an implied hill and a cross in the negative space) was developed into a brand illustration with three hills and three rings, which amplifies the symbolism without changing the mark.
