# A single dominant image can tell the story, if the copy supports it rather than repeats it

> **The idea in one sentence:** the classic examples yield an art direction rule: one large image, stripped of everything that is not essential, with copy that adds what the image cannot say; Ogilvy warns, though, that the image alone does not sell.

## Explanation

Watkins offers several examples in which the image carried almost the whole message:

- **Kodak, "Pictures from Home" (1917).** An Army officer in front of a field tent gazes at the snapshots that came in a letter. L. B. Jones had written three hundred words, but looking at the page he realized that the picture told the story. He cut the text out altogether, except for the headline.
- **Pillsbury (Burnett, 1950).** The photographer captured the "inherent drama" of a cake, stripped of all embellishment; Burnett asked for it on the page as big as possible, and the layout became "startlingly simple".
- **Young & Rubicam, "Impact" (1930).** Rubicam, art director Vaughn Flannery and the Bruehl photographers tried at least 20 other picture ideas before coming back to the first one they had rejected: two prize fighters, one getting a sock in the jaw. The copy: two definitions of the word, and nothing more.
- Uncle Sam (Flagg, 1917), cited by Richards: the finger pointing at the viewer is the whole message (see [[HIS-019-the-world-wars-turned-advertising-from-a-tool-of-commerce|HIS-019]]).

The second thread is the tension. Ogilvy, whose Hathaway eyepatch inspired a whole school of "visual gimmicks", deplores it: "pictures don't sell unless you put some hard-selling copy underneath them." At Marlboro, the image got almost the whole page, but the copy, though short, was written as directly as the photograph. And at Hamm's, the agency insisted on buying only the highest quality art, in print, on TV, on the package and at the point of sale.

## Application

- Choose a single hero image and remove everything that competes with it: setting, typefaces, secondary elements.
- If the image already tells the story, the copy has to add what is missing (reason, offer, name), not describe it.
- Try many visual variants; Rubicam reached the final image only after 20 attempts.

## Example

For "Impact", Rubicam went back to the boxers only when the photographer changed the casting, and the picture took on the fresh and dramatic character they wanted.

## Questions

- In which situations should the image dominate (outdoor, social), and in which the copy (complex products)?
- Ogilvy or Kodak: who is right when the image seems to say everything?
