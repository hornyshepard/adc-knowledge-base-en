# Marlboro: repositioning a feminine brand through a single masculine image

> **The idea in one sentence:** in 1954–1955, Leo Burnett turned Marlboro, a class-market cigarette for sophisticates and women, into a mass brand designed "for men that women like", mainly through image and layout decisions.

## Explanation

Watkins publishes the Burnett agency's account (he worked there). Marlboro had existed for more than 30 years as an expensive, unfiltered cigarette, preferred by sophisticates and women. Philip Morris put the name on a new product: a filter cigarette, popular-priced, with a new blend and a crush-proof, flip-top box. The objective, as the client defined it: a "cigarette designed for men that women like."

The agency made three decisions to give things up:

1. **No health.** The promise of "safe" filtered smoking was in fashion, but competitors were not doing well with it, and psychologists pointed out that people who have fears resent being reminded of them. The agency let people take it for granted that the filter was effective.
2. **No package as the main theme.** The package was the most significant advance in cigarette packaging in 38 years and would have made a lot of initial sales. It was rejected, because a lasting impression of the cigarette itself had to be built.
3. **No old image.** Research showed that many people thought of filter cigarettes as a woman's smoke and of the old Marlboro as a fancy smoke for dudes and women. The image had to be reversed.

The execution: large photographs of men, first the cowboy, America's No. 1 symbol of masculinity, then the tattoo, which suggested a rugged and romantic past and separated Marlboro men from other he-man illustrations. The picture took almost the entire area of a full-bleed page; the heading and logotype fell in reverse within the picture; the package and the copy sat in a narrow strip at the bottom. The copy was pure vernacular, with no wasted words. Philip Morris had the courage to follow its instincts, although some early copy tests had come out negative.

Richards credits the Marlboro Man to Draper Daniels, Burnett's creative director.

## Application

- In a repositioning, decide first what you will **not** say: the most tempting short-term arguments can block the long-term image.
- Let the image carry the personality and the copy carry the minimum information.
- A distinctive detail (the tattoo) makes a common archetype (the cowboy) the brand's property.

## Example

The slogan "You get a lot to like" summed up a simple selling story set by the agency: a filter cigarette that delivers on flavor, in a new crush-proof box.

## Questions

- How do we judge this branding lesson today, knowing what product it sold?
- Which brand today could be repositioned through image alone, without changing the product?
