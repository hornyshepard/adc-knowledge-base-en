# Fine art entered advertising through the poster and through "Bubbles": the beginning of the image ad

> **The idea in one sentence:** once printing technology allowed it, advertisers borrowed the prestige of art, and Pears' "Bubbles" (1887) opened the way for the ad that does not talk about the product's function but about what you feel looking at it.

## Explanation

Richards traces the moment the image becomes an argument. In 1820, an ad for Warren's Blacking shoe polish, illustrated by the caricaturist George Cruikshank (a cat hissing at its own reflection in a boot), became famous. The real break, though, comes in 1887. Thomas J. Barratt, managing director of A. & F. Pears, buys Sir John Everett Millais's painting *A Child's World*, a young boy blowing bubbles, renames it *Bubbles* and adds a bar of soap at the boy's feet, much to the chagrin of the painter. It is the first use of a bona fide work of "fine art", not originally created for advertising, and, according to the author, an early "image" advertisement, because it did not focus on the product's function. The move sparked a debate, but an art magazine editor quoted by Richards saw artistic advertising as a way of spreading good art in the most public manner possible. Pears' Soap used it for decades.

At the same time, color lithography created the modern poster. Jules Chéret (from 1866) and Henri de Toulouse-Lautrec made posters for Paris that are museum pieces today. The line between art and advertising was quite blurred; there were poster exhibitions, poster clubs and poster magazines, and in 1900 the Crystal Palace hosted the International Advertisers' Exhibition, a gallery of ad posters. The Art Directors Club of New York has held an annual exhibition since 1921.

Watkins adds the star illustrators of the first half of the 20th century. Coles Phillips, with the Holeproof Hosiery girl, is credited by *Fortune* with initiating "cheesecake", a dainty sensuality that sold silk stockings.

## Application

- Borrowing artistic prestige works when the work says something about the brand (cleanliness, childhood, purity for Pears), not when it only decorates.
- Discuss rights and changes with the artist: the Millais and Pears conflict is repeated today with photographers and illustrators.
- An image with no functional argument is a strategic image choice, not a lack of idea.

## Example

In 1893, the first full-page full-color ad (Mellin's Food, on the back cover of *Youth's Companion*) also used a work of fine art: *The Awakening of Love* by Perrault.

## Questions

- When does using art in an ad become a form of "borrowed interest" (see Burnett)?
- Can today's collaborations with artists (limited editions, NFTs) create the same effect as *Bubbles*?
