# The "one wall test": all the materials of a brand must appear to come from the same source

> **The idea in one sentence:** put all of a brand's creative output on one wall; if it does not look written and drawn by the same "person", the brand elements are not doing their job.

## Explanation

For McTigue and Rucker, a brand is a set of elements and associations linked to a name, a mark or other symbolic cues. They have two parts: the **signals** that say "this is the brand" (they aid recognition) and the **equity**, good and bad, associated with the brand. Their example: for one segment of consumers, Taco Bell means the color purple (neutral), inexpensive (positive) and lower quality (negative).

The obvious signals are the name, the logo and the tagline. Great brands also use subtler elements: music and sounds, colors, the style of imagery or photography, spokespeople, and in physical spaces touch, smell and sound (Disneyland).

Orbit gum rose to number one in the US with absurd but always branded ads: the same spokesperson with a distinct accent, a close-up of the package with a trumpet sound, a smile with an over-the-top glint and a "ting", the word "fabulous" and the same tagline. Nike passes a test from the authors' classes: from the first fifteen seconds of a Nike ad from another country, with no logo, students recognize the brand by the music, the look on the athlete's face and the way the camera shoots the action.

**The one wall test**: you put the ads, posts, white papers, in-store materials and search engine language on a wall and check whether they appear to come from the same source. The authors' verdict: they usually don't. Codifying the brand personality in a guide matters most in social media, where several people speak as the voice of the brand.

## Application

- At the start of the project, list the brand signals: what is mandatory (the logo), what is recommended (a signature color), what is free.
- Run the wall test before hand-off: the same typography, the same image style, the same voice across all formats?
- Check the linkage with the brand: if you can retell the ad without mentioning the brand, the linkage is weak.

## Example

In the revised Stark brief, the logo becomes mandatory and the "Stark Blue" is "to be considered". The difference between an imposed signal and a recommended one is written down explicitly.

## Questions

- Where does consistency end and monotony begin? How do you adapt the tone for TikTok without losing recognition?
- Which non-visual signals (sound, smell) could be included in the brief?
