# The strategy visual: a visual demonstration of the promise is advertising's holy grail, because what you see you believe without decoding

> **The idea in one sentence:** a picture that shows the promise in action is processed instantly, believed because "you saw it" and can be reused for years; the goal for a headline is zero words.

## Explanation

Milavsky calls the visual that sums up the creative strategy in a single image a **strategy visual** (or *strategic graphic*). His arguments:

- the fast brain processes the image immediately, while a word has to be decoded;
- a graphic proof is immensely more persuasive than a verbal claim: viewers feel they have experienced the result themselves;
- visual signs work as semiotics: a thumb held up needs no explanation;
- once found, the demonstration can be placed in different scenarios, across many commercials, as a brand asset.

The demonstration does not have to be perfectly logical to convince, but it has to be **honest and believable on camera**. On Joy, P&G's technical staff supervised every take, so that nothing was done to "fix" the demonstration. For food, the search for the "gotta have it!" appetite shot is like looking for a holy grail: this is not the place to save money.

## Application

- In the creative brief, ask explicitly: what does the promise look like, without a single word?
- Look for a before and after, a direct comparison or a gesture that shows the end benefit (the plate as a mirror), not only the product.
- Once you find the demonstration, keep it: change the contexts, not the image.
- If the product does not allow a demonstration, move the promise to what can be shown (Subway gave up on a visible "fresh taste" and went for the sandwiches looking healthy).

## Example

**The number-five mayonnaise.** Studying the product, the agency noticed it was a little thicker than the leader's. A spoon stuck in the client's jar stayed erect; in the leader's jar it drooped over. The advertising tied thickness to more eggs and more flavor: "A little thicker; a lot more flavor." Although the test was of thickness, not flavor, the brand rose from fifth to second place within a year. Other demonstrations in the book: the cheese stretching as a slice of Pizza Hut pizza is lifted, used for several years, and the drop of Joy that disperses the grease in a white dish.

## Questions

- The mayonnaise demonstration ties thickness to flavor without proof. Is that legitimate persuasion or misleading?
- What does a strategy visual look like for an intangible service (insurance, a bank, an app)?
