# Color tells the story, sets the brand apart from competitors and motivates, and saturation matters more than hue

> **The idea in one sentence:** color is chosen neither by taste nor from a table of "meanings", but for three jobs: to tell the brand's story, to set it apart from competitors and to give the emotional energy that suits the purchase motive.

## Explanation

The sources in the library cover three jobs of color:

| Job | What the authors say |
|---|---|
| **The story** | Allan Peters (SRC-017): a logo of a tree will usually be green; color confirms the subject of the mark. |
| **The difference** | Peters: do a competitive analysis of the competitors' colors before you choose; saturated colors stand out on the shelf and on screen, and marks that last 50 years tend toward them. No hue is "better"; red and blue are simply the most common. |
| **Motivation** | Percy and Rosenbaum-Elliott (SRC-049): color attracts attention, but its main effect is motivational. Black and white works for an ad that only informs, never for positive motives (pleasure, social approval). |

The authors disagree about "color psychology". Sachdev's textbook (SRC-031) gives the popular table of associations: red for sales, blue for trust, black for luxury. Laura Guido-Clark, interviewed in SRC-016, indirectly contradicts it. Color family is only one dimension, and saturation and brightness have a greater impact than the hue itself. For surprise or delight you dial up the brightness, and a glossy finish comes toward you, while a matte one draws you in. She chooses color based on emotion, not trend, but checks the symbolic meaning of the color in each culture. Ingledew asks the same for symbols.

## Application

- Start from the brand's target emotion and associations, not from a favorite hue.
- Put the colors of all competitors on one board and look for the empty space.
- Adjust saturation and brightness before you change the hue; "warm up" or "cool down" the black and the white to tie the palette together (Peters).
- In transformational strategies, do not use black and white; in informational ones it can work.
- Check the meaning of the colors in each market.

## Example

Bobby Calder (SRC-014) gives two complementary cases. UPS brown was chosen for its association with a basic, routine service, and the trucks and uniforms repeat it. Pepsi's red and blue were originally used only to differentiate it from Coca-Cola, but over time the colors themselves came to be associated with the brand.

## Questions

- Can a palette be a brand's "property"? What do you do when a competitor copies it?
- Are tables of color meanings useful as a starting point, or do they only reinforce clichés?
