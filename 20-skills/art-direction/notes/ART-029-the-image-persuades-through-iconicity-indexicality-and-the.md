# The image persuades through iconicity, indexicality and the lack of an explicit syntax, which lets it suggest what could not be said

> **The idea in one sentence:** a photograph looks like proof, an image comes loaded with emotions, and the fact that images have no precise grammar lets them communicate implicitly what would be unacceptable spelled out in words.

## Explanation

In the "active audience, social level" sector of the map of perspectives, the authors present social semiotics (Kress & van Leeuwen) and, above all, Paul Messaris's theory of **visual persuasion**, "perhaps the first comprehensive theory of visual persuasion" of its kind. It uses three semiotic concepts:

| Concept | What it does in an ad |
|---|---|
| **Iconicity** | The image represents an aspect of the real world and comes with the associated emotions, from each individual's experience and from shared culture. The emotion is communicated by both visual content and visual **form** (framing, angle, light). |
| **Indexicality** | Especially in photographs: the image serves as **documentary evidence**, as proof of the claim, because of its authenticity. |
| **Syntactic indeterminacy** | Contrary to Kress & van Leeuwen, Messaris maintains that images have no precise syntax and cannot make explicit propositions ("X causes Y"). |

It is precisely this relative "deficiency" of visual syntax that gives images their persuasive power. They escape explicit interpretation, so they can suggest what might be slightly unacceptable if fully spelled out. They can thus evade legal and moral restrictions through implicit communication. The authors note that these are very important issues for advertising and persuasion. The idea also has an ethical side.

Related to this is a study by Elliott and his colleagues on overt sexuality in ads: if the audience could classify the image as "art", the interpretation became positive. This happened especially when the product was given little attention, which moved the ad from the commercial register into the artistic one and suspended moral judgment.

## Application

- When you need credibility, use documentary photography (indexicality): real people, real places, real results.
- Work on the form, not only on the content of the image. Angle, distance and light change the associated emotion.
- Check ethically what the image "says" implicitly. If you could not write that message in the copy, ask yourself why you are suggesting it visually.

## Example

A perfume ad that places the bottle next to a couple embracing never states "the perfume makes you irresistible". An explicit proposition would be implausible and perhaps illegal, but the image suggests it without stating it.

## Questions

- Is the lack of visual syntax an advantage or a problem for informational strategies, which need clarity?
- How has the indexicality of photography changed in the era of AI-generated images?
