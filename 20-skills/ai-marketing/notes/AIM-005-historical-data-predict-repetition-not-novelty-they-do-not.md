# Historical data predict repetition, not novelty: they do not answer "what if" and do not show perceptions

> **The idea in one sentence:** transactions tell you well what happens if you do more of what you have already done; they do not tell you what would have worked better, why people buy, or which new idea to pursue.

## Explanation

Struhl deflates the promise of big data in three points:

1. **Measurement is not insight.** If a non-profit radio station raises money after a pledge drive, you know the drive worked, but you do not know how much you could have raised by other means. Observed behavior shows neither the alternatives you did not try nor the reasons.
2. **Transaction data do not see perceptions.** Repeated coupons and promotions raise sales in the short term, but they can position a brand very firmly as not being worth full price. JC Penney lived for years on heavy discounting, then tried to "reposition" itself as an exclusive brand without the discounts. The effort failed so badly that the chain advertised an apology and brought many of the discounts back.
3. **Forecasts are necessarily short term.** A new or disruptive product can invalidate models built on history entirely.

The book also takes apart an urban legend: the "diapers and beer" story (young fathers sent out for diapers before the weekend also buy beer), cited for years as proof of the power of data mining, even in a textbook. The man who made it up later confessed he had invented it to show how ridiculous such anecdotes could become. Struhl's conclusion: "no amount of digging through transactions will think up a new strategy." The hard thinking remains the job of people.

## Application

- When a client asks for "insight from data", separate the questions: *who* and *how much* (historical data) from *why* (qualitative research, surveys) and *what if* (experiments).
- Before an aggressive promotion, ask what it does to brand perception, not only to the quarter's sales.
- Treat any anecdote about "patterns discovered by chance" as a hypothesis to be checked.

## Example

The JC Penney case shows the cost of ignoring perceptions: customers did not recognize the new identity as their store's, and the damage lingered even after the discounts returned.

- Which brand questions cannot be answered from a retailer's sales dashboard?
- How would you show a client that a brand campaign had an effect if their data show only short-term sales?
