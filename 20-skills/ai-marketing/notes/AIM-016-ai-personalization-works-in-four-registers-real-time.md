# AI personalization works in four registers: real-time, behavioral, micro-segment and predictive

> **The idea in one sentence:** AI does not "personalize" in general but in four distinct ways, each with different data and different risks; the strategist has to know which one the brief is asking for.

## Explanation

Karen Noil starts from the idea that personalization on demographic criteria (age, gender, city) is static and too broad: it says little about intent. Behavior says more. From the chapter on personalization you can draw a useful typology in four registers:

| Register | What data it uses | Example from the book |
|---|---|---|
| Real-time personalization | what the person is doing now, in the session | someone browsing winter coats → gets suggestions for scarves and gloves |
| Behavioral personalization | browsing, purchase and listening history | Spotify's "Discover Weekly", a new playlist every Monday |
| Micro-segment (hyper-segmentation) | very narrow groups with shared traits | customers who bought summer dresses receive new arrivals in their favorite styles |
| Predictive personalization | the likelihood of the next action | the online bookstore that anticipates the next mystery novel |

The book also names two limits: **over-personalization**, when the message feels intrusive and erodes trust, and **data silos**: if the CRM, the online store and social media are not integrated, the unified profile does not exist and personalization stays cosmetic. Iroegbu ([[SRC-042-the-future-of-marketing|SRC-042]], ch. 11) adds a social cost: over-personalization can shut people inside filter bubbles, which limit their exposure to new ideas. The digital marketing textbook by Zahay et al. reaches the same limit from email practice ([[DIG-069-personalization-works-only-with-relevance-respect-and|DIG-069]]).

## Application

- When a client asks for "personalized communication", ask: in which register? Each calls for different data, a different budget and different production.
- For the creative team, personalization means a **modular system**, not a single ad: variables (image, headline, offer) that combine according to the rules of the chosen register.
- Set a "common-sense line" from the start: which data you will not use in the message, even if you have them (health, finances, private life).

## Example

Amazon ("Customers who bought this also bought...") and Netflix are the book's standard examples: both combine the behavioral register with the predictive one, also using what similar customers chose. Najafov ([[SRC-039-chatgpt-for-marketing|SRC-039]], ch. 7) details the mechanics of Spotify: behavior signals (skipped songs, songs played on repeat, playlists), collaborative filtering (what people with similar tastes listen to), analysis of lyrics and metadata, and audio analysis (tempo, key).

## Questions

- Where does personalization turn "creepy"? Would a message that shows the brand knows what you searched for last night bother you?
- The figures cited in the book (91% of consumers are more likely to shop with brands that provide relevant offers, according to Accenture) come with no methodology. Are they evidence or sales arguments?
- The Ehrenberg-Bass school (Byron Sharp) holds that brands grow through broad reach, not narrow targeting. How do the two views fit together?
