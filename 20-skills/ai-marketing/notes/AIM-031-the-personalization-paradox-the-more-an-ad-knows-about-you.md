# The personalization paradox: the more an ad knows about you, the more it risks scaring you

> **The idea in one sentence:** personalization raises relevance and conversion, but beyond a threshold ("I know too much about you") it produces distrust. The authors disagree on where the threshold is and who sets it.

## Explanation

**The enthusiastic position (Wuebben, SRC-008).** By 2030, ads will recognize you as in *Minority Report*. Billboards with facial recognition from NEC or IBM would show messages for each passer-by. "Gladvertising", outdoor advertising that reads your mood and pushes the right product, could become mainstream. Need-Based Ads and Predictive Ads (the refrigerator that places the order, the closet display that suggests a new suit jacket before an important meeting) would make advertising purely relevant. Wuebben compares opting out to keeping horse-drawn carriages after Henry Ford built the Model T: not many people will opt out. He also cites Yates Buckley's idea of an AI personal assistant that negotiates your data for you ("freexpensive"), trading preferences for access.

**The cautious position (DX Review, SRC-009; Ejeke, SRC-010).** Personalization works: 72% of customers only act on marketing messages tailored to their interests (SmarterHQ). EasyJet got a 25% greater click-through rate with emails built from customers' travel history, and Cadbury matched Dairy Milk flavors to each user's Facebook profile. Still, Ejeke warns: "When developing customized communications, avoid being too precise." People may freak out if you show them how much you know. The DX Review asks for transparency about the data you collect, consent (GDPR), user control over the level of personalization, and trust as the foundation of the relationship.

**The tension.** Wuebben starts from what technology can do, the others from what people accept. Between them sits the practical rule: personalization is welcome when it is *predictable* ("you recommended this because I bought that") and rejected when it looks like *guesswork about private life* (mood, health, relationships). The research in *The Psychology of Advertising* sides with the cautious camp: an ad that fits closely *and* is visibly targeted becomes suspect and lowers purchase intention ([[ADV-068-online-targeting-helps-but-an-ad-that-is-both-matched-and|ADV-068]]). Zahay et al. reach the same conclusion from email practice ([[DIG-069-personalization-works-only-with-relevance-respect-and|DIG-069]]).

## Application

- Use a ladder: context (place, moment) → segment → individual. Climb only if you can explain in one sentence why the person sees the message.
- Do not infer sensitive states (emotions, health) without explicit consent, even if the technology allows it.
- Say in the copy where the recommendation comes from. Transparency can even be part of the idea.

## Example

The EasyJet campaign (12.5 million unique emails) worked because the data belonged to the relationship with the customer (their flights), and the benefit was obvious: a destination recommendation (SRC-010).

## Questions

- Who draws the "too much" line: the law, the platform, the brand or each user?
- Is "gladvertising" manipulation or a service?
