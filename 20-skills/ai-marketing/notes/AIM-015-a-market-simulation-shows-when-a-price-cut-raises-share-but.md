# A market simulation shows when a price cut raises share but lowers revenue

> **The idea in one sentence:** a simulator built from a discrete choice study lets you play out your move and the competitors' response before you make them in the market.

## Explanation

The most valuable output of a discrete choice study, Struhl says, is the **market simulator**: an Excel file with controls for each product's features and prices. You change a setting and immediately see how shares of preference move. A good simulator has:

- a **base case** (today's market), against which every change is compared;
- adjustment controls for awareness and distribution;
- besides share, **revenues** (for example per 100 sales), because the highest share is not always the highest profit.

The base case is needed because utility does not turn into share in a straight line. The relationship is an **S-shaped curve**: a small brand has to add a lot of value to stand out from the noise; in the middle range, small improvements move a lot; at very high shares, it is hard to convert customers who are loyal to other brands. The same gain in utility has different effects depending on the starting point. The curve is close to the one in prospect theory (Kahneman).

Struhl adds rules on price: four price levels are enough for almost any shape of the price versus share curve. Do not extrapolate beyond the prices tested, because there may be a "kink" or a **reservation price**, beyond which sales drop rapidly.

## Application

- When the client wants to cut the price "to gain share", ask for the scenario in which the competitors respond in the same way.
- Present the results in the language of the decision. An executive in the book demanded, irritated, not elasticities but how many sales he would lose if he raised the price by a dollar.
- Use the simulator in workshops with the client: it is a tool for strategic dialogue, not just a report.

## Example

**Mobile phone towers (Ace T&T).** The market leader, with a 38% share at the average price of $68,000, simulated a cut to $52,000. Alone, it would have risen to 46%, but with slightly lower revenues. If the competitors followed, it would end up back at about 39%, with revenues about 22% lower. Only Insignificant Co. would have come out nearly unscathed. Management issued the call to **lock up the reports**, for fear of a leak. A price increase made by Ace alone brought a small gain in revenue.

## Questions

- Why is market share a misleading objective for a leader?
- What is the simulator's limit if the market changes radically after the study?
