# The best headlines are often found, not invented: listen to the client and the street

> **The idea in one sentence:** many of the best-known headlines and slogans of the twentieth century were first spoken by a client, a passer-by or a tired driver; the copywriter's job was to recognize them and to have the nerve to use them.

## Explanation

Watkins returns obsessively to the same observation: good ideas are everywhere, but you have to know one when you see it, or hear it. His examples form a pattern:

| Headline / slogan | Who actually said it |
|---|---|
| "I'd Walk a Mile for a Camel" | a stranger to whom a sign painter gave a cigarette |
| "Ask the Man Who Owns One" (Packard, since 1902) | the founder, J. W. Packard, in a letter to a prospect who asked for proof |
| "Imagine Harry and Me Advertising Our Pears in Fortune" | David, one of the Harry & David brothers, looking out of the agency's window |
| "A hog can cross the country without changing trains — but you can't!" | Robert R. Young, head of the C&O railroad, in a speech |
| "That's a Hell of a Way to Run a Railroad" | an office remark by a Boston & Maine manager, a stock phrase taken from a cartoon |
| "The Longest Left Turn in History" | a tired driver, after endless laps of the Bonneville test course |

Richards adds the Avis case: "We Try Harder" (1962) came about when Bernbach asked the president, Robert Townsend, how his company, number two in the market, differed from Hertz.

The lesson is not that inspiration comes on its own. These copywriters waited in the station with angry passengers (Boston & Maine), read transcripts of the client's speeches (C&O) or listened closely in a meeting. The natural phrase has two advantages: it sounds true, because it is real speech, and it has already been tested on a person.

## Application

- Write down word for word what the client, the salespeople and the buyers say; look for the phrase that sums up the problem better than the brief.
- Spend time where the product is used or complained about; that is where the language is.
- When you hear the phrase, strip out everything extra: "Look at All Three Low Priced Cars Before You Buy" became "Look at All Three".

## Example

For Boston & Maine, the agency sent a man to wait for hours in the station, during a snowstorm, among the delayed passengers. The ad explained, without apologizing, why the trains were late, and closed by saying that the railroad always runs.

## Questions

- How do you tell a living phrase from a cliché that only sounds familiar?
- Who is the author of a slogan the client said: the agency or the client?
