# Image advertising sells a feeling, not a spec sheet

> **The idea in one sentence:** the second great tradition of classic copywriting drops the list of features and sells the meaning of the product (status, freedom, nostalgia, belonging), trusting the intelligent reader to make the connection.

## Explanation

Richards presents Theodore MacManus as the anti-Kennedy: he did not believe in research, wrote what he himself would have wanted to know, and held that reason-why treats people as fools. His answer was emotion and impression, what we now call image advertising (*soft sell*).

Watkins shows why the list of features could be dropped:

- **Cadillac, "The Penalty of Leadership" (1915).** No specifications; an essay about the envy that follows every leader. It appeared only once, and it was on the list of every professional Watkins consulted.
- **Jordan, "Somewhere West of Laramie".** Ned Jordan had spent years selling cars on "offset crank shafts" and "ejector manifolds". When the electric starter opened the women's market, he dropped "the old mechanical chatter" and wrote about people: a girl from Wyoming riding toward the sunset, in a car built "for her". The very first Playboy ad in *The Saturday Evening Post* had already brought a deluge of letters from women.
- **Steinway, "The Instrument of the Immortals" (Rubicam, 1919).** The earlier ads showed lovely ladies at the piano, with no headline. Rubicam read the client's file: practically all the greatest pianists and composers had played a Steinway. The phrase changed everything; the budget grew from some $25,000 or $30,000 to $500,000 a year.
- **Packard, "When Heaven Was at the Corner of Sycamore and Main".** The nostalgia of the boy who looked at the cars in the showroom window beside his father.

Watkins insists that the feeling must be anchored in something true: Steinway was in fact the masters' piano, and Packard had in fact become affordable.

## Application

- When the products in a category are equivalent, sell what the product means to the person who uses it.
- Anchor the emotion in a fact (Steinway) or in a shared experience (Packard); otherwise it sounds false.
- Leave room for the reader: a good image ad suggests, it does not explain.

## Example

"To a Man Who Is Afraid to Let His Dream Come True" (Rolls-Royce, Jack Rosebrook at Y&R) appeared only once and, according to Watkins, sold more cars off the floor than any other Rolls-Royce ad of record. He calls it an example of "emotionalizing facts".

## Questions

- When does image advertising become an escape from the lack of a real advantage?
- How do you measure an ad that appeared only once but has been requested for 30 years?
