# Reason-why: the ad as "salesmanship in print" that gives concrete, verifiable reasons to buy

> **The idea in one sentence:** the reason-why ad is written like the pitch of a salesman face to face: each paragraph brings a precise fact or argument that makes the purchase rational, and its effectiveness is judged by sales.

## Explanation

In 1904, John E. Kennedy sends Albert Lasker (Lord & Thomas, Chicago) a note: he can tell him what advertising is. The answer, "salesmanship in print", moves the ad from repeating the name to **persuasion**. Claude Hopkins, hired by Lasker in 1907, turns the idea into a method: he researches the product before writing, uses coupons and trial offers to compare arguments, and treats sales as the only measuring rod (*Scientific Advertising*, 1923).

Fernández Gómez (SRC-018), Richards (SRC-027) and Watkins (SRC-028) describe the same school. Watkins adds the textbook examples:

| Ad | The concrete reason |
|---|---|
| Ivory (1882) | "99 44/100 percent pure", a figure kept for decades |
| Pepsodent (Hopkins) | a named problem (the "film" on the teeth), an explanation, a free trial |
| The Sears catalogue | honest, detailed descriptions; an ad with 10 facts was 44% more effective than one with 4 |
| Canada Dry (George Cecil) | an "emotionalized" reason-why: the facts, told with warmth |

Reason-why does not mean dry copy: Sears copywriters combined facts with similes, pictorial nouns and lively, active verbs. The idea then passed into the P&G copy strategy (as proof of the benefit) and into Reeves's USP (a single, unique reason). Theodore MacManus attacked it as a style that treats people as fools, and the tension with image advertising runs through the whole twentieth century.

## Application

- For each promise, write the list of "because": ingredient, process, test, figure, guarantee. Choose the most specific reason, not the most boastful one.
- Look for a fact that competitors also have but that nobody has stated (Hopkins's method, taken up by Reeves).
- Tie each fact to a benefit for the reader: not "99.44% pure", but what purity means for their skin.
- Where the channel allows it (direct response, e-commerce), test versions of the argument, as Hopkins did with coupons.

## Example

Pepsodent: Hopkins named a problem the reader did not know about, explained it and offered a free trial. Watkins recommends the campaign as a way into the "reason-why school".

## Questions

- Martineau accused the "salesmanship in print" formula of ignoring feeling. Who is right, and in which categories?
- Does reason-why still work where all products have the same facts? Can a six-second spot have a reason-why?
