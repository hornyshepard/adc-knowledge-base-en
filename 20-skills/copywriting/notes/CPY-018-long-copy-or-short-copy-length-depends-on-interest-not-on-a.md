# Long copy or short copy? Length depends on interest, not on a rule

> **The idea in one sentence:** ads of 6,000 words and ads of four words both rank among the greatest in history; what unites them is not length but the fact that every word left in is interesting to the chosen reader.

## Explanation

Watkins explicitly fights the dogma that "people won't read long copy" and, at the same time, praises ruthless cutting. His evidence for both camps:

| Long copy | Short copy |
|---|---|
| "The Diary of a Lonesome Girl" (George Cecil): the diary of a girl with no pretty dresses; it sold dressmaking courses "as never before" | Kodak "You Press the Button — We Do the Rest": edited out of a long piece of copy by George Eastman himself |
| Merrill Lynch (Louis Engel): more than 6,000 words about the stock market; 5,033 requests in a month, though it was not designed to pull inquiries | Kodak "Pictures from Home" (1917): 300 words cut down to none, because the photograph told the story |
| Ford "The Longest Left Turn in History": an endurance report read like a novel | Tecla "A $10,000 Mistake": two sentences by Frank Irving Fletcher, a disciple of brevity |

Watkins's conclusion comes through a comparison: *Anthony Adverse* ran to more than a thousand pages, *Good-bye Mr. Chips* to little more than a hundred, and both were best sellers. Copy has to be made interesting and cut from the whole cloth of everyday experience; then it will be read. The moral of Merrill Lynch: never be afraid to tell the full story if it clarifies the issue and gives people information they can use.

Richards adds the case of Howard Gossage, famous for long-copy ads for Fina, Qantas and Heileman Brewing. One of them was all headline, a single sentence of more than 40 words in pink capitals, ending with the request to stop in at the gas station.

## Application

- Decide the length by the product and the decision: an investment, a course or a car can take, and needs, explanation; a drink or a chocolate bar rarely does.
- Write long, then cut everything that is not interesting to the person you are writing for. The Rogers Peet rule: "easy writing makes hard reading."
- Long copy needs structure: subheads, narrative, rhythm.

## Example

The Rogers Peet partners told young copywriters to state the facts with a smile and, if tempted to say too much, to save the rest for another advertisement.

## Questions

- On a phone screen, what is the equivalent of "long copy": the carousel, the thread, the three-minute video?
- Why do we believe people don't read, when they read for hours about what interests them?
