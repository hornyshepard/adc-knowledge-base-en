# Unexpected words draw attention in the headline, but learning needs familiar, concrete words, active sentences and no negatives

> **The idea in one sentence:** what attracts attention can get in the way of understanding (McGuire), so surprise is used in the headline, while the text that must be learned is written simply, concretely and positively.

## Explanation

The authors sum up psycholinguistic research (Greg Myers in particular) in principles that are starting points, not laws: interaction with the picture can cancel them, so an idea is tested, not rejected automatically.

**For attention** (Table 13.3):

- infrequent letters or unexpected spelling (Q, X, Z: Exxon, Oxo, FCUK);
- unusual stress on a word, in print or audio ("What makes Baxter's Soup *is* what makes it");
- **headlines of fewer than seven to eight words**, which are understood in the moment of reflexive attention ("Healthy Happy Hair"); essential for outdoor.

**For learning** (Table 13.4):

| Principle | Why | Example from the book |
|---|---|---|
| familiar words, used in familiar ways | surprise in the copy gets in the way of understanding | the unexpected only in the headline |
| **concrete** words, with strong imagery | they arouse mental images quickly and are better remembered | "For skin this soft" vs. "Because it really matters" |
| **avoid negatives** | they need two steps (recognize the negative, reverse the meaning) and can be overlooked | "This is not a normal day"; "An innovation this big doesn't pass unnoticed" (double negative) |
| puns only if they are obvious and reinforce the benefit | they demand processing effort | done right: the Ultra Chloraseptic throat spray, "Don't be a sucker, spray" (*sucker* = a fool and a boiled sweet) |
| simple sentences, **active voice** | the passive reverses the order and is harder to understand | "Children living in non-fluoride water areas should take a fluoride supplement" vs. "A fluoride supplement should be taken by children living in non-fluoride water areas" |
| personal address (questions, firm statements) | it simulates a conversation | "Tired of being tired?" |

Rhythm and rhyme help memory ("A Mars a day helps you work, rest and play" echoes the proverb "an apple a day"). Word order matters: what comes first becomes the retrieval cue, so the parent must learn "my children need…", not "supplements are for…". Milavsky (SRC-021) reaches the same rules from agency practice: short headlines, rhyme, rhythm and positive wording instead of negatives.

## Application

- Separate the roles of the text: the headline draws attention, the subhead and the copy get the benefit learned.
- Put the target or the target's need at the start of the sentence, and the product after it.
- Reread every negative and turn it into a positive statement.

## Example

A bank headline, "Don't waste any more time at the counter", becomes "Your account, open in 5 minutes, from your phone": it is positive, concrete and puts the target at the center.

## Questions

- The British school of copywriting loves puns. Is the authors' criticism too harsh?
- How does the seven-to-eight-word rule apply to text laid over video on social media?
