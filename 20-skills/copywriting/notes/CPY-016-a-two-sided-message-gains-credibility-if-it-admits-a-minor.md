# A two-sided message gains credibility if it admits a minor, plausible flaw linked to the main benefit

> **The idea in one sentence:** admitting a weakness makes the positive claims more credible, but it does not automatically improve the attitude toward the brand: everything depends on which flaw you choose and how much room you give it.

## Explanation

The one-sided message contains only favorable arguments; the two-sided message also includes negative aspects. Early research (Hovland, Lumsdaine & Sheffield, 1949) showed that the one-sided message is more persuasive with an audience already favorably disposed, and the two-sided one with an uninformed or initially hostile audience.

Fennis and Stroebe sum up the newer research: two-sided messages stand out from the clutter, reduce counterarguing, are perceived as more credible and strengthen the perception of the positive attributes mentioned. But the effect on overall attitude and purchase intention is inconsistent. The explanation, in the logic of the Fishbein model: the flaw lowers the evaluation, the credibility raises the certainty about the positive attributes; there is an optimal point.

Practical rules derived from this (Crowley & Hoyer, 1994; Pechmann, 1992; Pizzutti et al., 2016):

1. The negative information should not exceed **two-fifths** of the message.
2. Don't admit flaws on **important attributes** (jeans that "are not comfortable" lowered purchase intention, even though the salesperson seemed more honest).
3. A trivial flaw is too obvious as a tactic; flaws that can only be checked after purchase are more credible.
4. The best flaw is the one **correlated** with the benefit: the ice cream that was "creamy, but high in calories" won on attitude, because the calories confirmed the creaminess.
5. Refute the flaw when competitors are likely to attack on that attribute; attitudes formed this way hold up better.

Percy and Rosenbaum-Elliott (SRC-049) reach the same solution from another direction: for high-involvement informational strategies, they recommend the refutational approach: you first acknowledge the target's disbelief, bring evidence, then restate the claim firmly.

## Application

- Look for "the flaw that proves the benefit": expensive because it is handmade, slow because it is cooked as at home.
- Don't use two-sidedness for the attribute that decides the purchase.
- For new brands or skeptical audiences, two-sidedness is a useful omega tactic.

## Example

Avis: "we're number two", "so we try harder", a weakness turned into a benefit (ch. 1, p. 24).
