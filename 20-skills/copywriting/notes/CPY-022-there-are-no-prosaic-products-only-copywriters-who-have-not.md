# There are no prosaic products, only copywriters who have not yet found the person in the product

> **The idea in one sentence:** an alarm clock, a handkerchief or a tombstone seems impossible to make interesting until the copywriter gives it a personality, a story of how it is made or a person who uses it.

## Explanation

Watkins brings together several ads for products with no apparent charm and draws a rule from them: the next time somebody gives you a copy assignment for a "prosaic" product, "There ain't no such animal." The techniques they illustrate:

- **Personification.** Leroy, the BIG BEN (Westclox) copywriter, decided always to refer to the clock with the pronoun "he", never "it", like a person and not an object. The alarm clock that first whispers, then shouts became a member of the family.
- **The story of how it is made.** George Bijur for Weber & Heilbroner: the new handkerchief buyer, interested in nothing but handkerchiefs, went all the way to the flax fields of Ireland, ordered the linen direct and had it torn, not cut, because that makes better handkerchiefs. The result: the same box 65 cents cheaper. The headline says they traveled 2000 miles to save 65¢.
- **The person instead of the object.** The Barre Granite Association ran realistic photographs of tombstones with headlines such as "ETERNITY". Watkins, whose agency (the H. B. Humphrey Company) then took over the account, saw that the monument business lived because someone worth remembering had died. The ads began to talk about people: "Aunt Meg … who never married". The ad ranked first in Read Most in the Starch ratings.
- **The absurd association.** George Gribbin (Young & Rubicam): shirts have collars, horses have collars too, and a horse collar does not shrink. The result: a fanciful story about his friend Joe Holmes, who is now a horse, to say simply that Arrow shirts don't shrink. Gribbin forgot to put the price in the copy.
- **Real people.** Shell replaced the male-model service station operators with two likeable mugs, one of whom tells the sales manager he is "nuts". The series was often better read than the editorial content of the magazines it ran in.

## Application

- Ask: who is the person behind the product (the one who makes it, the one who uses it, the one it is bought for)?
- Look for a stage in the making or the journey of the product that surprises.
- If the product is an object, try giving it a pronoun, a voice and a character.

## Example

Mennen's "Listen, You Biggest City in the World!" (circa 1919) sold shaving cream through a character, Jim Henry, who spoke in the first person and flattered the hurried New York reader.

- When does personification become childish for a serious category?
