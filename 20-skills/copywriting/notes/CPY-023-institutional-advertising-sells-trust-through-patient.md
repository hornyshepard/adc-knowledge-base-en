# Institutional advertising sells trust through patient explanation, not praise

> **The idea in one sentence:** the best company ads in Watkins's collection ask for nothing; they explain a policy, a problem or a conviction, because public goodwill is won over years, through facts told honestly.

## Explanation

Watkins gives a whole group of entries to ads that do not sell a product. The clearest document is an internal report by the Ayer representative in Boston (1908) about AT&T. The company did not need subscribers; it had more than it could connect. It needed the public, convinced that a monopoly cares for no one, to understand the real difficulties. The report proposed ads that would lead the reader by the hand, step by step, through the intricacies of the business, year after year: a slow process, but a sure one. Watkins notes that the report could be reprinted, almost word for word, as AT&T's advertising policy four decades later. The ad "Telephone Service: A Public Trust" (1928) was the speech of the president, Walter S. Gifford: a public statement the company then had to live up to.

Other variants of the same genre:

- **Explanation without apology.** Boston & Maine, during snowstorms: vice president Nichols asked for an explanation that would win sympathy for the operating people, not an apology and no false promise.
- **The company's value through its people.** Royal Typewriter, "The 2258th Part": the 2258th part of the typewriter is employee co-operation.
- **Advocacy.** Warner & Swasey's "Wonder What a Frenchman Thinks About" (1941) drew requests for 4,400,000 reprints. C&O and Southern Pacific each changed an industry policy with a single ad.
- **The agency's ad about itself.** "Brown's Job" (BBDO), "The Stranger at the Gate" (Rubicam at Ayer). Rubicam also wrote the Y&R series in *Fortune* to a double rule: leave the reader a valuable thought, and make the ad itself a demonstration of skill.

## Application

- Use institutional advertising when the problem is one of perception (monopoly, crisis, scandal), not of product.
- Explain; do not apologize and do not promise what you cannot control. Ask for understanding for the real people behind the service.
- A public statement of principles becomes a commitment: write only what the company will actually live up to.

## Example

"Brown's Job" (F. R. Feland, 1920), a text about a manager who cannot be replaced because "Brown's job is where Brown is," makes no reference to the agency beyond its signature. Watkins says BBDO was still receiving requests for reprints decades later.

## Questions

- Is yesterday's institutional ad the ancestor of today's "brand purpose" or of crisis communication?
- When does a company's explanation become propaganda?
