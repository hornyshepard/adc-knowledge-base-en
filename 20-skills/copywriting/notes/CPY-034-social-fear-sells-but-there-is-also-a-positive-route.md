# Social fear sells, but there is also a positive route

> **The idea in one sentence:** many of the classic ads with the biggest sales played on the fear of social rejection, but the same collection shows that a positive appeal can win in a category dominated by fear.

## Explanation

Watkins documents a whole family of ads built on embarrassment:

- **Listerine** ("Often a Bridesmaid, but Never a Bride"). When the Lambert brothers wanted to take the product into the commercial field, the company's chief chemist read through its uses and mentioned **halitosis**. Everybody asked what that was; someone said, "Maybe that's the peg we can hang our hat on."
- **Odorono** (James Webb Young, 1919). A taboo subject, handled "openly". Some 200 *Ladies' Home Journal* readers canceled their subscriptions, several women told Young the ad was an insult, and the deodorant's sales increased 112% that year.
- **Book of Etiquette** ("Again She Ordered Chicken Salad"). The young woman who orders chicken salad again because she cannot read the French menu.

The mechanism has two steps: you **name a problem** the reader did not know about or would not admit, then offer the solution as the way out. It works because the fear of exclusion is universal. Research supports Watkins: the meta-analyses summarized by Fennis and Stroebe (SRC-012) show that a more severe threat increases acceptance of the recommendation, and the idea that strong fear "blocks" the message has not been confirmed. The risk remains ethical: the problem can be inflated or invented.

The counterpoint also comes from Watkins. Don Calhoun (McCann-Erickson) had been frightened by an insurance agent at 29 and saw that the category's ads were full of weeping widows and flaming houses. For American Mutual he created the character Mr. Friendly and sold the relief of being protected, not the dread. The company rose from fourth to second place in sales. Burnett (Marlboro) also turned down an appeal to the fear of cancer, on the grounds that people who have fears resent being reminded of them.

## Application

- Before you write a fear appeal, ask: is the problem real and significant for the audience? Does the solution actually solve it?
- Also test the positive version (relief, pride, pleasure); in a category saturated with fear, it stands out.
- Be careful with vulnerable groups: appeals of this kind have disproportionately targeted women.

## Example

Gordon Seagrove was the sole creator of all Listerine advertising for twenty-two years, on the same named problem: halitosis.

## Questions

- Is "naming a problem" an insight or a manipulation? Where is the line?
- What does the appeal to social fear look like on social media (FOMO, constant comparison)?
