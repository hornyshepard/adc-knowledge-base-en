# Literally true slogans can mislead through the inferences they leave to the audience

> **The idea in one sentence:** consumers fill in what is missing from a claim on their own, almost always in the direction the marketer wants, and that can be persuasive but also misleading.

## Explanation

Comprehending an ad means forming inferences that go beyond the information given. Fennis and Stroebe, following Kardes, list the phrasing tactics that lead to faulty inferences and have drawn the attention of consumer protection authorities (the US Federal Trade Commission):

| Tactic | How it works | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Pragmatic inference | literally true, figuratively false | "Brand X may be the best beer in the world": "may" is heard as "is" |
| Omitted comparison | the term of comparison is missing | "Brand X relieves pain longer!": longer than what? |
| Implied consensus | the plural suggests a majority | "Dentists recommend Brand X toothpaste": two out of several thousand? |
| Juxtaposition | causality is inferred, not stated | "Be cool, buy Brand X" |
| Affirmation of the consequent | cause and effect are reversed | "If you can see it, you can make it: Brand X" |

The general context: Jacoby and Hoyer (1982) found that about 80 percent of television messages are initially miscomprehended in some way. And the truth effect means that a claim accepted in haste is rarely corrected.

Parsons, Maclaran and Chatzidakis (SRC-013) add a technique typical of the cosmetics industry: **scientific-sounding jargon** ("Glycation-Reversing Technology", "peptide-B3 complex"), combined with active verbs (repairing, redefining, restoring). A survey by the British ASA of 445 cosmetic ads found obvious breaches of the codes in 7 percent of cases, and 19 percent for skin creams. And for decades about 70% of consumers have believed that advertising is often untruthful, while still finding it informative.

## Application

- Use the list as an **ethical and legal checklist** when reviewing copy: what will the ordinary person understand, not what does the text literally say.
- If you make a comparison, name the term and back it with evidence.
- Lasting persuasion is built on claims that hold up after purchase (see the dilemma of the promise).

## Example

The slogan paraphrased from Carlsberg ("probably the best beer in the world") is the authors' example of pragmatic inference: "probably" is heard as "certainly". Percy and Rosenbaum-Elliott (SRC-049) read the same slogan differently: as a humble, Nordic understatement, an asset in the markets that recognize it. The difference shows that the inference depends on the audience's cultural code.

## Questions

- Where is the line between accepted exaggeration (*puffery*) and deception?
- How would you rephrase "Dentists recommend X" so that it is both persuasive and honest?
