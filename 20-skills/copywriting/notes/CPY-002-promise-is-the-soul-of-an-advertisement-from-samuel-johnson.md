# Promise is the soul of an advertisement: from Samuel Johnson (1759) to mail-order headlines

> **The idea in one sentence:** as early as 1759, when ads were already so numerous that they were read negligently, Samuel Johnson observed that the only way to gain attention is a large, well-told promise; the classic headlines that sold the most confirm the rule.

## Explanation

In essay no. 40 of *The Idler* (1759), Johnson describes a problem we think of as modern: clutter. Advertisements are so numerous that people skim them, so attention is gained through the magnificence of the promise and through eloquence. His most famous line: "Promise, large promise, is the soul of an advertisement." Richards stresses the ambivalence of the text: Johnson defends advertising and at the same time cautions advertisers to use care. He remarked, with irony, that the trade was so near to perfection that it could not be improved. In the same period, the word "puff" had become a synonym for an advertisement, meaning exaggerated praise or an ad disguised as news.

Watkins shows the promise in its mature form, as a headline that tells readers what they gain:

- "To Men Who Want to Quit Work Some Day" (Phoenix Mutual): freedom in old age, not a policy.
- "Who Else Wants a Whiter Wash — With No Hard Work?" (Rinso): the result and the end of the effort, which housewives asked for in the agency's field research.
- "Do You Make These Mistakes in English?" (Sherwin Cody): a promise hidden in a question; the ad ran almost unchanged for decades.

And the reverse: Shackleton's ad for the South Pole expedition promised danger, not comfort, and Watkins credits its success to the lure of adventure and the power of deadly frankness. A promise can be the adventure itself, if it is true for its audience.

## Application

- Put the promise in the headline: what does the reader get? If the headline promises nothing, the body copy will not be read.
- The size of the promise must be in proportion to the proof; otherwise it slides toward "puff" (see the substantiation standard).
- Test different wordings of the same promise: a direct benefit, a question, a "who else".

## Example

Phoenix Mutual pretested dozens of ads; the ad with the fisherman who had retired from work brought in nearly three times as many inquiries, and four times as many sales, as the average.
