# The anatomy of a print ad: visual, headline, subhead, body copy and signature, each with its own job

> **The idea in one sentence:** an ad is read in order (picture, headline, subhead, text, signature), and each element has a single job; the copywriter writes for this path of the eye, not for a page read from top to bottom.

## Explanation

Grewal and Levy describe the step in which the message and the appeal become words and pictures. Their example is an ad for the Ila DUSK personal alarm:

| Element | Its job |
|---|---|
| **The visual** | is noticed first; stops the eye, identifies the subject and shows the product being used, arouses interest in the headline |
| **The headline** | the large type designed to draw attention; short, with simple words, the primary benefit, the brand name and an interest-provoking idea, ideally with an action verb |
| **The subhead** | adds the information the headline cannot carry ("it screams for your safety") |
| **The body copy** | builds on the interest, explains, arouses desire and gives enough information to move to action |
| **The signature** | the brand elements: logo, slogan or USP |

Their practical rule: the headline should give enough information for learning even if it is the only element read.

The authors converge on the order. Percy and Rosenbaum-Elliott show that 70% of the 1.65 seconds spent on a magazine ad goes to the picture, so the picture must be seen before the words, and the headline should have fewer than seven to eight words. Milavsky asks for the same thing (seven words or less, ideally zero if the visual says the strategy). The difference lies in the length of the text: Grewal and Levy accept long body copy when the product needs explaining, like the alarm, which has to be shown and explained. Ogilvy would add that the picture does not sell without copy underneath that sells.

## Application

- Before the first word, sketch the path of the eye: what the person sees in the first second, what they read in the second.
- Give each element a single job. If the headline and the subhead say the same thing, one of them is useless.
- The headline-alone test: cover the rest of the ad. Does the reader learn what the product is and what they gain?
- The length of the body copy is decided by the purchase decision, not by fashion (see the long copy vs. short copy debate).

## Example

In the Ila DUSK ad, the headline only names the product, the subhead gives it its promise ("it screams for your safety"), and the body copy turns it into a party-season fashion accessory that "carries" a scream for help. The Marks & Spencer logo signs the ad.

## Questions

- Does this anatomy still apply to a social media post, where the text sits below the picture, or to a vertical story?
- When is it a good idea for an ad to have no headline at all?
