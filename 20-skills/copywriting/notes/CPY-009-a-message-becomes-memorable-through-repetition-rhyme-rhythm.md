# A message becomes memorable through repetition, rhyme, rhythm, chunking, a twist on a familiar phrase and audiovisual synchronization

> **The idea in one sentence:** if the audience does not retain the selling idea, it does not act; memory can be helped with techniques as old as fables and songs.

## Explanation

Milavsky ties the techniques to the "retention" step in McGuire's chain:

- **Repetition**: bad style in writing, but the rule in advertising. His commercial with the highest day-after-recall score repeated the same phrase, in two variations, six times in 30 seconds. A viewer needs at least three exposures before they actually "see" a commercial.
- **Rhyme and rhythm**: the slogan "Nothing says lovin' like something from the oven" is much easier to retain than a paraphrase without rhyme. Internal rhymes split the line into two chunks and announce the second. The order of the vowels matters (*ding-dong*, not *dong-ding*).
- **Chunking**: grouping into pieces, as with telephone numbers. Headlines and slogans respect the limit of three to seven items; in print, break the lines where an idea ends.
- **The twisted familiar phrase**: one word changed in a saying takes over the familiarity of the original.
- **Acronyms** condense an idea for quick recall.
- **Sound effects**: points of attention at the opening or at key moments, used with care.
- **Audiovisual synchronization**: show what you are saying at the moment you say it. Ideally, the commercial can be understood from the visuals alone and from the audio alone.
- **Depth of meaning**: a double meaning (in the Joy ad, the mirror-like plate is also a "reflection" on the housewife) makes a line richer and longer-lasting. Milavsky aims for three levels of meaning, without becoming cryptic.

For print he adds: headlines of seven words or less (ideally zero, if the visual says the strategy), no excess of cleverness, visuals that suggest a story, and careful proofreading, because a mistake suggests a sloppy product.

## Application

- Read the slogan aloud three times: does it have rhythm? Can it be hummed? Does it break naturally into two pieces?
- Do the "visuals only / audio only" test on the animatic or the storyboard.
- Repeat the brand name and the key idea in varied ways, not mechanically.

## Example

Against a competitor's expensive campaign that called its gasoline "No Trouble Gasoline" (a negative), copywriter Mike Hart proposed a positive formula for Texaco: "Clean Gasoline", "…always has been." Simpler and more direct, with a fraction of the budget, it kept the gasolines comparable in perception; some respondents even felt that Texaco might be better.
