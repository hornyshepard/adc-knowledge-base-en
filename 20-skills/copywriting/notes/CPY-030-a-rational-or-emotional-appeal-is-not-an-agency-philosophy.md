# A rational or emotional appeal is not an agency philosophy but a match with the product and the person

> **The idea in one sentence:** the dispute between agencies that swear by emotion and agencies that swear by arguments is settled empirically: both work, for different people and different products.

## Explanation

Fennis and Stroebe show that informational appeals (the "tell" approach, "hard sell", the "reason-why approach") and affect-based appeals ("soft sell") have coexisted since the beginning of the twentieth century (Fox, 1984), rather than one evolving out of the other. Some agencies (Leo Burnett) have claimed that the emotional appeal is more effective, others (Ogilvy & Mather) the rational one.

What the research says:

- **The product:** experiential products (wine, soft drinks, perfumes, designer clothing) are evaluated by personal preference and lend themselves to affect-based appeals; durable and functional products (computers, washing machines, washing powder) to rational appeals. Some combine both (sports cars, watches). Even for soft drinks, though, arguments can be tried: Pepsi's blind tasting, "no caffeine".
- **The person: need for cognition.** Venkatraman et al. (1990): consumers who enjoy effortful cognitive activity were more influenced by rational appeals, those low in the need for cognition by emotional ones.
- **The person: self-monitoring.** Snyder and DeBono (1985): people concerned with their image (*high self-monitors*) preferred the image-oriented ad for Canadian Club whisky and were willing to pay more; the others preferred the quality-oriented ad. Cheese "from France" drove the taste ratings of the former, the actual taste drove those of the latter; an attractive bottle mattered more than the scent for the former (DeBono et al., 2003). Replications, however, are inconsistent.

## Application

- Do not start from the "house style"; start from the product and the target's profile.
- For products with a double function, use both: the copy for quality, the image for self-expression.
- Segment by traits when you can (online): the same product, different frames for "those who want details" and "those who want to look good".

## Example

Canadian Club: "You're not just moving in, you're moving up" (image) vs. "When it comes to great taste, everyone draws the same conclusion" (quality).

## Questions

- What kind of appeal would a bank use for young people low in the need for cognition?
- Is "emotion sells" an agency claim or a scientific finding?
