# The picture hooks, the headline anchors: denotation informs, connotation adds meanings the target has to decode

> **The idea in one sentence:** every ad combines a literal meaning (denotation) with subjective, cultural and implicit meanings (connotations); because the picture is polysemic, the words have the job of controlling which meaning the target reads.

## Explanation

*La boîte à outils de la publicité* starts from semiology (Barthes, *Rhétorique de l'image*, 1964). In the picture, the relation between the signifier (*signifiant*) and the thing signified (*chose signifiée*) is **analogical** (through resemblance); in language, the relation is **arbitrary** and depends on the code, culture, mood and values.

| Discourse | What it does | Example from the book |
|---|---|---|
| **Denotative** (*discours dénotatif*) | describes, explains, demonstrates; words with a primary meaning, the picture shows what is | L'Oréal: the product described, the packaging shown so it is recognized in the store, the innovation put forward, balanced by a star the consumer can identify with |
| **Connotative** (*discours connotatif*) | suggests more than it shows, works with imagination and emotion; second-degree (*second degré*) meanings | Renault Twingo: a cartoon world in which the car adapts to any environment and plays its way around every obstacle (agile, sparkling, colorful) |

Connotation does not necessarily come from the sign itself but from the way it is used: a particular stress, an unusual association. It depends on the context and can produce several "effects of meaning" (*effets de sens*). The practical conclusion: in a saturated space with little time (especially in print), the picture more and more often takes on the role of the hook (*accroche*), while the headline comments on the visual and **controls** its meanings.

The authors note that messages that work with humor and indirect, strongly connoted elements are also the ones that give the target the most satisfaction, through the complicity of having grasped the hidden meaning. The condition: the cultural worlds of the message and of the target must overlap.

## Application

- Show the visual without text to three people and note what they read. Then write the headline that keeps the intended meaning and closes off the others.
- Add "qualifying" elements (*éléments qualifiants*) to the picture (an object, a gesture, a color) that tilt the reading toward the brand.
- The more connoted the message, the better you need to know the target's culture and what it already knows about the brand.

## Example

The same visual, a couple on a bench in the rain, can be read as melancholy, romance or loneliness. The headline of a bank, of an umbrella brand or of a social campaign fixes completely different readings.

## Questions

- When is it good to leave the picture open, without anchoring, and let the audience interpret it?
- Does a strongly connoted message work in an international campaign, for different cultures?
