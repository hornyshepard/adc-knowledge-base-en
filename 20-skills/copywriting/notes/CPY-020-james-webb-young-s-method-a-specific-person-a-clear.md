# James Webb Young's method: a specific person, a clear proposition and the point where they meet

> **The idea in one sentence:** Young wrote starting from a very precise picture of the person he was talking to and of the proposition he was making, then looked for the headline and the illustration exactly where that person's interests meet the proposition.

## Explanation

In a 1948 letter to Watkins, James Webb Young (JWT, author of the Odorono ad and of the necktie ad that sold more than 26,000 ties from one *Life* page) describes his process. He says he learned to write copy selling books by mail to Methodist ministers, and that since then he has believed two things: people read ads, and they respond if you make a complete sales canvass.

| Step | What he does |
|---|---|
| 1 | gets in mind "a very sharp conception of the kind of person I am going to talk to" |
| 2 | formulates the proposition: why the person should buy something, think something or do something |
| 3 | looks for the place where the person's interests or problems and the proposition come together |
| 4 | from that point builds the headline and the main illustration as one unit; writes many headlines, tests where he can |
| 5 | does not start writing until he has seen the completed ad in his mind; then the first draft usually stands, with few revisions |
| 6 | while writing, keeps asking: Have I made this clear? What doubt will he have here? Have I made this interesting? |

Other ads in the collection confirm the first step. Leo McGivena sold advertising for the *New York Daily News* by personalizing the whole circulation as one family, the Sweeneys: married early, a good-sized family, ambitious, believers in God, the United States and life insurance. Carl Spier (BBDO) wrote the Christmas ad for Hamilton after 40 or 50 attempts, asking himself what he would write to his own wife; the copy came out in seven minutes. Max Sackheim wrote the Sherwin Cody ad for himself, a man with no formal education who worried about his own mistakes in English.

## Application

- Before the first headline, write a paragraph about a specific person (name, age, what worries them), not about a "target".
- State the proposition in one sentence; if you can't, you are not ready to write.
- Look for the intersection: not "what I want to say", but "what this person cares about, linked to what I offer".

## Example

The "Tell It to Sweeney" series (from 1922) persuaded conservative advertisers that the masses buy more than the classes: the paper's advertising grew from five million lines (1924) to twenty million (1937).
