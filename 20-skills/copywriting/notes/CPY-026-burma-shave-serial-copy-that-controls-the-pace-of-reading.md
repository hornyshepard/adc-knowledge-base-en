# Burma-Shave: serial copy that controls the pace of reading and turns the road into a game

> **The idea in one sentence:** by splitting a funny rhyme across six small signs set apart along the roadside, Burma-Shave controlled the reading speed and the pauses and made the ad a family ritual, with a contest that foreshadowed user-generated content.

## Explanation

Richards says this chapter in sign history deserves special attention, because the signs were a notable part of culture. Burma-Shave (1925) was the Burma-Vita company's 143rd shaving cream formula; its owners had tried, without much success, giving out samples that the customer paid for only if they liked them. Allan Odell saw a gas station's serial signs (gas, oil, restrooms…) and realized he always read every one of them. Advertising people told him it would never work.

The first signs, on secondhand boards, had no rhymes: four simple statements and the brand name. They worked, so Odell and his father became copywriters. The difference came from the tone: in an era of serious, long, hard-sell copy, they went for humor and irreverence. A six-sign series became the default: a jingle cadence, with the pauses controlled by the distance between the signs. Families read them aloud, in unison, and laughed.

By 1932, when they ran low on ideas, the company sponsored annual verse contests, paying $100 for every jingle used. For some contests entries exceeded fifty thousand, sorted by hired copywriters. Richards calls it the ancestor of the Doritos "Crash the Super Bowl" contest (2006–2016). The brand was then allowed to run jingles that had nothing to do with shaving, for example about highway safety. There were eventually about seven thousand sets of signs in forty-five states.

The format died with its context: the Interstate Highway System (1956) raised travel speeds and the small signs became less readable. After Philip Morris bought the company (1963), the serials ended.

## Application

- Think of the medium as time, not only as space: how many seconds does the reader have, and how do you divide them?
- A series (carousel, stories, successive signs) creates suspense: the last part is the "payoff" for the attention.
- When you run out of ideas, the public can write for you, if the format is simple and clear.

## Example

One of Odell's safety jingles (1935) reminded drivers to keep to the right of the oncoming car, then ended with close shaves from the half-pound jar.

## Questions

- What is the Burma-Shave equivalent on social media, where the user controls the scrolling speed?
- Why did a brilliant format disappear only because of traffic speed?
