# Disrupt, then reframe: a small oddity in how the price is stated, followed by a persuasive conclusion, increases sales

> **The idea in one sentence:** a slightly odd wording ("300 pennies… that's $3") distracts the audience from counterarguments for a moment, and the sentence that follows at once ("It's a bargain!") is accepted as a simple cue.

## Explanation

The disrupt-then-reframe (DTR) technique was described by Davis and Knowles (1999) and studied extensively by Fennis and his colleagues. The structure:

1. **The disruption**: an odd element in the standard script, usually the price stated in an unusual way ("This package of cards sells for 300 pennies…").
2. **The pause**: about two seconds.
3. **The reframe**: a persuasive conclusion ("…That's $3. It's a bargain!").

Results reported in the book:

- Selling Christmas cards for a charity: purchase rates 1.5 to 2 times higher. Only the combination works: neither the disruption alone, nor the reframe alone, nor the reverse order.
- The "Dayzers" lottery tickets: 43% bought with DTR, against 25% without. DTR reduced the number of objections voiced, and this explained the effect.
- The petition for higher tuition fees: DTR amplified the effect of the preceding series of questions (90% vs. 37% signatures).
- Kardes et al. (2007): the effect is greater in people with a high need for cognitive closure; the confusion creates the need for a conclusion, which the reframe provides.
- Replications in Poland and France and a meta-analysis (Carpenter & Boster, 2009) confirm the robustness of the effect.

The mechanism: the disruption works as a gentle distraction (without "awakening" the person into careful scrutiny), and the reframe is a peripheral cue that closes the confusion.

## Application

- In sales, telemarketing or fundraising scripts, test unusual wordings of the price followed at once by a clear conclusion.
- In written copy, the effect is probably weaker (the reader can reread); run an A/B test.
- Use the technique with ethical caution: it works precisely because it reduces critical thinking.

## Example

"This package of cards sells for 300 pennies … That's $3. It's a bargain!" (Davis & Knowles, 1999).

## Questions

- What would be the visual equivalent of the disruption in a banner or on a product page?
- Is the technique acceptable for charitable causes but not for commercial sales?
