# A good slogan is repeatable, sets the brand apart and lasts for decades; the jingle gets it sung

> **The idea in one sentence:** the slogan was born together with the trademark, as the short form of the brand's difference, and it works only if it is repeated consistently; music carried the same principle into auditory memory.

## Explanation

Richards notes that slogans (*taglines*) took off in the second half of the nineteenth century, together with professional copywriting and trademark law. The first examples are the miracle cures and the politicians, then Kodak ("You Press the Button, We Do the Rest") and Ivory ("It Floats", 1891). Their functions, according to the author: they convey the brand's essence, differentiate it, make it memorable, sometimes touch emotion. The common condition: **memorability**, obtained through repetition.

Several origin stories contradict the idea that a slogan takes months of research. "Breakfast of Champions" (Wheaties, 1933) was hastily written for a ballpark sign. For Squibb, Raymond Rubicam covered dozens of yellow sheets with headlines and, at two in the morning, saw two fragments from different headlines that fit together: "The Priceless Ingredient of Every Product is the Honor and Integrity of its Maker." The phrase stayed on everything that carried the Squibb name. Steve Jobs called the "Here's to the Crazy Ones" script "shit", but the commercial and the "Think Different" slogan helped turn Apple around. Richards's conclusion: the client isn't always right.

**The jingle** is the descendant of the rhymed street cry. The first broadcast jingle (Wheaties, 1926) is said to have saved the brand from extinction. "I'd Like to Teach the World to Sing" (Coca-Cola, 1971, Bill Backer) became a hit without the brand name. Keith Reinhard wrote "Two all-beef patties…" to show the McDonald's client how silly a listing of contents would be, and the client loved it.

## Application

- Test the slogan on three criteria: can it be repeated without wearing out? Does it say something the competitor cannot say? Can it live for ten years?
- Look for it in the fragments already written: often it is a piece of a headline, not a new sentence.
- A distinctive sound or tune can become a brand identity as strong as the logo.

## Example

"Thirst Knows No Season" (Coca-Cola, 1922) was repeated until it became the public's attitude toward the product. Winter sales came to exceed the old summer sales (see [[HIS-016-great-campaigns-invented-consumption-occasions-coca-cola|HIS-016]]).

## Questions

- Does a fixed slogan still make sense when communication is personalized for each user?
- Why did jingles lose popularity in the twenty-first century, and why, according to Richards, will they come back?
