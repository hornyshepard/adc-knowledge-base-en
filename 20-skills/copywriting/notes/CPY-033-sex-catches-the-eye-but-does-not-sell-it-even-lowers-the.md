# Sex catches the eye but does not sell: it even lowers the attitude toward the brand, among men and women alike

> **The idea in one sentence:** the industry's belief that "sex sells" is not supported by the data: sexual imagery increases attention to the ad and memory of the ad, but not memory of the brand, and the attitude toward the brand goes down.

## Explanation

Fennis and Stroebe note that sexual imagery in magazine ads rose from 15 percent (1983) to 27 percent (2003), mainly through female models, and is most frequent in ads for health care, beauty, drugs/medicine, clothing, travel and entertainment (Reichert et al., 2012). It is measured by the degree of undress (demure dress, suggestive dress, partially clad, nude) and the type of physical contact.

The meta-analysis by Wirtz, Sparks and Zimbres (2018, 78 studies):

| Effect | Result |
|---|---|
| Ad recognition and recall | increases (d = .38) |
| Brand recognition and recall | not significant |
| Attitude toward the ad | neutral on average: positive for men, negative for women |
| Attitude toward the brand | **negative** for both genders |
| Purchase intention | no effect; slightly negative when the sexual appeal is irrelevant to the product |

The relevance of the imagery to the product improved ad recognition and recall, but not brand recall. The result is inconsistent with the dual mediation hypothesis: men's liking for the ad did not carry over to the brand. The authors cite David Ogilvy's warning, "The test is relevance": nudes for beauty products, not for a detergent or a cooking stove.

Limit: almost no study measured actual purchasing behavior; still, with no effect on brand memory and a negative brand attitude, a positive effect on sales is extremely unlikely.

## Application

- When someone proposes sexual imagery "to stand out", ask: what will people remember, the model or the brand?
- Use sensuality only where it is the product's actual benefit (perfume, underwear, cosmetics), and watch how women react.
- Look for other sources of salience (relevant humor, novelty) that do not penalize the brand.

## Example

Ogilvy's first ad, showing a naked woman for a cooking stove, which he himself called a mistake because it was irrelevant to the product (p. 230).

## Questions

- Why does "sex sells" persist in the industry, despite the data?
- How have cultural norms (what counts as "demure") changed since the studies of the 1980s?
