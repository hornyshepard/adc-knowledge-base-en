# The challenger wins by changing the game: a sporting comparison, self-irony and a refusal of superlatives

> **The idea in one sentence:** the small or number-two brands in Watkins's collection did not try to shout louder than the leader; they invited comparison, owned their position or made fun of the category's superlatives.

## Explanation

**Plymouth, "Look at All Three" (J. Stirling Getchell, 1932).** Plymouth was a very new and very small contender, and the whole country was waiting for the new Ford V-8, due out the same week. Getchell, invited only as a courtesy to the presentation of another agency's "orthodox" campaign, asked to try his own version at his own risk. The strategy: get Plymouth through the swinging door on the push of the suspense Ford had created. Walter Chrysler posed at night, with his foot on the bumper of a car, and the headline "Look at All Three Low Priced Cars Before You Buy" was boiled down to four words. The executives wanted to drop it because it invited inspection of competitors' products. Chrysler smacked his hand on the desk and demanded that it run. Getchell's partner credits the sportsmanship of the appeal and the forthright tone of the copy. Plymouth became a real contender overnight, and the agency got the account four months later.

**Stryker Soap ("No Phoolium").** A small California company in a field of Goliaths: the competitors had already used every superlative, so its only chance was to be different. Watkins recounts that, years before, an executive at a large soap company had told him that if he were a small soap company this is exactly what he would do: "pan the hell out of the big fellows advertising."

**Southern Pacific (1946).** "A Short Course in Railroading for Airline Executives" answered the airlines' misleading price comparisons with good humor. Shortly after it appeared, the comparative advertising stopped.

Richards adds the canonical example: Avis, number two behind Hertz, turned its position into a promise: it tries harder. And Volkswagen made the small car an argument (see [[HIS-013-the-creative-revolution-of-1959-put-the-copywriter-and-the|HIS-013]]).

## Application

- If you are small, do not imitate the leader's language; change the evaluation criterion (comparison, honesty, humor).
- A direct comparison requires confidence in the product: invite it only if you can win, or at least come out with honor.
- Use the attention the leader creates (its launch, its campaign) as a ramp for yourself.

## Example

El Al (DDB, 1958) proclaimed that the Atlantic was 20% smaller, and Watkins calls it a "Look at All Three" for airlines.

## Questions

- When does a direct comparison help the leader more than the challenger?
- Is "we're number two" a strategy that can last after you become the leader?
