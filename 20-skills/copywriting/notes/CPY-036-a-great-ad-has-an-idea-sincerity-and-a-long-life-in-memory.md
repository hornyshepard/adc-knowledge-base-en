# A great ad has an idea, sincerity and a long life in memory

> **The idea in one sentence:** for Watkins and Rubicam, an ad is not "great" just because it sold; it needs an idea that cannot be buried, a sincerity you can feel, and to be remembered for a long time by the public and by the trade.

## Explanation

Watkins admits that no one can objectively establish the 100 greatest ads. To choose, he uses two juries: the people who read (or do not read) advertisements, and **time**. He finds that the ads the professionals voted for differ enormously in concept and execution. Only two things unite them: **an idea**, "an idea, or concept, that can't be buried regardless of presentation", and **sincerity**, the belief that reaches from the page to the reader. Beauty, he adds, is not the condition; in fact, the reverse is often true.

Raymond Rubicam, in the foreword, proposes a three-step test:

1. The results have to be **phenomenal**, not merely successful.
2. The message has to be made memorable by originality, wit, insight or conviction.
3. The ad has to be **honest** and must not impair the customer's good will toward the advertiser or the public's good will toward advertising.

Roy Durstine (BBDO), quoted with Barton's ads, explains sincerity: two ads may be as different as a subway guard and an Episcopal bishop and both work. If a man is sincere, you can forgive him almost anything. A lack of sincerity can ruin the best materials.

Watkins's examples: "The Greatest Reason in the World" (Travelers), written by a member of the client's publicity staff, not by the agency; "We Hope We Set a Boy to Dreaming" (General Motors), written after the copywriter caught the client's vision; "The Kid in Upper 4". All of them drew letters and requests for reprints for years.

## Application

- Judge a piece of work in three steps: what idea does it have? Is it sincere (does someone believe in it)? Will it be remembered a year from now?
- If you do not believe in the product, look for what is true in it or turn down the brief; the public senses a lack of conviction.
- Results without honesty do not make a great ad: they damage trust in all advertising.

## Example

Hayward Anderson (Kudner), who wrote the GM ad, says that moments of sincere inspiration are rare and generally cannot be repeated deliberately; the later requests to "do another boy ad" never worked as well.

## Questions

- Can an ad be great without having sold (or without our knowing whether it sold)?
- Is sincerity compatible with a brief written by someone else for a product that does not interest you?
