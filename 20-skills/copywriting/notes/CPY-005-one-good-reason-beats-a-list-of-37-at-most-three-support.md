# One good reason beats a list of 37: at most three support points, because the weakest becomes the target of counterarguments

> **The idea in one sentence:** each argument added after the first two or three does not add strength but gives the receiver a target that is easier to dispute; a message with a single promise and a few solid proofs convinces better.

## Explanation

Milavsky's parable, "the Nobel Prize winning creative strategy" (a pun on *No Bell*): a bishop asks why the church bells were not rung for his arrival. The priest pulls out a list of 37 reasons, and the first is "we have no bells". The bishop stops him: "One good reason is worth more than the other 36."

The psychological logic:

- **The limit of three.** The fast brain easily holds at most three items (Miller). After the third, the points get weaker and weaker.
- **Counterarguing.** If the promise conflicts with people's beliefs, they do not argue with the promise but with the weakest support point, and in this way reject all of it.

Rules for the strategy: **one promise** (a simple sentence), **at most three support points**, all linked directly to the promise and all present later in the ad. Specific support points (an exclusive recipe, "23 herbs and spices", a proprietary ingredient, independent evaluators) are more credible than popularity or "the business is family owned", unless those connect to a quality superiority.

Other authors get here by another road. Fennis and Stroebe (SRC-012) show that people asked to come up with ten reasons are less persuaded than those who come up with one, because the difficulty is read as a lack of arguments. McTigue and Rucker ask for the same thing in the brief: one benefit, not five.

## Application

- Put the support points in order of strength and cut everything after the third. Then ask yourself whether the third is not the one a skeptic would attack.
- In "bingo card" retail ads (dozens of offers, fonts and conditions), choose a single defining difference from competitors and build on it.
- Milavsky's metaphor: Michelangelo frees the statue from the block by chipping away the extra stone; in strategy, the hardest part is what you leave out.

## Example

Looking at the newspaper ad of a prominent auto dealer, Milavsky and his team stopped counting the fonts and the claims: the languages the staff spoke, illegible descriptions, loan conditions with footnotes. The real message was "we specialize in everything", which means in nothing.

## Questions

- Are there categories (B2B technology, pharmaceuticals) in which the buyer does want a long list of arguments?
- How do you apply the principle to a website or a product page, where space seems unlimited?
