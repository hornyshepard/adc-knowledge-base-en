# The world wars turned advertising from a tool of commerce into a weapon of public opinion

> **The idea in one sentence:** the war campaigns proved that advertising can sell ideas and behaviors, not only products, and the industry came out of them more respected, better organized and with a permanent institution for public service advertising.

## Explanation

In World War I, the United States created the Committee on Public Information (the Creel Commission). Its Division of Pictorial Publicity mobilized three hundred volunteer artists, who produced 1,484 ad designs in nineteen months. James Montgomery Flagg's poster of Uncle Sam pointing at the viewer (1917, after a 1914 British poster with Lord Kitchener) was considered one of the war's most successful designs. The ads did more than recruit: they asked people to conserve resources, buy bonds and work harder. Richards cites Wood's conclusion about advertising after the war: "It was better, it was bigger, it was reputable."

In World War II, the industry itself pushed for the War Advertising Council (1942). In its first year alone it produced hundreds of times as much free advertising as the CPI did in all of the previous war. After 1945 it became the **Ad Council**, which still exists. Watkins (1949) lists its postwar results: the Red Cross goal 99% subscribed, thousands of lives saved by the traffic safety campaigns, a 20% reduction in man-caused forest fires.

The dark side: the same techniques demonized the enemy, and under Goebbels German advertising was under total state control, which tarnished the word "propaganda".

## Application

- Social campaigns need the same tools as commercial ones: a simple idea, a strong picture, a clear call to action.
- When a brand ties itself to a national cause, the gain in reputation is real, but so is the risk of opportunism.

## Example

"The Kid in Upper 4" (New Haven Railroad, 1942) set out to stop passengers complaining about crowded trains. The copywriter, Nelson Metcalf, saw that ads with military transport statistics moved no one. He reworked a personal story, about a young recruit in the upper berth on his way to war, 25 or 30 times. The ad was read on radio programs and made into a song and a movie short; more than 8,000 letters came in.

## Questions

- Where is the line between public interest advertising and propaganda?
- Why did it take a war for advertising to gain legitimacy?
