# As long as people could not read, advertising was image, symbol and voice

> **The idea in one sentence:** the literacy of the public decided the form of early advertising; where text was not read, communication went through the emblem, the visual pun and sound.

## Explanation

Richards cites an estimate of literacy in the Roman Empire at about 15%, and after its fall it was even lower. For almost a millennium, a written ad reached only the elites and the clergy. That is why, if you compare the ratio of text to image in an old ad, you can guess its target: Caxton's printed poster (c. 1477) was aimed at the clergy, while inn signs were aimed at everyone.

The repertoire for those who could not read was rich:

| Means | Example from the book |
|---|---|
| The emblem of the trade | the ivy bush for wine, the boot for the cobbler, the barber's red-and-white pole |
| A distinctive animal or object | the white rabbit of Jinan Liu's needle shop (12th–13th century), the earliest known non-humanoid advertising character |
| The visual rebus | the name Hancock shown as a hand and a rooster |
| The voice | criers, organized in France as early as 1141, who would "cry the wine" and hand out samples |
| Family names | Miller, Baker, Carpenter: a person's name announced their trade |

At first, signs had a purely informative function: they helped customers find the workshop before streets were numbered (18th century). Only competition made them persuasive: merchants began to compete with ever larger signs, until England had to limit them by law.

## Application

- For audiences who do not read the ad (a driver in traffic, fast scrolling, children, multilingual markets), think first in symbol, color and sound, then in text.
- Test whether your identity can be recognized without the logotype: a sign, a shape, a sound.
- The rebus and visual play are old memory mechanisms; they are worth using deliberately, not just as ornament.

## Example

The barber pole was born after a law of 1307 banned barbers from putting bowls of their clients' blood in their windows; the red-and-white stripes represented bloody bandages hung out to dry. A functional symbol became, over time, the logo of an entire profession.

## Questions

- What is today's equivalent of the public's "illiteracy": lack of time, of attention, of a shared language?
- When a symbol becomes generic for a category (the barber pole), does it still help a particular brand?
