# Invented characters never misbehave; celebrities do

> **The idea in one sentence:** a brand character (cartoon, animal, fictional person) gives the brand a personality for decades without risk, while a celebrity brings attention at once but can pass its scandals on to the brand.

## Explanation

Richards defines trade characters as any figure, human, animal, cartoon or even an inanimate object, used to represent a brand and lend it a personality. He finds them as early as the rabbit of Jinan Liu's needle shop (around 1200 CE). The modern difference: print, then radio and TV gave them consistency, a voice and movement, and trademark law made them protectable.

Watkins collects their life stories. Nipper, the dog in *His Master's Voice*, was the real dog of the painter Francis Barraud, acquired by Victor in 1901; Watkins's moral is that good ideas are everywhere, but you have to know one when you see it. Phoebe Snow, created by Calkins for a railroad that burned clean anthracite, always wore a spotless white gown. The painting of the Fisk boy ("Time to Re-tire") was spoiled by successive retouchings until a restoration at the Metropolitan Museum of Art. Borden's Elsie the cow made her debut in medical journals (1936), and 99% of housewives recognized Betty Crocker. All of them are "secondary spokesmen" who say what the company could not say as warmly.

Endorsement follows a different logic. Lillie Langtry for Pears (1882) and Gillette's athletes (1910) borrowed fame, and "Where's the beef?" pushed Wendy's profits up 31 percent. But Richards lists the failures: O. J. Simpson, the most popular athlete spokesman (Hertz), charged with murder in 1994; Jared Fogle (Subway), convicted; Tiger Woods. His conclusion: it is a wonder there is not greater reliance on cartoon characters, which never age and "can't easily go rogue".

## Application

- Choose the celebrity for quick impact, the character for long-term brand equity.
- Contracts with celebrities need morals clauses and an exit plan.
- Keep the character up to date without changing its essence; successive "improvements" spoiled the Fisk boy.

## Example

Mr. Friendly (American Mutual, 1947), a tall, skinny fellow in a derby hat drawn by Jack Tinker, replaced the widows and burning houses of insurance advertising. The company rose from fourth to second place in sales.

## Questions

- Can virtual (CGI) influencers combine the advantages of both models?
- What do you do with a character built on a stereotype (Aunt Jemima, Rastus)?
