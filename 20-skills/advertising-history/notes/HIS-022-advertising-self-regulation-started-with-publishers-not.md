# Advertising self-regulation started with publishers, not with agencies

> **The idea in one sentence:** the first to filter out dishonest ads were the publications protecting their readers; the industry followed only when its own legitimacy was at stake.

## Explanation

Richards reconstructs an order that contradicts the trade's pride. In 1860 Orange Judd, publisher of *American Agriculturist*, announced that he would exclude deceptive ads and refund readers who were cheated. In 1880 the *Farm Journal* printed a "Fair Play" notice in every issue. The farm press, then the *Ladies' Home Journal* (1892), *Good Housekeeping* (a guarantee in 1902, the Seal of Approval in 1909) and the newspapers built the first filters. The **Curtis Advertising Code** (1910) is the first full set of standards, a prototype for the clearance policies of other publications.

Only then did the industry move. N. W. Ayer abandoned its remaining patent medicine accounts in 1905. The 1911 "Truth Conference" was followed by the adoption of the slogan "Truth in Advertising". *Printers' Ink* proposed a model statute, adopted by about half the states. In 1921 the vigilance committees became the Better Business Bureau. The AAAA wrote its code in 1924, the International Chamber of Commerce in 1937.

The second big stage came under pressure from critics (the Nader Report, 1969): in 1971 the American associations created the **NARB** and the National Advertising Division, inspired by Britain's **ASA** (1962), where ads ruled against could be refused by the media. The advantage of self-regulation, the author says, is that it can reach where the law cannot, for example matters of taste or offensive ads, which are protected by free speech. Its weakness is enforcement. His conclusion is severe: in the 20th century, "media, not the advertising industry, probably had the greatest impact" in limiting unethical practices.

## Application

- Before launch, check the ad against the local self-regulation code too (in Romania, the RAC Code), not only against the law.
- A medium has the right to refuse an ad; a brief that ignores the media's clearance policies puts the execution at risk.
- When a client asks for a bold claim, ask: would it get past an editor who protects the readers?

## Example

In 2010 the AAF set up the Institute for Advertising Ethics, with eight principles: truth, a clear distinction between advertising and editorial content, disclosure of payments to endorsers, respect for privacy and the team's right to raise ethical concerns. Richards notes that it took a full century from the Truth-in-Advertising movement to a general set of principles for the industry.

## Questions

- Can an industry regulate itself credibly when the jurors are the advertisers themselves?
- What "editor" role do the platforms (Meta, Google) play today when they reject ads?
