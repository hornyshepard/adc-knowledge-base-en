# The American standard for deception: a claim must be substantiated before it is published

> **The idea in one sentence:** American law arrived at the rule that an ad is deceptive if it is likely to mislead a consumer acting reasonably on a point that matters, and the proof must exist when the claim is made, not afterwards.

## Explanation

Richards, a lawyer by training, shows how slowly the protection was built. In *Chandler v. Lopus* (England, 1603), a seller of a fake "bezar stone" got off because he had only made an affirmation of fact, not a warranty. For the next couple of hundred years, a claim created no liability unless one of three words was used: warranty, promise or guarantee.

The modern stages:

| Stage | What it brought |
|---|---|
| FTC Act, 1914 | Bans "unfair methods of competition"; the FTC applies the rule to advertising too. |
| Wheeler-Lea, 1938 | Adds unfair or deceptive acts or practices, so the protection covers consumers, not only competitors. |
| Pfizer, 1972 | **Substantiation**: the advertiser must have the proof in hand when the claim is made. |
| 1970s | **Corrective advertising**: besides stopping the ad, the advertiser can be required to repair the misimpression. |
| The 1983 statement | Deception = a representation or omission that is likely to mislead consumers acting reasonably and that is **material**, meaning it affects their choice. |

On the other side, the Supreme Court gradually extended free speech protection to commercial advertising (*Virginia Pharmacy*, 1976). The *Central Hudson* test (1980) allows restrictions only if the speech is misleading or if the government has a substantial interest, the regulation directly advances it and the restriction is not more extensive than necessary.

## Application

- For every factual claim in the copy ("the fastest", "twice as much"), ask for the file of proof before production, not after a complaint.
- Watch out for omissions: what you leave out can be as deceptive as what you say.
- Obvious exaggeration, of the "best coffee in the world" kind, is tolerated as puffery; a precise number is not.

## Example

The Liquormart ad with pictures of bottles and the word "WOW" (1996) was upheld by the Supreme Court: the state cannot keep true information from the public on the assumption that people will respond "irrationally" to it.
