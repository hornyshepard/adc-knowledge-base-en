# AIDA was invented by copywriters, not psychologists: the genealogy of effect models

> **The idea in one sentence:** the step models of "how advertising works" started from one copywriter's rules for writing around 1900 and were later extended by researchers, not the other way around.

## Explanation

Richards reconstructs the lineage:

| Year | Author | Model |
|---|---|---|
| before 1904 | E. St. Elmo Lewis, copywriter | good copy should (1) attract attention, (2) awaken interest, (3) create conviction |
| 1904 | Dukesmith et al. | attention, interest, desire, conviction |
| 1921 | C. P. Russell | attention, interest, desire, action (**AIDA**) |
| 1961 | Lavidge & Steiner | Hierarchy of Effects: awareness, knowledge, liking, preference, conviction, purchase |
| 1984 | Preston & Thorson | the Association Model, with twenty-nine steps |

At the same time, academic psychology was entering advertising. In 1895 Harlow Gale (Minnesota) performed the first psychological studies of advertising. In 1901 Walter Dill Scott gave a talk at Chicago's Agate Club on "involuntary attention", then wrote the first books on the psychology of advertising (1903, 1908). In 1904 he taught the first university course about advertising, and in 1909 he became the first professor of advertising. His fellow psychologists considered his work too "applied". J. B. Watson, the father of behaviorism, joined J. Walter Thompson in 1920.

The historical lesson: effect models began as checklists for writing and became theories about the consumer's mind. That is why they also have the limits of a checklist: they assume a linear sequence that does not necessarily describe real buying.

## Application

- Use AIDA as an execution checklist (does the ad have a stopping moment, a reason for interest, a desire, a clear action?), not as a description of the decision.
- For campaign planning, the finer hierarchies (Lavidge-Steiner) help set stage objectives.
- Remember the origin: a model created for print may not fit an impulse purchase or a habit brand.

## Example

Scott argued that man rarely reasons, so ads should tap emotions, the exact opposite of the reason-why fashion of Kennedy and Hopkins in the same period (see [[HIS-008-the-genealogy-of-copy-styles-news-reason-why-image-usp-big|HIS-008]]).

## Questions

- Which alternative (non-linear) model would better describe a purchase influenced by social media?
- Who would contradict AIDA? Think of routine purchases, where "desire" is missing.
