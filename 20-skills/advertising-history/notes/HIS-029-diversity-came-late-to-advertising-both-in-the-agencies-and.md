# Diversity came late to advertising: both in the agencies and in the picture the ads show

> **The idea in one sentence:** who makes the ads and who appears in them changed together and slowly; the stereotypes in the ads lasted as long as those groups were missing from the agencies and were not seen as a market.

## Explanation

**Women.** As early as the late 1800s women made 90 percent of purchases, so agencies began hiring women who might understand them. Helen Lansdowne (JWT, 1908) was the first female copywriter at a major agency, and Ruth Waldo supervised copy for the entire JWT network. Phyllis Robinson (DDB), Shirley Polykoff and Mary Wells followed, then Charlotte Beers and Shelly Lazarus, CEOs at Ogilvy. Even so, in 1931, 97 percent of agency staff were men, and in 1960 Leo Burnett had forty-five vice presidents, none of them women. In the ads, the image of women went from the 1951 Van Heusen ad, with a woman on her knees serving a man breakfast, to Dove's "Real Beauty" (2004) and #LikeAGirl (2015).

**African Americans.** In the 19th century they appeared almost only in slave notices, then on trade cards and in characters such as Aunt Jemima or Rastus, which offered White people a "slave in a box" (Bosarge). Watkins (1949) praises the Cream of Wheat chef without reservation, a sign of the times. The big agencies refused to hire for fear of their Southern clients. The pioneers were Vince Cullers (1956), Barbara Proctor (1970) and Tom Burrell, who insisted that "Black people are not dark skinned white people." In 1992 fewer than 1 percent of the creative staff in the top agencies were Black. Aunt Jemima was renamed only in 2020.

**Latino and LGBTQ+.** The Frito Bandito (1967) was withdrawn after protests from Mexican Americans. For the gay audience, the first major brand was Absolut (1981), the first TV commercial IKEA (1994). Subaru chose coded "winks" (license plates like "XENA LVR", taglines with double meanings), because its tests showed the audience preferred them to overt pitches.

## Application

- Homogeneous teams miss nuances the audience sees at once (Dove and Nivea, 2017). Check a concept with people from the group being represented.
- The stereotype makes production easier and can cost the brand: Corn Pops, the Frito Bandito, Aunt Jemima.
- Inclusion is also a market, but Reinhard warns that a social stance must be backed by action (Pepsi's failure with Kendall Jenner, 2017).

## Example

The Cheerios commercial with a mixed-race family (2013) drew so many racist comments on YouTube that comments were closed.

## Questions

- How much of the progress Richards describes is ethical conviction, and how much is market calculation?
- How do you represent a group without falling into either stereotype or tokenism?
