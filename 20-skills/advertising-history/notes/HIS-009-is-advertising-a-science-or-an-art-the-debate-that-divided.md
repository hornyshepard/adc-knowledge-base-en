# Is advertising a science or an art? The debate that divided the 20th century

> **The idea in one sentence:** from Hopkins to Bernbach, the profession split between those who believe an ad can be tested and optimized like an experiment and those who believe research kills the idea; history shows that both camps had great successes.

## Explanation

Richards (ch. 10–11) lines up the camps:

| "Science" | "Art" |
|---|---|
| Claude Hopkins, *Scientific Advertising* (1923): research, coupons, tests | Theodore MacManus: wrote what he, as a consumer, would want to know; rejected reason-why |
| Stanley Resor (JWT): laws of human behavior can be discovered scientifically; hired J. B. Watson (1920) | Bill Bernbach: "I warn you against believing that advertising is a science." |
| John Caples, Raymond Rubicam, George Gallup: testing the creative work | George Lois, Leo Burnett: research in second place |
| Daniel Starch, A. C. Nielsen: measuring readership and audience | Walter Weir: no one has yet made an ad by setting a research report in type |

Psychology came in early: Walter Dill Scott (Northwestern, 1901–1908) argued that man rarely reasons at all and that ads should tap emotions, contrary to the reason-why fashion. From 1939, Ernest Dichter brought qualitative motivation research.

Watkins gives examples for both camps. Phoenix Mutual pretested its coupon ads, and the Fisherman Advertisement produced nearly three times as many inquiries, and four times the sales, as the average of its predecessors. Marlboro, at the other pole, followed its instincts despite some negative findings in early copy testing.

## Application

- Use research to ask the right questions (see account planning) and to choose between options, not to generate the idea.
- Test what can be tested (headlines, offers, channels); defend what cannot yet be measured (the consistency of an identity).
- When a test contradicts your instinct, ask what exactly the test measured.

## Example

Richards also notes that the industry has now reached the "science" extreme: an algorithm tested 6,500 versions of a Google text ad in a single day. In the final chapter, Keith Reinhard warns that we must learn the difference between big data and a big idea.

## Questions

- If A/B testing is cheap and instant, is there still any point in defending the "art"?
- Which big ideas would not have passed a pretest (Lemon, Marlboro, 1984)?
