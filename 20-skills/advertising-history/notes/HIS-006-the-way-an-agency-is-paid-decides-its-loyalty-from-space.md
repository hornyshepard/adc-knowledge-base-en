# The way an agency is paid decides its loyalty: from space broker to fee-paid partner

> **The idea in one sentence:** the history of the agency is the history of one question, whose agent am I?, and each time the answer was given by the payment mechanism.

## Explanation

The first agencies (William Tayler, London 1786; Volney Palmer, Philadelphia 1841) were **newspaper space brokers**: they bought in bulk and resold, so they were in fact the newspapers' agents. Ralph Hower identifies four phases: agent for publishers, "space-jobbing", "space-wholesaling" (Rowell, c. 1865) and the "advertising concession agency", which also takes on liability for deadbeat advertisers. The client wrote its own ads.

The break comes in 1876 with N. W. Ayer's **open contract**: the agency becomes the advertiser's agent, negotiates the lowest rates and receives a percentage (10 percent, then 12.5 percent, then 15 percent). In 1887 American publishers introduced the commission paid by the medium, and Curtis (1919) fixed it at 15 percent, cutting off any agency that rebated part of it to the client. With loyalty moved to the client came the services: copywriting (Jem White as early as 1800), art departments (the 1890s), full service (Batten, 1891).

The commission, however, had a flaw: the agency earned more when the client spent more, no matter how many services it provided. Richards notes that TV was favored partly because it was expensive. In 1960, Ogilvy won Shell by proposing a fee, which he considered morally superior. IBM moved to performance-based pay in 1990. The internet dealt the final blow: producing websites and banners took a great deal of work on cheap space. A 2013 ANA study found the classic commission in only about 5 percent of agreements. In parallel, spinning media departments off into independent companies (the 1990s) took away from the creative agency the very source of its money.

## Application

- When you negotiate a collaboration, check what behavior the payment formula rewards: media volume, hours, results?
- A creative agency without media loses control over execution; ask to be involved in the media plan even if you do not buy it yourself.

## Example

When Nissan offered only 8 percent of billings in 1987 for a $150 million account, Ogilvy & Mather pulled out of the competition, with an argument that became a proverb: "If you pay peanuts, you get monkeys."

## Questions

- Does performance-based pay align interests, or does it push the agency toward goals that are easy to measure?
- What does the client lose when creative and media sit in different companies?
