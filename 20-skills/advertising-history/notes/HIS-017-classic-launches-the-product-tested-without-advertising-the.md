# Classic launches: the product tested without advertising, the teaser and the feint (Camel, Ford Model A, Lux)

> **The idea in one sentence:** three historic launches show three rules that still hold: first check that the product sells on its own, build curiosity in stages, and control what the competition sees.

## Explanation

**Camel (R. J. Reynolds and N. W. Ayer, 1913).** Reynolds offers $250,000 to introduce the brand. The account man, William Armistead, refuses the money: the people around the company who praise the blend may say so to please the boss, and if the public does not like it, the advertising will only kill the brand faster. His recommendation: one carton on the counter of each of 125 of the best stores in Cleveland, with figures on repeat orders. The cigarette repeats on its own, region after region; only then does advertising come in. The campaign opens with teasers in newspapers: first "The Camels Are Coming", then that tomorrow there will be more camels in town than in Asia and Africa combined, then "Camels Are Here". In five years Camel grows from 4th place to 1st, with about 40% of the cigarette business.

**Ford Model A (Ayer, 1927).** Ford had lost sales leadership. The campaign built suspense over five full-page ads: first a page signed by Henry Ford, with no picture of the car, then the general description, then the specifications, and only then the pictures and prices. On the first day more than 10,000,000 people came to see the car, and within a few weeks orders passed 800,000. Edsel Ford approved about $1,500,000 of advertising in less than forty minutes, with a single change: "correct" instead of "perfect".

**Lux Toilet Soap (Lever Brothers and JWT, 1920s).** While it tested Lux in two Massachusetts towns, Lever loudly tested a green soap, OLVA, in three other cities, with posters changed every month and extravagant sampling. The competition sent its sleuths there. Meanwhile, Lux went quietly into most of the stores in most of the cities throughout the nation.

## Application

- Before the launch budget, ask for proof that the product gets repeat purchases without advertising support.
- A teaser works when there is already curiosity or a real question in the market; otherwise it is just a delayed announcement.
- A restrained tone ("correct", not "perfect") is credible precisely when expectations are huge.

## Example

The slogan "I'd Walk a Mile for a Camel" came from what a stranger said to a sign painter who had given him a cigarette (see [[CPY-007-the-best-headlines-are-often-found-not-invented-listen-to|CPY-007]]).

## Questions

- What would the "carton on the counter" test be today for a digital product?
- Is an OLVA-style feint ethical, or is it just competitive strategy?
