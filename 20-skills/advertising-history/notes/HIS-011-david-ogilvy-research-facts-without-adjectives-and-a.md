# David Ogilvy: research, facts without adjectives and a picture that asks a question

> **The idea in one sentence:** Ogilvy combined the discipline of research (learned at Gallup) with a memorable visual detail and factual copy, and his briefs start from the real obstacles in the buyer's mind.

## Explanation

Richards summarizes the path: a door-to-door salesman of AGA cooking stoves in Scotland, the author of an instruction manual that reached Mather & Crowther, a spell at George Gallup's Audience Research Institute, service in British Intelligence. In 1948, with $6,000 and a name lent by Mather & Crowther, he opened in New York the agency that would become Ogilvy & Mather. He was vice president of research before he was the creative star. He also broke the commission system, winning Shell in 1960 with a fixed fee (see [[HIS-006-the-way-an-agency-is-paid-decides-its-loyalty-from-space|HIS-006]]).

Two cases from Watkins show the method:

- **Hathaway (1951).** On the drive to the photo shoot, Ogilvy bought an eye patch and had the model wear it, to make him look more interesting. With an average space budget of $62,000 a year, sales grew from $5 million to $13 million in eight years. Ogilvy himself deplored the school of visual gimmicks he had inspired: pictures do not sell without hard-selling copy underneath them.
- **Rolls-Royce (1958).** The budget was less than two percent of Cadillac's. The brief sent to the client named two problems: **ignorance** (Americans believed a Rolls-Royce still cost $20,000 and needed a chauffeur) and **inverted snobbery** (the rich American fears looking ostentatious). The answer: many facts, set down austerely, without adjectives, and "no baronial halls or footmen" in the pictures. The headline about the electric clock, the loudest noise in the car at 60 miles an hour, was met by the senior Rolls-Royce executive with the remark that they really must do something to improve the clock.

## Application

- Write briefs that name the obstacles of perception, not only the benefits.
- An unexpected visual detail makes people ask "what happened here?"; use it to pull the eye toward the copy, not to replace it.
- In luxury categories, precise facts are more persuasive than superlatives.

## Example

The Hathaway client, Ellerton Jette, promised Ogilvy that he would never fire him and never change a word of his copy. Both promises were kept, and Watkins sees in this a condition of the campaign: the client's consistency (see [[CPY-038-consistency-beats-novelty-do-not-change-an-ad-that-still|CPY-038]]).

## Questions

- Does the success of the eye patch contradict Ogilvy's thesis that the picture does not sell on its own?
- What does "facts without adjectives" mean in a 10-second post?
