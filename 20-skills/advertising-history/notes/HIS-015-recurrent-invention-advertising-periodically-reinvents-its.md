# Recurrent invention: advertising periodically reinvents its strategic mechanisms under new names

> **The idea in one sentence:** many of the agencies' "revolutionary methodologies" are old ideas with a new name, created because the industry has to sell its differentiation; a good strategist recognizes the source.

## Explanation

Fernández Gómez builds the whole book in a demystifying tone. The key example: the **dominant idea**. In the 1910s, McJunkin and Finn (McJunkin Advertising Company, Chicago) published *Master Merchandising and the Dominant Idea*, which argues that a campaign not based on a distinct, interesting central idea falls short of its possibilities. The dominant idea is the one distinctive major selling feature of a business, as free as possible of confusing minors. A comparison with Reeves's USP (1961) shows the same three rules: a well-defined proposition, uniqueness ("A business without individuality gets nowhere nowadays."), selling power. Sabaté et al. call the phenomenon **recurrent invention**: USP → ESP → MSP, the same thing invented and reinvented as many times as required.

The same pattern appears in the emotional paradigm: brand image (1950s–1960s) → star strategy (1980s) → Lovemarks and Passionbrands (2004–2005). Kapferer points out the paradox: the more brands lose market share, the more books are published about love, passion and emotion.

The author's explanation is economic. Agencies have to justify the value of an intangible "product", so they differentiate themselves through philosophies, acronyms and new roles. The author deliberately leaves aside the in-house methodologies that never left the agencies: the T-Plan (JWT), ROI (DDB), the creative work plan (Y&R), brand character (Grey), belief dynamics (DMB&B), UPGA (Unilever). He considers them useful in professional practice, but often just names with no new content.

## Application

- When you are handed a "proprietary" framework, look for its ancestor: which classic mechanism does it reword? What does it actually add?
- In a pitch, you can use a new name for your method, but ground it in a proven idea, not in how it sounds.
- Read the primary sources (Hopkins, Reeves, Gardner & Levy): many "novelties" are already there.

## Example

McJunkin and Finn already wrote that advertising may make the first sale, but never the second: the product must do that. The formula still circulates today, usually without anyone knowing its authors.

## Questions

- Is repackaging always negative? A new name can make an old idea usable by a new generation.
- How do you tell a real evolution (for example, iconic brands) from a mere relabeling?
