# Account planning moved research from answers to questions

> **The idea in one sentence:** account planning was born when Stanley Pollitt saw that research departments gave correct answers to the wrong questions, and proposed a consumer expert who would decide what actually needed to be found out.

## Explanation

Richards describes a path in three stages. First, quantitative research: the first market survey by an agency (N. W. Ayer, 1879, for a threshing machine account), copy testing from the 1920s, the Starch method, Nielsen audits and Gallup. Then Ernest Dichter's qualitative research (from 1939): in-depth interviews, projective techniques, observation. Then, in 1965, the change of role.

At Pritchard Wood & Partners (London), Stanley Pollitt saw the weakness of the model: the campaign team sent questions to research, but they could be the wrong questions, or even questions designed to support decisions already made. He proposed a consumer "expert" who, through in-depth understanding of consumers, would determine which questions needed to be answered. At the same time and independently, Stephen King was working on the same idea at JWT London; Tony Stead (JWT) coined the term "account planning" (1967). In 1968 Pollitt founded Boase Massimi Pollitt, the first agency designed around planning.

It spread slowly: by 1985, eighteen of the top twenty UK agencies had account planning departments. Jane Newman took the method to Chiat/Day (1982). The big US agencies adopted it only in the early 1990s, often merely renaming the research department and hiring nearly anyone with a British accent. All the variants (strategic planning, strategic account planning) look for the same thing: a consumer **insight** to drive sales.

## Application

- Before you commission research, write down which decision you want to make with the result; if no decision depends on it, do not commission it.
- The planner represents the consumer inside the agency; the creative brief is the planner's main product.
- Combine quantitative data with qualitative observation: the numbers say what is happening, the interviews say why.

## Example

George Gallup told John O'Toole that market research "really began" when Lasker sent a large part of his agency's staff to knock on doors in a test-market town in Indiana. Sales of a canned evaporated milk had dropped to almost nothing after the first weeks, and the housewives gave the cause: a slight almond taste, easily corrected.

## Questions

- If behavioral data is endless today, why do we still need a planner?
- Is planning at risk of becoming just a layer of justification for ideas already sold?
