# Shockvertising: attention won through scandal has a price, and the brand pays it

> **The idea in one sentence:** campaigns that shock on purpose (Benetton, FCUK) won global awareness on small budgets, but they moved the debate from the product to taste and ethics, and the brand remains the prisoner of its author.

## Explanation

Richards counts Oliviero Toscani among the historic figures of advertising for what he did at Benetton (hired in 1982). His early work was unremarkable; then he broke the molds of fashion brands with provocative, sometimes offensive images. The book's list: a Black woman breastfeeding a White baby (1989); the hands of two men, one White and one Black, handcuffed together, leaving viewers to decide which was the prisoner; a family with their son dying of AIDS; an albino Zulu woman stared at by her village. In 2011 the UNHATE campaign showed world leaders kissing; the image of Pope Benedict XVI and an Egyptian imam was condemned by the Vatican.

In the United Kingdom, Trevor Beattie ran a campaign for French Connection in 1991 with the headline "FCUK Fashion!". His response to critics was that his ads were not shocking: what was shocking was the mediocrity of most British advertising, which makes anything remotely different stand out.

Richards presents the phenomenon as proof that taste and ethics evolve: the first condom commercial in the United States came only in 1975, the first with a live model wearing a bra in 1987. Still, Toscani's career shows the risk: back at Benetton in 2017, he was fired in 2020 after an offensive remark about a bridge collapse that had killed forty-three people.

## Application

- Ask: does the shock serve the brand's idea or only attention? Benetton tied provocation to a message (unity beyond race), FCUK only to a play on letters.
- Count the cost: boycotts, bans by self-regulatory bodies, dependence on a single creative personality.
- Tell courage from gratuitous provocation; the first builds, the second uses up capital of trust.

## Example

The UNHATE image of Benjamin Netanyahu and Mahmoud Abbas kissing was displayed on an enormous billboard in Tel Aviv, to the displeasure of many.

## Questions

- Does shock still work in a scrolling culture, where everything tries to shock?
- Who has the right to decide what is "too much": the law, self-regulation or the public?
