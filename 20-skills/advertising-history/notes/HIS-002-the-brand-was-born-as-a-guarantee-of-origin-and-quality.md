# The brand was born as a guarantee of origin and quality, when the maker moved away from the buyer

> **The idea in one sentence:** from the potter's mark to the Uneeda package, the mark appeared every time buyers could no longer see who had made the product and needed a sign they could trust.

## Explanation

Richards traces an unbroken line. The potters' marks of the Near East (5000–4000 BCE) were probably used to identify the vessels in a shared kiln; only with trade did they evolve into something more like an ad for the potter. Moore and Reid argue that all brands carry two kinds of information: **origin** and **quality**; the seals of the Indus Valley, stamped on crates, carried both. Medieval heraldry took the idea further: the coat of arms distinguished friend from foe, and today's logos are its direct descendants.

As soon as printing made copying possible, counterfeiting appeared too. In 1478, the Bologna printer Benedict Hector warned his buyers to look for his sign, because other printers were putting his name on their faulty books. The formula "beware of counterfeits" then turns up in toothpaste ads in 1660.

The decisive step comes with the Industrial Revolution. Mechanized production and the railroad put the flour and butter of producers hundreds of miles away on the same shelf. That is when the brand became necessary, and trademark laws (France 1857, the US 1870, England 1875) made it protectable. In 1851 Babbitt was the first to sell soap in branded wrappers. In 1899 Uneeda Biscuit took crackers out of the cracker barrel and into a sealed 5¢ package (Watkins). The Smith brothers put their portraits on the package to set themselves apart from knockoffs, with "Trade" and "Mark" under the faces.

## Application

- When you build a new brand, answer the two old questions first: where does the product come from, and why can I trust its quality?
- Visual identifiers (a sign, a color, a character) work as a signature against confusion, not as decoration.
- The package is the brand's first medium; treat it like an ad.

## Example

Charles E. Hires (1893) went straight to retailers with arguments for his root beer extract (a *push* strategy). Others urged consumers to demand the brand in stores (*pull*). The brand changed the balance of power between producer, retailer and buyer.

## Questions

- Does origin still carry the same weight in a world of private labels and marketplaces?
- Where does the guarantee of quality end and the brand's "aura" begin?
