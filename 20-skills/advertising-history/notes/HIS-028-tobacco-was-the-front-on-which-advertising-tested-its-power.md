# Tobacco was the front on which advertising tested its power and its limits

> **The idea in one sentence:** in tobacco, advertising showed it could create whole markets (women smokers, for example), and it was also here that society built the most complete set of limits: warnings, bans, contractual agreements.

## Explanation

Watkins, in 1949, looks at the cigarette campaigns with a craftsman's eye. Camel (1913) created the first national brand (see [[HIS-017-classic-launches-the-product-tested-without-advertising-the|HIS-017]]) and grew from 4th place to 1st in five years. Chesterfield's "Blow Some My Way" (1926) introduced the first woman in cigarette advertising, and Watkins calls them "perhaps the most important four words of copy ever written", for the market they opened. Lucky Strike's "Reach for a Lucky Instead of a Sweet", the idea of George W. Hill, who saw on the street a stout woman eating candy and a slender one smoking a cigarette, took American Tobacco's profits from $21 million to $46 million (1925–1931); many believe it created more women smokers than any other campaign.

Richards shows the other side. Between 1914 and 1928 the number of smokers increased by a factor of seven. The medical evidence (1927, 1936) was met with ads suggesting benefits for the throat (Philip Morris, 1937) or medical approval (Camel, 1946, with the doctors who supposedly preferred the brand). After the British report (1962) and the Surgeon General's report (1964), Bernbach and Ogilvy announced that their agencies were out of tobacco, and Emerson Foote quit McCann Erickson, which would not reject the accounts. Then came the warning on the pack (1965), the TV ban in the United Kingdom (1965) and the United States (1971), then Joe Camel, the cartoon character that six-year-olds knew as well as Mickey Mouse. Because the federal government kept running into the First Amendment, the states obtained a contract through the **Master Settlement Agreement** (1998): $206 billion, no targeting of children, no billboards, no major sponsorships.

## Application

- Choose your clients deliberately; Ogilvy's and Bernbach's refusal of tobacco was a decision about reputation.
- "Brilliant" ideas that create a new market must also be judged by their consequences, not only by sales.
- When the law cannot ban a message, a contract and self-regulation can.

## Example

The Camel billboard in Times Square (1943–1966) blew smoke rings (in fact steam rings) every four seconds. It was one of the city's attractions.

## Questions

- Do vaping, sports betting or ultra-processed foods today resemble the tobacco of the 1950s?
- Is it fair to read Watkins with hindsight, when he was writing before the clear medical evidence?
