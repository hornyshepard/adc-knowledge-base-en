# Miracle cures invented the techniques of modern advertising and ruined its reputation for a century

> **The idea in one sentence:** the sellers of miracle cures (patent medicines) were the first national advertisers, the most inventive and the most dishonest, and advertising paid for it with the public's distrust.

## Explanation

As early as 1652, an English writer accused the newsbooks of filling their pages with the ads of mountebanks. Defoe described the London of the Great Plague (1665) full of deceivers pushing pills and potions. In the 19th century these products, often unlabeled alcohol, cocaine or opium, came to account, according to Richards, for something like half of all American advertising after the Civil War; annual sales grew from $3.5 million to $75 million.

The paradox the author stresses: these very swindlers pushed the trade forward. They were the first to seek a national market, and they created almanacs, collectible trade cards, testimonials and eye-catching designs. P. T. Barnum, at the same time, showed the power of the "paid adjective" and of the publicity stunt. The lesson many people learned, though, was that the truth matters little.

Two moments turned the tide:

- **Carlill v. Carbolic Smoke Ball Co. (1892).** The company promised £100 to anyone who caught the flu after using its product. A customer caught the flu and asked for the money. The court ruled that the ad was a contract offer, not mere puffery.
- **The Ladies' Home Journal (1892)** stopped accepting patent medicine ads, giving up its biggest advertisers, and its editor, Edward Bok, exposed their ingredients. *Collier's* series "The Great American Fraud" (1905) and the novel *The Jungle* led to the Pure Food and Drug Act (1906).

## Application

- A specific promise can legally be a commitment; write only what the client can honor.
- Do not confuse short-term effectiveness with long-term value: techniques that sell today through exaggeration erode trust in the whole category.
- When you present a "new" persuasion technique, ask yourself whether you are reinventing a carnival trick.

## Example

Radithor, radium water sold legally in the 1920s because its label told the truth ("radioactive water"), killed the industrialist Eben Byers after about 1,400 bottles. The case shows why banning false labels was not enough without control over safety.

## Questions

- Which current categories (supplements, "wellness", crypto) resemble the market for miracle cures?
- Where is the line between an enthusiastic promise and deception?
