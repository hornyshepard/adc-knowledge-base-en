# The genealogy of copy styles: news, reason-why, image, USP, Big Idea

> **The idea in one sentence:** the copywriting styles of the 20th century do not replace one another; they swing between the "hard-sell" of reasons and the "soft-sell" of image, and each generation recombines them.

## Explanation

Richards (ch. 10, drawing on Beard, 2005) sketches a sequence worth keeping as a map:

| Period | Style | Champion | Core idea |
|---|---|---|---|
| circa 1880 | "The news of the store" | John E. Powers (Wanamaker's) | facts, ordinary language, no exaggeration and no fine writing |
| circa 1905 | **Reason-why** | John E. Kennedy, Claude Hopkins (Lord & Thomas, Lasker) | advertising as "salesmanship in print": a specific reason to buy |
| 1915 → | **Image / soft-sell** | Theodore MacManus (Cadillac) | emotion and respect for the reader; reason-why treated people as fools |
| 1940s–1960s | **USP** | Rosser Reeves (Ted Bates) | one unique benefit, simple, repeated; once it is found, the copy almost writes itself |
| 1950s–1960s | Syntheses | Ogilvy (hard-sell and soft-sell, no humor), Burnett (soft-sell, with humor), Bernbach (irony, surprise) | Big Idea: strategy plus a reward for the reader |

Humor is the common thread: shunned by Powers and Kennedy, viewed with suspicion by Lord & Thomas (which warned advertisers not to rely on cleverness), gradually accepted in the 1920s, embraced by Burnett and Bernbach. Watkins, writing in 1949, reaches the same conclusion from practice: every school had great successes, and the only thing that never worked was dullness.

This note keeps the historical angle. How to write each style is in the copywriting notes (reason-why, image advertising), and the strategic logic of the USP versus image dispute is in advertising strategy (Reeves against Ogilvy).

Reeves is the extreme case of the focus on effectiveness: a client throws two newly minted half-dollars onto his desk and asks him to prove that his is the better one. The M&M's line about chocolate that melts in your mouth, not in your hand, is the typical answer.

## Application

- When you get a brief, work out which style the problem calls for: a product with a real, demonstrable difference calls for reason-why or a USP; a parity category calls for image.
- Combine on purpose: a clear benefit (the strategy) delivered with a reward (humor, surprise, beauty).
- Read the primary sources: *Scientific Advertising* (Hopkins, 1923), *Reality in Advertising* (Reeves, 1961).

## Example

Cadillac's "The Penalty of Leadership" (MacManus, 1915) mentions no technical feature; it talks about the envy that any leader stirs up. It appeared only once, and according to Watkins, copies were still being requested decades later.

## Questions

- Is today's "brand purpose" a new form of soft-sell, or something different?
- What does a USP lose in a category where all the products are at parity?
