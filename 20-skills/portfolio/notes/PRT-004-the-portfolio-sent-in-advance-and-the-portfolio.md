# The portfolio sent in advance and the portfolio presentation are two different documents

> **The idea in one sentence:** the portfolio read alone has to deliver the narrative in your absence, while its presentation relies on your voice; that is why you need two similar but not identical documents.

## Explanation

Fenn makes a basic distinction: when you **present** your portfolio (in the room or remotely), you provide the narrative. When you **share** it in advance, it is read by someone who has most likely never met you, so the document has to deliver the narrative on its own. For effectiveness, you need two versions.

Their roles in the hiring process also differ: for most companies, reviewing the portfolio is the first filter (no portfolio, no interview). The portfolio you share is a *conversation starter*; the interview and the presentation are the conversation itself. If the first interview is just a conversation, do not be surprised if your portfolio guides it. That is why Fenn recommends sending the portfolio as a *pre-read* in advance of meetings.

For the presentation itself, appendix 2 (The Case Study Canvas) gives a rule of order: **lead your slide deck with the results**, then the business problem, the project challenges and how you approached them, illustrated with images and artifacts.

**Convergence with Colenso.** In *Cut-Through*, the presentation deck and the takeaway deck left with the client likewise "should be completely complimentary but completely different": the first brings the argument to life at a big-picture level, the second goes into the detail. The rule applies to the portfolio in exactly the same way.

Morgan applies the same distinction to the presentation-style resume shared online: the slide deck has to be able to stand by itself, because you are not there to present it. The first slide has to grab attention and be relevant to the employer, each slide carries a single point with a strong visual element, and the end contains a clear request (to contact you).

## Application

- Make a "to read" version (short texts, clear captions, achievement headlines) and a "to present" version (large images, little text, the order result → problem → process).
- Send the "to read" version the day before the interview; bring the "to present" version.

## Example

An art director presenting a campaign in an interview shows the key visual and the result figure on screen, then tells the story of the insight; the document sent in advance contains, instead, the written context, the exact role and the team.

## Questions

- Is it worth the effort of two versions for a junior with three projects?
- How do you adapt the "results first" order for a project without measurable results (a school project)?
