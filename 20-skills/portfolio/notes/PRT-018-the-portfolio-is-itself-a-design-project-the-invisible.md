# The portfolio is itself a design project: the invisible structure lets the work speak, decoration covers it

> **The idea in one sentence:** the way the portfolio is laid out says as much about you as the projects in it; a grid, margins, white space and a clear hierarchy, applied with restraint, enhance the work, while effects, borders and templates dilute it.

## Explanation

Kubišová opens the book with the idea that the portfolio is a manifesto of your identity as a designer and, above all, a design project in its own right. Her layout principles:

- **Think in spreads, not pages**, and start with thumbnail sketches (or wireframes for a website), not directly in software. Look for inspiration outside your field (editorial, magazine layout) so as not to copy styles that are already worn out.
- **The invisible elements** (the rule of thirds, consistent margins, white space, a grid, preferably modular) eliminate random decisions and make the portfolio easy to update; "the best design often is the invisible". The grid is a guide, not a prison: combining modules, full-bleed images and "pause pages" creates rhythm.
- **The visual elements**: proximity (things close together seem related), alignment, scale and proportion, color (neutral backgrounds; the color comes from the content), hierarchy (your power tool), visual balance (well-done asymmetry captures more attention), similarity (consistent project introductions).
- **Typography**: two families at most, not the program's default font, dark body text, no long centered or all-caps text.
- **The theme** should not be forced; the style should follow from the content, otherwise it looks as if you put style before the work.

**Common mistakes:** crowded pages with no white space; images overlapped just to fit more in; margins that are too narrow; unnecessary graphics (rectangles, decorative lines, drop shadows, gradients); borders around images in place of white space; ready-made templates; an over-decorated cover or, conversely, a generic one; a cover copied from someone else; your name as the largest element on the cover (it looks like self-promotion: let the image lead). The cover should be thought of as a billboard: understood at once.

## Application

- For art directors, the portfolio is the first sample of craft: a consistent grid, two fonts and generous space say "I know what I'm doing" before any project.
- Exercise: take a crowded page from your portfolio and remove all the decorative elements; keep only the key image, the title and the caption. Compare.

## Example

A portfolio with a white background, a 12-column grid, a single sans-serif typeface in three weights and large images with short captions puts the campaigns in the foreground; the same material on colored backgrounds, with shadows and frames, looks amateurish.

## Questions

- Your name on the cover: Fenn wants it to be the first thing visible, Kubišová wants it subordinate to the image. How do you reconcile the two pieces of advice?
- When is an "experimental" portfolio that breaks all the rules appropriate (for example, for an avant-garde design studio)?
