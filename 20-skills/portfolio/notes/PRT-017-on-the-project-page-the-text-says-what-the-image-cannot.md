# On the project page, the text says what the image cannot show: specific titles, short descriptions, informative captions

> **The idea in one sentence:** each project needs a gateway page that separates it and sets its tone, and the text on its pages (title, details, description, keywords, captions) must fill the gaps the visuals leave, not describe them.

## Explanation

**The introductory project page** has a double role: it marks clearly where a new project begins and gives a first glimpse of its essence (for example, a full-bleed image on the right-hand page and a block of text on the left). Introductions should be consistent across projects (the principle of similarity), but each with its own identity. They contain: the title; the project number (optional); the grouped details (for a professional project: location, date, client, team; for an academic one: course, semester, instructor, collaborators); a short description; optionally, keywords.

Text rules from the book:

| Element | Rule |
|---|---|
| Title | specific to the project, short, captivating; avoid generic titles that would fit any project |
| Description | about 200 words or fewer; reduce text as much as possible, so that what remains has impact; convey what is not obvious from the image (experiential, procedural aspects); direct language; start from the client's needs or the brief, then the challenges, the concept, the process, and the ending sentence sums up the results or what you learned; make your own role clear in team projects |
| Keywords | specific phrases of 2–4 words ("Library as studio approach"), not generic ones ("school") |
| Captions | not "Sections" or "Kitchen Interior" but "NorthWest Sections" or "Kitchen Interior view from the adjacent living room"; captions are read more often than descriptions |
| Redundancy | no label that restates the obvious ("a description", "photograph of model"), no image that repeats the information of another |

On the project pages themselves, one or two spreads are usually enough: show only the images that best convey the idea and bring new information. The internal rhythm: a strong image at the start, supporting images for depth, an impactful image at the end.

## Application

- Rewrite the captions in your portfolio: each should say *why* the image matters ("The bus shelter version: the message changes with the time of day"), not *what* it is ("OOH poster").
- For each campaign: an idea title, three lines of context, five lines of description and 2–3 specific keywords.

## Example

Weak: "Social campaign: poster, spot, posts." Good: the title "The Quiet Hour", the keywords "Sound as message" and "Ad-free public space", and a description that explains the insight and the public's reaction.

## Questions

- How do you adapt the "200 words or fewer" rule to a strategy case study, where the thinking is the product?
- Is the caption the right place for results (figures), or do they deserve a separate element?
