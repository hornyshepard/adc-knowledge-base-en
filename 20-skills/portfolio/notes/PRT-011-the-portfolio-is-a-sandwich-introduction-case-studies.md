# The portfolio is a sandwich (introduction, case studies, additional information) and some things never go in it

> **The idea in one sentence:** a structural convention has emerged that readers recognize instantly (content about you, the case studies, then information that fills the gaps and builds credibility), and departing from it has a cost.

## Explanation

**The conventional structure.** PDF: cover → about you → case studies → additional information. Website: a home page that links to the case studies, the "about me" content and a contact page. Fenn calls it the **"case study sandwich"**: the opening demonstrates an immediate connection with the employer, the case studies provide the evidence, and the close completes the picture. You can depart from it, but at a risk.

**Useful additions** (ch. 8):
- *Additional projects*: short case studies of around 50 words each, because employers rarely give you enough information to optimize everything.
- *Training and education*: described, not certificates scanned in one per page; they can "unlock" a new area.
- *Awards*: a page if you have several, otherwise in the relevant case study.
- *Speaking engagements and talks*, *volunteer work* (a 2016 Deloitte study: 82% of US hiring managers prefer candidates with volunteer experience): treated like case studies, with results.
- *Activities and interests*: specific and real; they give topics for the interview (interviewers always ask Fenn about his training as a professional Chinese chef).
- *Testimonials*: others can say about you what would sound big-headed coming from you.

**What not to include:** "left-brained / right-brained" and your Myers-Briggs type (no scientific basis, and a researcher or designer should know that); your GPA after 2–3 years of work experience; your Mensa membership; your zodiac sign; the story of "how you got into the field" and "what I love about this work" (the portfolio shows how you will meet the reader's needs, not yours); the resume mixed into the PDF (it is a separate document, or an appendix at the end with the same page orientation).

Kubišová proposes a similar structure for the printed or PDF portfolio: cover page → resume (optional) → design statement (optional) → table of contents → for each project, an introductory page followed by the project pages → extras (creative work from other fields, research) → a thank you page with a clear request (contact, website). On a website, the contact page is the last option in the menu and also appears in the footer of every page.

## Application

- For an ADC student: a cover with your name and the role you want → a 40-word introduction → 3–4 case studies → student awards and competitions, workshops, volunteer work with results → a testimonial from a teacher or mentor → contact.
- Review the portfolio against the "what not to include" list and remove everything on it.

## Example

A student who coordinated a local non-profit's campaign as a volunteer writes: "I organized the fundraising campaign 'X': 3 volunteers, 6 weeks, 12,000 lei raised", not just "volunteer at a non-profit".

## Questions

- Is the convention a support for the reader or a pressure toward uniformity in creative portfolios?
- Which personal interests would be relevant for a copywriter? And for a strategist?
