# A junior without experience needs one good case study and evidence of the qualities sought, not volume

> **The idea in one sentence:** good hiring managers make allowances for a lack of experience and look for enthusiasm, curiosity, quick learning, attention to detail and openness to criticism; one case study, if it is a good one and backed by this evidence, can be enough.

## Explanation

All the hiring managers Fenn interviewed say they make allowances for someone early in their career. What they look for in a junior: enthusiasm for the field, curiosity about people and technology, the ability to learn quickly, attention to detail and a willingness to receive constructive criticism and develop from it (in practice: evidence of teamwork and collaboration). If you have only one case study, supplement it with articles, a blog about your learning journey and other signs of these qualities.

Ways to get material, with the author's warnings:

| Route | How to use it well |
|---|---|
| A course | check whether it produces a real portfolio project, its reputation, the lead instructor's experience, whether it is worth the money |
| A project for a non-profit | donating your skills to a cause you believe in, treated like a paid project; you document the process |
| A hackathon | a product development cycle compressed into a few hours, under pressure; contacts as well |
| A made-up project | solve a real problem, not "decoration" for other designers ("the dribbblisation of design"); take real-world constraints into account; a realistic scope |
| An unsolicited redesign | risky: it shows how much or how little you know about the company's business |
| Design contests (spec work) | **never**: free speculative work, damaging to the industry |

The example of constraints: in 2010, Tyler Thompson's striking redesign of the Delta Air Lines boarding pass would have required replacing over 10,000 printers at airports: gorgeous, but unrealistic. The success story: Francine Lee published an analysis and redesign of the photos experience in Dropbox, with a strong research focus, and joined the company three months later.

The rule of tone: do not sell yourself as an "accomplished strategist" when you only have course projects; it will be noticed. Say what you are studying and what you are looking for.

Morgan offers similar options for those who have nothing ("I've Got Nothing"): mock-ups or sample reports created to prove the skill, personal samples (a website, a budget, a calendar), a volunteer project for a nonprofit, family, a friend or a neighbor, or a presentation of your own accomplishments published online. Unlike Fenn, she also sees design contests as a legitimate source of material (see the debate on skill charts and contests).

## Application

- ADC student: turn your best course project into a complete case study; add a pro bono project for a non-profit and a personal one that solves a real problem, with real constraints (budget, channel, legislation).
- When you do a project "for a real brand" with no brief, research the category as you would for a real client.

## Example

A made-up campaign for a local coffee brand, with a realistic budget, shelf research and a social media plan, is worth more than a "Nike campaign" with no constraints.

## Questions

- Are student advertising competitions (with real briefs from brands) "spec work" or a legitimate exception?
- How does the risk of an unsolicited redesign differ between UX and advertising (for example, an "unofficial" campaign)?
