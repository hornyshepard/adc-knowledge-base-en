# Passion projects bring into the portfolio the work you want to be given

> **The idea in one sentence:** clients hire you for what you have already shown, so if your job does not give you the kind of work you want, do it on your own and put it in the portfolio; passion projects become a magnet for clients.

## Explanation

Allan Peters repeats throughout the book that the portfolio should be filled with the type of work that fuels your passion. If a strong-minded creative director at the agency does not give you the opportunity, do it on the side; look for opportunities to use your gifts to help others while you fine-tune your portfolio.

His examples:
- **Badgehunting Club**: crests for his friends' cities, posted on social media, have "brought in a lot of client work over the years".
- **Loving All Peoples**: a logo made for a small church for a fraction of his normal fee, with thousands of hits on Instagram and Behance and several new clients, because it is simple, clever and easy to share.
- **Camp**: an invented brand, sketched on a paper napkin in Yellowstone; posted on LinkedIn, it drew multiple offers to buy the system or to build the brand as partners.
- **Negative Space Alphabet**: the 36 Days of Type challenge, used as a morning logo exercise and as promotion for the studio.
- **The online shop** (2020): when the pandemic stopped projects, Peters uploaded over 100 products and learned merchandise design.

Two complementary lessons. The first: every client deserves your best, because your next project will be sparked by the latest work you share; Peters considers the identity for Epilogg (an online obituary service) better than his work for Nike and ESPN. The second, from the start of his own career: in his senior year he redesigned every piece of his portfolio, knowing that what mattered to employers was his portfolio and his attitude, not his grades.

**Convergence.** Ian Fenn reaches the same rule from the hiring side: the portfolio attracts offers for the kind of work it shows, so you put in it only what you want to keep doing ([[PRT-002-two-or-three-fantastic-projects-beat-five-projects|PRT-002]]). Peters lives it as a designer; Fenn deduces it from his interviews with hiring managers.

## Application

- Identify the kind of project you want two years from now and do two or three personal projects in that direction.
- Pick public challenges with a set rhythm (36 Days of Type, a weekly series): they build your discipline and your visibility.
- Treat any small or unglamorous brief as an opportunity to make the star piece of your portfolio.

## Example

Wits Realty: in an industry of cheap, predictable logos, the identity that "doesn't look anything like a realty brand" and the yard signs of wood and metal became a distinctive case study, exactly the work Peters wanted to attract.

## Questions

- How do you present a personal project so that it does not look less "real" than one for a client?
- When do personal projects become an escape from paid work?
