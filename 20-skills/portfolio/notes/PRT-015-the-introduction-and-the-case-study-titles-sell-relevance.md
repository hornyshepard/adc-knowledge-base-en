# The introduction and the case study titles sell relevance to the reader, not adjectives

> **The idea in one sentence:** the first words of the portfolio and the project titles are the two opportunities to show that you meet the hiring manager's need; "I craft meaningful experiences" says nothing, "I specialise in helping early-stage startups achieve product and market fit" does.

## Explanation

**Your name first.** Fenn shows an aesthetically pleasing portfolio homepage on which the creator's name is not mentioned anywhere.

**The introduction** (about 40 words) is the first opportunity to communicate your uniqueness, and most people waste it on formulas used by 90% of other designers ("I push perfect pixels", "I design products that people love"). The fictitious "Neil" shows the progression: the plain job role (correct, but indistinct) → "passionate about research" (not a differentiator) → his location → an obvious specialism → grandiose jargon → a mini biography with Lyft and Google (better, but centered on him) → finally: "I'm a product designer. I specialise in helping early-stage startups achieve product and market fit." The last version speaks directly to the reader's need. Juniors should not imply experience: better to say what they are studying and what they are looking for (for example, an internship).

The anti-hyperbole test (Kim Bieler): are you prepared to defend each extraordinary claim with an actual, believable example? If not, take it out.

**Case study titles** are the second opportunity. Weak: titles hidden behind images, only the project name and your responsibility, what the app does. Strong: what you did and what you achieved. Laszlo Bock's **X-Y-Z formula** (former Chief Human Resources Officer at Google): "Accomplished [X] as measured by [Y] by doing [Z]": an active verb, a numerical result with a baseline for comparison, and the method. The benefits that matter to employers: increase profits, sell more, gain market share, raise quality, enhance customer satisfaction, improve efficiency, reduce costs.

**About me** builds on the introduction. The template cited: "I'm a [what you are]. I help [who you help and what you do]. When I'm not [doing what you do for work], you can find me [doing what you do outside work]. Want to work together?" Optionally, an "At a glance" block with key statistics, years of experience, awards.

## Application

- Write three versions of an introduction under 40 words and keep the one that *only* you could sign.
- Rewrite each project title with the X-Y-Z formula; if you have no figures, use the qualitative result or the recognition (an award, a shortlist).

## Example

Weak: "Campaign for water brand X". Strong: "Increased water brand X's Instagram engagement by 30% in three months by turning its labels into a map of local springs."

## Questions

- Does the X-Y-Z formula fit school projects with no real results?
- Where is the line between a memorable introduction and one that is "too creative" for a recruiter?
