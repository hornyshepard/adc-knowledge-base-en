# For your portfolio, ask for structured critique, not general "feedback", and check the tone before you send it

> **The idea in one sentence:** scattered comments in online groups ("It looks interesting and professional") do not help; useful critique starts from who the reader is and what you want them to think, and the owner listens without getting defensive.

## Explanation

**Checks before you show the portfolio to anyone:**
- *Spelling*: every portfolio Fenn read over the past three months contained a typo, the opposite of the "attention to detail" that job ads ask for.
- *Tone*: trash-talking colleagues, the client or the original product is a red flag. A bad stakeholder decision is presented as *a constraint followed by a solution* (the team could not interview users directly, so you suggested a modular design that could be quickly amended after launch).
- *No profanity*, not even on the social media accounts linked to the portfolio.
- *Edit with a highlighter* (Henneke Duistermaat): highlight the essential words and rewrite using only them.
- *Read your writing aloud* (or with a screen reader): if you take a breath in the middle of a sentence, the sentence is too long; write in the first person, as you speak.

**Critique, not feedback.** You handle the portfolio as you would product critique:

| Role | What they do |
|---|---|
| The portfolio owner | decides in advance what they want comments on; prints the portfolio; starts by explaining *who* the reader is and *what they want them to think*; asks whether the portfolio is effective for this reader, and why; listens, takes notes, does not get defensive (you are not your design); says thank you |
| The reviewer | puts themselves in the target reader's shoes, not in their own preferences; asks questions instead of giving verdicts (what did you want the reader to think here?); leaves room for others |
| The facilitator (in a group) | explains the rules, asks for context, limits each person to 1–2 pieces of feedback, uses sticky notes so quiet voices are not drowned out, keeps track of time, reminds people to be kind |

After a rejection, you can politely ask for feedback, but expect not to get it (discomfort, fear of legal trouble, lack of time).

Kubišová ends her book with the same stage: ask experienced people for critique (not praise), observe directly, one on one, how someone navigates your website, re-check the requirements of each firm or school, update the portfolio as you grow, and proofread with fresh eyes: a single mistake can undermine the whole work.

## Application

- In the portfolio seminar, each student starts with one sentence: "The portfolio is for the creative director of agency X, and I want them to think that…"; classmates respond only in relation to that target.
- Rewrite any sentence that sounds like an excuse or a reproach aimed at a client.

## Example

Instead of "the client ruined the campaign with the enlarged logo", a student writes: "The client asked for a bigger logo for visibility on the shelf; I met the request by moving the message to the top of the poster."

## Questions

- Why is group feedback online so contradictory? What is missing from it?
- How do you critique a classmate's work without imposing your own taste?
