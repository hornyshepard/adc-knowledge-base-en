# The candidate treats their career as a business: a personal brand hosted on a platform they own

> **The idea in one sentence:** because hiring has moved online and there are no more "jobs for life", every professional is their own marketing department: they build a consistent brand, with a home base they control.

## Explanation

Hannah Morgan starts from ten changes in hiring: the death of the paper resume, the flood of applications (an average of 74 per job posting, according to CareerXroads), applicant tracking systems (ATS) that become a "black hole", the rise of referrals, the mismatch between the skills employers seek and those candidates communicate, the gig economy, the web as a tool for the masses and the rise of LinkedIn. Her conclusion: the candidate must think **like the owner of a business**.

That means wearing many "hats": business owner, graphic artist, public relations specialist (reputation), marketing manager (branding, visibility), sales representative (consultative selling), copywriter (bios, emails), photographer, website designer, search engine mastermind, business consultant (results, not just tasks) and even hiring manager (seeing your message through the eyes of the person who hires).

Two practical principles:
- **Own your platform.** You do not own your LinkedIn profile; LinkedIn does. Buy the domain with your name and host your portfolio there (home, about, portfolio, projects, blog/news, contact); your other profiles point to it.
- **Consistency.** The same name and the same photo on every platform, the same message in your resume, bio and profiles. The author quotes Reid Hoffman (cofounder of LinkedIn): you must be able to complete the sentence "A company hires me over other professionals because …", by being first, only, faster, better or cheaper.

The examples come from the occupations that have always done this: actors with their headshots, artists with their portfolios of work, programmers who lived on their reputation, CEOs who manage their networks.

## Application

- ADC student: buy the domain with your name by your second year; use the same handle everywhere; write Hoffman's sentence about yourself and check whether your portfolio proves it.
- For creatives, the personal brand also shows in the form of the portfolio: typography, tone, selection, but consistent across all channels.

## Example

A copywriter with their own domain, the same name on LinkedIn, Behance and Instagram and the same positioning sentence everywhere is found and remembered more easily than one with three versions of their name and scattered portfolios.

## Questions

- Where does the personal brand tip into tiresome self-promotion?
- Is "your own domain" still relevant when recruiters search almost exclusively on LinkedIn and Behance?
