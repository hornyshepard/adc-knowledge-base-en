# The portfolio is designed for 30 seconds: a fast track and a slow track

> **The idea in one sentence:** the reader has 30 seconds or less for a first impression, so the essential information must be visible immediately (the fast track), and the details must be there for whoever wants to go deeper (the slow track).

## Explanation

Recruiters and hiring managers have one thing in common: lack of time. A 2012 study by TheLadders found that a resume gets about 6 seconds before the initial "fit/no fit" decision; Fenn's research suggests 30 seconds or less for portfolios. An important difference: external recruiters look for reasons to rule candidates *in*; hiring managers look for reasons to rule them *out*, so as to have a short shortlist.

**Two reading tracks.** The *fast track*: the name, the intro, the headings, the captioned images and the result figures convey the essentials at a glance. The *slow track*: the text explains and fleshes it out. The portfolio has to demonstrate your ability to synthesize and prioritize.

**Length.** A case study that so-called experts online recommend as "best in class" did poorly with Fenn's hiring managers: 6,200 words, about 25 minutes of reading. Rules of thumb: a PDF portfolio of no more than about 15 pages; a case study of no more than 2,000 words.

**Looks matter.** Every portfolio that impressed the hiring managers was beautiful, not just well written: the aesthetic usability effect (Donald Norman). Layout, typography and color must support the message.

Portfolio-specific rules: a grid and white space; captions under every image; artifacts cropped only enough to support your point; no stock photography; no bar charts showing "80%" for a tool like Figma (it means nothing and shows that you do not know information design); no inspirational quotes (Steve Jobs) and never made-up user quotes; writing in the active voice, short sentences, no jargon and no hyperbole.

Morgan cites the same data (TheLadders: about 6 seconds for the first review of a resume) and a CareerBuilder study in which one in five HR managers said they spend less than 30 seconds on an application; from this she builds the case for visual elements that can be taken in at a glance.

## Application

- The 30-second test: give the portfolio to a colleague, take it back after half a minute and ask what they remember about you.
- For an advertising portfolio: each project has, on its first page, the idea in one sentence, the key image and the result.

## Example

A case study with an achievement headline, one large captioned image and three bullets (problem, idea, result) on the first page, followed by two pages of detail, serves both kinds of reader.

## Questions

- Does optimizing for 30 seconds risk favoring spectacular projects over deep ones?
- How much of these rules is specific to UX, and how much holds for an art director's portfolio?
