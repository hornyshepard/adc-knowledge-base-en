# Skill charts and design contests: Morgan recommends them, Fenn and Kubišová reject them

> **The idea in one sentence:** for Morgan (2014), the bars, doughnuts and word clouds that show skill levels are natural elements of an infographic resume, and a contest on 99designs is one way to get one; for Fenn (2024), the former mean nothing, and the latter is speculative work that damages the industry.

## Explanation

| Topic | Morgan (*The Infographic Resume*) | Fenn (*UX Portfolio Design*) |
|---|---|---|
| Skill level | suggests bar graphs, doughnuts, pyramids and word clouds ("The larger the word, the stronger the skill"); even personality traits can be shown as bars | 80% or 3 out of 5 stars for Figma means nothing; such a chart tells the reader you know nothing about information design |
| Career timeline | the timeline is a visual element recommended in many examples | careers are messy; timelines quickly become congested, a simple table or list is often clearer |
| Design contests | 99designs is presented as an option: you describe what you want, designers submit drafts, you choose the winner ($249 to $999) | never: it is speculative work in which most participants are guaranteed to lose out |
| What matters more | the visual packaging attracts attention in a crowded market | the content and the story; the design only supports them |

**Points of convergence.** Both say the visuals must communicate, not decorate: Morgan cites Blount's advice that the story matters as much as the design and recommends graphics for *results* (sales figures, profit turnarounds); Fenn asks that charts show cause and effect or the impact of the work over time, and that the ratio of data to decorative elements stay high.

**How to read the tension.** The difference is partly one of era: Morgan's book captures the infographic wave of 2012–2014, when the novelty of the format was itself an advantage. Fenn writes after a decade of identical infographic templates, when self-rated skill levels have become a cliché. The difference is also one of audience: Morgan writes for candidates in any occupation, Fenn for designers, who are expected to master information design.

**A third voice.** Kubišová (2025) sides with Fenn: do not rate your skills, since self-assessments are subjective and unreliable; list your tools simply and let the work in the portfolio prove your level. She also criticizes overloading the resume with icons and decorative graphics, which give a gimmicky impression.

## Application

- Use charts for measurable results (increases, before/after comparisons), not for self-assessing your skills.
- For an ADC student, the ethical question about contests must be asked explicitly: a real brief from a brand with a prize is different from a platform that asks for dozens of free proposals for a single winner.

## Example

Instead of a "Photoshop 90%" bar, an art director shows a simple chart, "engagement +40% after the page's visual rebrand", with the source of the data.

## Questions

- Are there situations in which a skill chart is useful (for example, foreign languages on a standardized scale such as the CEFR)?
- Is a student advertising competition an acceptable form of speculative work? Why?
