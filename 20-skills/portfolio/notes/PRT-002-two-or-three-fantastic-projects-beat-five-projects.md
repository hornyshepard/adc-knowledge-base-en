# Two or three fantastic projects beat five projects surrounded by mediocrity

> **The idea in one sentence:** curation is itself a skill being assessed; a portfolio that shows everything forces the reader to find their own way through the material and makes you look weaker than you are.

## Explanation

The hiring managers Fenn interviewed consider curation an essential design skill, and they complain that most portfolios are poorly curated, leaving them to "choose their own journey" through a mass of material. The longest portfolio the author has seen ran to 264 pages.

The testimony of Ken Musgrave (then Executive Director of Experience Design at Dell): sometimes he opens the portfolio in Acrobat, erases everything weak and re-evaluates it, and the candidate looks completely different from the one who showed every product they had ever worked on. Designers think they need to show breadth; in reality, a couple of projects show enough experience, and companies would rather have two or three fantastic projects than the same projects surrounded by mediocrity.

Rules that follow:
- **There is no magic number.** The online advice of "3 to 5 case studies" is a rule of thumb; sometimes two case studies are better than five.
- **Do not repeat the same story.** If several projects prove the same need, keep the best one.
- **Show only the work you want to keep doing.** The portfolio attracts offers of the kind of work it shows.
- **Do not force into the portfolio** projects that do not match the role; online, move them to a visibly secondary archive.

Selection is made with the matrix of the employer's needs and your evidence; the project that appears more than once in the matrix deserves a detailed case study, the others only summaries.

Kubišová reaches the same conclusion from the architect's point of view: the portfolio is not a catalog of your work history, and three great images are better than seven good ones; she also offers a concrete selection method, in three groups (YES / NO / MAYBE).

## Application

- The "erasing" exercise: print the portfolio, cut everything that is not among your best work and compare the two versions with a colleague.
- An ADC student with ten course projects chooses 3–4 integrated campaigns, each showing a different key skill, and puts the small exercises in a separate section.

## Example

An art director's portfolio with 3 solid campaigns (a big idea, executions across several channels, a result) is perceived as more senior than one with 15 exercise posters, however beautiful.

## Questions

- When is breadth still useful (for example, at a small studio that wants a generalist)?
- Who should decide what stays: you, a mentor or the data in the job ad?
