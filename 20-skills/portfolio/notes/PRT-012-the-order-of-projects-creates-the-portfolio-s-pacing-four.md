# The order of projects creates the portfolio's pacing: four methods, from chronological to relevancy

> **The idea in one sentence:** the pace at which the projects appear affects how well they are understood and appreciated; the chronological method is easy but often buries your best project at the end, while the three-act and relevancy methods start from what the reader sees and wants.

## Explanation

Zuzana Kubišová describes four basic strategies:

| Method | How it works | Advantage | Risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Chronological (or reverse chronological) | the projects in the order they were made | clear, shows progression; often required for academic applications | predictable; starts with older, weaker work; the best project ends up at the end, when interest fades |
| Three-act ("sandwich") | a compelling project at the beginning, stronger projects interspersed in the middle, the most striking one at the end; the less impressive work acts as transitions | the first impression and the lasting resonance are controlled; a dynamic rhythm | needs at least five quality projects; unsuitable for a brief sample portfolio |
| Category | logical groups (typology, design field, experience, intent: built, unbuilt, competitions) | cohesion; the categories are easy to reorder for each audience | does not necessarily start with the best work; requires diversity |
| Relevancy | first the projects most pertinent to the audience's needs, then those that show your range, and at the end a project that opens a discussion of their interests | engages the reader immediately | assumes you know well what they want; must be redone for each audience |

Related recommendations: if you have the option, avoid chronological order (the portfolio looks less distinct); start with your strongest project (ideally a built one); switch up the length of projects for rhythm; prefer fewer projects with more depth. The same principle applies *within* a project: you open with an impressive image, gradually add the supporting images and close with another strong image.

## Application

- For an advertising portfolio with 5–6 campaigns: the three-act method, with the most awarded or most relevant campaign in first position and a memorable idea at the end.
- For an application to a specialized agency (for example, digital): the relevancy method, with the digital projects first, then the rest.

## Example

A student who puts the first-year project (exercise posters) up front and the third-year integrated campaign only at the end risks losing the reader before the best page; reversing the order changes the overall impression.

## Questions

- When is chronological order honest (for example, when you want to show your development)?
- Does the three-act method contradict Fenn's advice to put the most relevant projects first?
