# The case study is written in four parts (The brief, What I did, Results, Tools and methods) plus highlights

> **The idea in one sentence:** the case study is the written equivalent of an in-person demonstration: it gives the context and the stakes briefly, what you did and why, what result it had, and goes deeper only on the points the employer is looking for.

## Explanation

Without case studies, says Fenn, you do not have a portfolio. They sell indirectly, through specifics rather than claims: they answer questions and objections in advance. Hiring managers do not want a blow-by-blow account of the process but stories; and not a manual they could use to replicate the project, since too much detail invites second-guessing of every decision. The limit: no more than 2,000 words (four pages of A4).

Many frameworks are acceptable (PAR, CAR, SCR, STAR, SOAR, SOARA…), and most lead back to the three-act structure. Fenn's structure:

| Section | Content |
|---|---|
| **The brief** | the client and why they are notable (scale, market position), the problem, what you were asked to do, the stakes ("So what?"); 2–3 sentences. Template: "[Client] is [who they are and why they are notable]. They [the problem]. They asked me to [task you were given]." A negative is framed positively. |
| **What I did** | the problems discovered and how, the challenges, the key milestones, the "aha" moments; your role made clear ("I" vs. "We", with "We" defined); the **why** of every decision; subheadings that say something |
| **Results** | immediate and longer-term benefits, with hard numbers (or percentages, if the numbers are confidential); otherwise, a testimonial; watch annual reports and the press |
| **Tools and methods** | the list of relevant methods and tools, useful to recruiters looking for keywords |
| (Reflection) | optional, more useful for juniors: what worked, what you would do differently, what you learned |

The quote Fenn chose (brand consultant Tate Linden): "Good designers are praised for their technique. Great designers for their impact."

**Highlights and sidebars**: in Fenn's view, the hallmark of an effective portfolio: short deep dives that answer the requirements of the role directly (teamwork, leadership, a fast pace, creativity) or show the work you want to do more of. Ideas: original research, a user's perspective, an obstacle overcome, an "aha" moment, an artifact explained, a testimonial from colleagues.

## Application

- For an advertising campaign: The brief (the brand, the business problem, the task) → What I did (insight, idea, your exact role in the creative team, why this channel) → Results (sales, awareness, earned media, awards) → Tools (research, channels).
- Add a highlight for the skill the job ad asks for, for example "How I convinced the client to drop the TV spot".

## Example

The template as Fenn applies it to his own experience: Tesco is the world's third-largest retailer; a third-party review called its e-commerce UX performance "mediocre"; he was asked to work with its digital teams to develop new design processes, standards and metrics.

## Questions

- How does this structure differ from the case study format for festivals (Challenge–Idea–Execution–Results)?
- How do you write "What I did" honestly for a team project in which your contribution was small?
