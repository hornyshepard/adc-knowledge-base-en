# Portfolio curation starts from analyzing the job ad, with a highlighter

> **The idea in one sentence:** the employer's stated needs are extracted from job ads with a highlighter, confirmed through online detective work and then set against the evidence from your projects; the resulting matrix tells you which case studies to write.

## Explanation

Fenn turns the job search into a small research project:

1. **You choose your targets.** What kind of organization do you want? Fenn compares corporates (job security, rigid processes, slow results), startups (great influence, chaos, risk) and agencies (variety, experience in client service, but decisions made to keep clients happy and pitch ideas made with little or no user research). Nathaniel Koloc's criteria: *Legacy* (the outcomes you want to achieve), *Mastery* (the strengths you want to improve), *Freedom* (salary, benefits, flexibility), *Alignment* (culture, belonging).
2. **You analyze the ads.** You highlight the main tasks, skills and personal qualities required, copy them into a separate document and look for patterns: if an ad mentions collaboration three times, the portfolio has to show it. For several target companies, you put the results in a spreadsheet and see what they have in common.
3. **You check "like a detective".** Job ads can be poor (Fenn was asked in an interview why he had mentioned techniques that the company... did not use, even though they were in the ad). You validate through the website, the blog, LinkedIn (who works there, where they came from), old ads on the Wayback Machine, Glassdoor, public talks, local events, and you request a coffee or an introduction.
4. **You fill in the Portfolio Planner** (appendix 1): column one, what the employer is looking for; column two, the best project or activity that proves it; column three, the artifacts that serve as evidence; column four, the most important requirements (they go in the introduction); column five, the portfolio contenders, in the order of presentation (first those from the same business sector, then by frequency of mention).

Projects that do not appear in the matrix are not a good match for the role. The gaps show what you need to learn. Fenn encourages candidates to apply even if they do not meet 100% of the requirements, but without pretending to skills they lack.

## Application

- For an advertising agency: read the ad, the agency's case studies page, its recent awards and the profiles of its creative directors; choose the projects that answer *that* kind of work.
- Keep a table of 5–10 target agencies and their shared requirements.

## Example

If three target agencies ask for "experience with social-first content", a student moves the social media project to the front and turns the print campaign into a secondary example.

## Questions

- How much should you tailor for each application before it becomes unproductive?
- What do you do when the ad is vague (just a job title)?
