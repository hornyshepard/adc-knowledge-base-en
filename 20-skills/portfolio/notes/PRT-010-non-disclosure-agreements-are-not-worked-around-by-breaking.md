# Non-disclosure agreements are not worked around by breaking them, a password or anonymizing, but negotiated in advance

> **The idea in one sentence:** putting confidential material in your portfolio shows an employer that you might do the same with theirs; the safe solutions are negotiating permission before signing and using what is already public.

## Explanation

Non-Disclosure Agreements (NDAs) protect the client's confidential information. Fenn confirms real cases in which agencies sued designers over work placed in their portfolio.

**What does not work:**
- *Breaking the NDA.* The risk of being sued may be small, but hiring managers react harshly: one received a financial organization's yet-to-launch digital strategy, stamped as commercially sensitive on every page, and immediately rejected the candidate. The message it sends: you cannot be trusted.
- *A password.* It adds a hurdle for the reader and you lose your public portfolio. It is acceptable only if the NDA explicitly permits it; then write "Made available with permission."
- *"Contact me so I can show you in private".* It can suggest that you will divulge sensitive information to anyone who leaves an email address.
- *Anonymizing.* It rarely works: the client can be identified from the resume.

**What works:**
1. Avoid the NDA altogether: ask about the portfolio policy *after* the company has shown interest in hiring you, not at the first interview.
2. Read the contract (ideally with a lawyer): the definition of confidential information, the limitation on use and disclosure, the term of the obligations.
3. Ask for amendments: to mention the client in your resume, to include the work in your portfolio, to present it in private; a time limit (a maximum of three years should be adequate). Later on it is harder, but a request for a single case study has a better chance.
4. Use what is public: press releases, the published job description; summarize the process without commercially sensitive detail.
5. Submit the project, with the client's agreement, for an award: the submission becomes public.
6. A "portfolio of wisdom": short articles on how you would solve the employer's common problems, in place of case studies.

Morgan adds a middle way: if you cannot use the real document, you can re-create a generic version, without specific details or proprietary information, noting explicitly that it is a re-creation. Her argument is the same as Fenn's: when you share confidential material, it is your integrity that is being evaluated.

Kubišová recalls two related rules: ask permission before using projects created in an office, and check your school's policy on the ownership of student work (some institutions grant the instructor a claim to it); in collaborative projects, state your exact contribution and credit everyone: integrity is part of what is evaluated.

## Application

- In agencies, unlaunched campaigns and lost pitches are usually confidential: do not put them in your public portfolio without the agreement of the agency and the client.
- At your first job, ask explicitly what you can use in your portfolio and after how long.

## Example

A junior who worked on a lost pitch can describe their role and way of working, without the client's name and without the actual materials, after checking with the agency what is allowed.

## Questions

- Is it ethical to put in your portfolio ideas the client rejected that were never published?
- Who owns the rights to a pitch idea: the agency, the creative or the client who organized the tender?
