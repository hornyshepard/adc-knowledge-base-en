# The portfolio is a product, and its user is the person who hires, not the author

> **The idea in one sentence:** a good portfolio is designed like any product, starting from the needs of the person who reads it, and it has two goals: to show that you can do the job and to make the reader want to work with you.

## Explanation

Ian Fenn starts from the observation that weak portfolios are collections of high-fidelity mock-ups or deliverables. What is missing are the **stories**: a portfolio is, at bottom, a collection of work stories, and everyone has such stories. The secret is to use the skills of the trade itself: the researcher discovers the needs of hiring managers, the designer designs what meets them.

**You are not the user.** The users are the hiring managers and everyone involved in the hiring process. They do not review portfolios because they want to, but because they have to, between two meetings. Fenn says that 9 out of 10 portfolios received are poorly curated. The portfolio is a sales document: you communicate your value by showing how you delivered it on previous projects.

**Two goals:** (1) to show that you can do exactly the job being recruited for; (2) to make the people reading it feel that they *want* to work with you.

**Who reads it matters.** Fenn cites a 2012 presentation by Lynn Teo (then Chief Experience Officer at McCann Erickson): UX leads look for methods and team spirit; project managers look for process, communication, delivery on time; creative directors look for conceptual thought, problem statements and the effectiveness of the solution. Agencies want to see broad portfolios, attractive documentation, pitch work and stakeholder management.

The working question: "What do I want hiring managers to ask me about?" And leave out what you do not want to be asked about.

**Convergence.** Hannah Morgan sets almost the same goals for any resume, as three questions in the reader's mind: Can you do the job? Will you do the job? Will I like working with you? Fenn's second goal (making them want to work with you) appears in her work as the third question, and her conclusion is that hiring is ultimately about likability and relationship.

## Application

- Before choosing projects, write down who will read the portfolio (a creative director? a head of strategy? a recruiter?) and what each one is looking for.
- For a junior applying to an advertising agency: show how you thought about the problem and the idea, not only the final execution.

## Example

Fenn's friend Dave spent an hour presenting research projects to a prospective client who, at the very end, asked where all the pretty designs were. He had not understood the reader's need, and he had not sent the portfolio in advance.

## Questions

- How does the "user" change when the portfolio is for a competition or a school, not for a job?
- Does adapting completely to the reader risk erasing your creative personality?
