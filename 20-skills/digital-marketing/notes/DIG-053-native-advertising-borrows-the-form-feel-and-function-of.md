# Native advertising borrows the form, feel and function of the medium: it draws its credibility from that resemblance, which is why it has to be clearly labeled as advertising

> **The idea in one sentence:** native advertising is paid advertising that looks and works like the rest of the content; it is effective precisely because it blends in, and that is exactly why the rules require it to be recognizable as advertising.

## Explanation

**The definition** is the same in all the sources. Zahay et al. cite the Native Advertising Institute, and Sachdev the Content Marketing Institute: the ad matches the form, feel and function (Sachdev adds: and quality) of the content of the media on which it appears. Zahay et al. specify that a piece of content is native if it fits the publisher's content style, is created for that publisher's target audience and sits on an external website. On social media, promoted posts (labeled "Sponsored," #ad) are sponsored content. The "native" units of the Meta Audience Network or Google, however, are a monetization option for publishers.

**Where the authors part ways.** Schwarz-Musch et al. (SRC-032) praise content ads precisely because they are often not recognizable as ads at first glance. That is why they are perceived as less intrusive and better accepted. In the XXXL Sports case, an article placed on media websites gives five reasons why now is the right time to buy an e-bike. The brand was deliberately not named as the source, to increase the article's credibility, but ads for XXXL Sports were placed around the article. Zahay et al. and Sachdev require, on the contrary, that native ads be labeled whether or not the reader notices the label. The US authority (the FTC, guidelines from 2015) judges the "net impression" the ad conveys: native ads should not suggest that they are not ads.

Zahay et al. add a nuance that softens the conflict. Consumers are savvy in detecting native advertising and do not necessarily trust it, but they still want the information. Part of the effectiveness comes from ambiguity, and regulation requires the ambiguity to go. The part that comes from usefulness remains.

## Application

- Write native content like a journalist for the publication: the topic has to be interesting even without the logo.
- Earn trust through usefulness, not camouflage, and label it ("supported by…").
- Negotiate placement and labeling with the publisher; check the rules of the market where the campaign runs, not only the US ones.

## Example

Adobe published, in the New York Times' brand space, an article about virtual reality (VR) shopping, clearly shown as sponsored and not too "salesy." The question for the seminar: how much of the XXXL Sports article's effect would be lost with the line "in partnership with XXXL Sports"?

## Questions

- Is native advertising a form of respect for the reader or a form of camouflage?
- What is left for the art director when the design has to follow the publication, not the brand?
