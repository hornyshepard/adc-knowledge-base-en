# A metric is just a number; it becomes a KPI when it is chosen because it shows progress against an objective, and a KPI can combine several metrics

> **The idea in one sentence:** the same figure can be just a measurement or a key performance indicator (KPI); what makes the difference is its link, decided by the manager, to a business objective.

## Explanation

Digital produces more data than anyone can comprehend, and "democratized" data is no longer an advantage: everyone has it. The problem becomes choosing. The three books reach the same distinction by different routes.

**Kaushik** (in Zahay et al.): a metric is a number (visits, impressions); a KPI is a metric that shows how you are doing against your objectives. His template has four levels: business objective → marketing goal → KPI → numerical target. The second lesson: "In aggregate, almost all data is useless." The value appears when you drill down, for example to the source of the registrations.

**Schwarz-Musch et al.** give three tiers: raw metrics (views, time on site, bounce rate), key metrics (the same figures, aligned to an objective) and KPIs (after Parmenter, the indicators of the aspects most critical for success). They add what is missing in Kaushik: a KPI can combine several weighted metrics.

**Sachdev** adds the ladder data → information → insight → intelligence and the distinction between monitoring (you track what is happening) and measuring (you check whether you have reached the objective). Zahay et al. also note that correlation does not imply causation, so you need experiments.

## Application

- Limit the report to 3–5 KPIs explicitly tied to objectives; the other metrics go in the appendix.
- Put each KPI next to a target and next to the segmentation that explains the result (source, segment, channel).
- When the objective is complex (engagement, traffic quality), build a composite score and justify the weights.
- When the client is pleased with "millions of impressions", ask: what objective does this figure move?

## Example

The hypothetical bike parts company in Kaushik's example wanted to build its customer database: the KPI was new registrations per month, with a target of 300 and a result of 332. Drilling down by source showed that paid search had brought in most of them, so the budget moved there. The eBike manufacturer in SRC-032 combines three metrics into a "User Engagement Score": bounce rate (40%), time on page (30%) and pages per session (30%), normalized between 0 and 1. With average values, the score comes out at 41%.

## Questions

- What KPI would a brand strategist choose for an awareness campaign with no direct response?
- When does a composite score become a black box that no one understands anymore?
