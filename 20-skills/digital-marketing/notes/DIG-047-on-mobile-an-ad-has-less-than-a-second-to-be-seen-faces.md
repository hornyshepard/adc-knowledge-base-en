# On mobile, an ad has less than a second to be seen: faces, contrast and instant emotion

> **The idea in one sentence:** the eye-tracking studies cited by Grewal and Levy show that people see and react to mobile advertising within about 400 milliseconds, against about 3 seconds on desktop, so creative work for mobile has to function in under a second.

## Explanation

An analytics box in chapter 2 (Marketing Analytics 2.1, "Making a First Impression in Less Than the First Second") sums up two studies:

- **The speed of attention.** A study sponsored by a trade group of mobile marketers, using eye-tracking tools and electroencephalography (EEG), found that people see and react to mobile advertising within about 400 milliseconds, and to ads on desktop devices in about 3 seconds. A complex picture or graphic that takes several seconds to load is probably never going to be seen: the user clicks away.
- **The phone in the store.** Shoppers who use their phones while grocery shopping spend more time in the store, devote more attention to products on shelves and spend more money than those who shop without them.

The proven methods for evoking rapid responses:

1. **human faces looking straight at the viewer** (they stop people from scrolling);
2. **color and contrasting tones** (they lengthen attention);
3. **signaling complex emotions in a split second**;
4. **viewability**: a static ad should appear at least halfway on the screen for at least 1 second.

The authors' conclusion: the question is no longer whether users prefer a 30-second or a 15-second ad, but how to develop ads that work in less than 1 second. For retailers, the phone is less a distraction than a channel through which they stay in contact with shoppers for their entire shopping trip.

## Application

- Test every mobile visual with a "one-second rule": can you get the brand and the idea at a glance?
- Put the brand and the key message in the first frames of a video. Do not build suspense that nobody will see.
- Use faces, strong contrast and one clear emotion. Leave the details for the landing page.

## Example

Wayfair (Social & Mobile Marketing 2.1, p. 46) posts silly, fun videos of its more unusual furniture (Murphy beds, hammocks) on Facebook to expand its reach, and on Instagram it posts arrangements of furniture that you can buy directly, with one click.

## Questions

- The study was sponsored by a trade group of mobile marketers. How far do you rely on it?
- Are there categories (luxury, social causes) where long attention is worth earning instead of avoiding?
