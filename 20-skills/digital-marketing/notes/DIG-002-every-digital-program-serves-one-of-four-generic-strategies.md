# Every digital program serves one of four generic strategies: acquisition, conversion, retention, value growth

> **The idea in one sentence:** direct marketing strategies follow the customer life cycle, and each stage needs a different type of effort, different tools and different metrics.

## Explanation

The paradigm that holds the whole textbook together is taken from Roberts and Berger (*Direct Marketing Management*):

| Strategy | What happens | Typical tools |
|---|---|---|
| Acquisition | the customer is attracted: trial, first purchase, sample | display, search, social, affiliates |
| Conversion | the prospect becomes a customer or takes the desired action (downloads, signs up, buys for the first time) | landing page, lead nurturing, incentives |
| Retention | repeat purchases: behavioral loyalty, then attitudinal loyalty | email, service, CX |
| Value growth | more purchases, engagement, advocacy | community, cross-sell, up-sell |

The economic engine is **customer lifetime value (CLV)**: the monetary value of the customer over time. In the Bain example for online apparel retail, the profit per customer turns positive only after one year; the average repeat customer spent 67 percent more in months 31 through 36 than in the first six months and, after ten purchases, had referred seven new people. Hence the mantra: it is cheaper to keep a customer than to win a new one (the studies cited put it anywhere from 3 to 30 times).

HubSpot reframes the same stages as *Attract, Acquire, Close, Delight*; chapter 14 repeats them as "mining" the life cycle: acquisition, repeat purchase, risk of churn, win-back.

## Application

- Before any idea, name the strategy: is the campaign about acquisition or retention? The message, the offer and the KPI differ radically.
- Budgets tend to favor acquisition ("It's easier to count the noses of new customers"); argue for retention with CLV.
- For conversion, think in small steps and sequential incentives, not only in "the sale".

## Example

A bank wanted its customers to make deposits at ATMs. It sent them a series of three checks, for $5, $3 and $1, each usable only with an ATM deposit: sequential incentives, decreasing in value as the habit formed. The mistake: it also sent the checks to customers who already made ATM deposits; it did not use its customer database.

## Questions

- When is it wrong to treat the brand only as a succession of conversion stages?
- Byron Sharp argues that growth comes mostly from new and occasional customers. How does that square with the retention mantra?
