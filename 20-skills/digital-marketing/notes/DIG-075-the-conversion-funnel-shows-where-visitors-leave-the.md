# The conversion funnel shows where visitors leave; the marketer's job is to find out why

> **The idea in one sentence:** analyzing the funnel and the paths through the site is not a performance report but a diagnostic tool: each point of abandonment is a hypothesis about the product, the price, the offer or the design.

## Explanation

Analytics platforms let you define a conversion funnel with the steps the marketer chooses: product page → adding to the cart → starting checkout → completion. In the example of the hypothetical store Zedesco (fig. 5.12), of 38,232 visitors who viewed the product page, 10,966 added something to their shopping carts, 4,106 started the checkout process and about 3 percent completed a transaction. The interesting part is not the final percentage but **what goes on in between**: where do those who leave go?

The authors turn each path into a marketing question: if they went to another Zedesco page and bought something there, they found what they were looking for; if they abandoned the cart after seeing the shipping charges, shipping is the problem; if they left for a competitor, there may be a product or pricing problem; if they went back to search, they may not have found the product. Sometimes the answer requires qualitative research, not one more metric.

Chapter 13 adds that **different market segments take different paths**: someone who comes to buy a specific game (Star Wars) behaves differently from someone who just dropped by to see what the site stocks. The marketer has to account for what happened to all 100 visitors and focus on the segments large enough to be worth optimizing. Behavioral segmentation (new vs. returning visitors, new vs. repeat buyers) reveals profitability better than demographic segmentation.

## Application

- Draw your campaign's funnel with at most five steps and note a hypothesis at each exit ("why would they leave here?").
- Turn the hypotheses into tests: shipping costs shown earlier, a shorter form, social proof next to the button.
- Remember that converting more of those who came is cheaper than bringing in new traffic.

## Example

The Zedesco report by segment: returning visitors referred by the affiliate network had the highest average revenue per order, but the network referred the fewest visitors; the free shipping offer produced the most orders in both segments.
