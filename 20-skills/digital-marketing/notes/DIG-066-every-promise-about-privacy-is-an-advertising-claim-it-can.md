# Every promise about privacy is an advertising claim: it can differentiate the brand, but it is judged and sanctioned like any other claim

> **The idea in one sentence:** "your data is safe" is a verifiable statement; if reality does not back it up, it becomes deceptive advertising, and if it does, it can become a competitive advantage.

## Explanation

Sachdev starts from the US rule: under the FTC Act, promises made about privacy and security must not be false or misleading, like any other marketing message. The cases in the book:

- **Snapchat** promised that Snaps would "disappear forever." The FTC alleged that they could be saved, were stored unencrypted on the device, the notifications about screenshots could be evaded, and geolocation information was used even though the privacy policy said it was not. In 2014 the company settled and was required to implement a comprehensive privacy program.
- **Facebook** was charged an unprecedented penalty of $5 billion in 2019 for deceptive privacy claims about consumers' control of their data.
- **Sephora** was the first company fined under the California CCPA ($1.2 million, 2022), for its alleged lack of transparency about selling consumer information.

The other side: **Apple** turned privacy into a differentiator, presenting it as a fundamental human right and putting control in the hands of consumers. The author warns that the strategy has a cost: any failure produces backlash, and the brand has to commit sufficient resources to privacy and resist the temptation of marketing approaches that contradict the promise.

## Application

- Run every statement about data past the client's lawyer, the same as a health claim.
- Word it in verifiable terms ("you choose what we keep," "you can delete it at any time"), not absolute ones ("100% secure").
- Use privacy as a brand idea only if operations can back it up every day.

## Example

The Snapchat–Apple contrast: the same message territory (control over data) brought one a sanction and compliance obligations and gave the other a distinct positioning, because the promise was backed up by the product and its settings.
