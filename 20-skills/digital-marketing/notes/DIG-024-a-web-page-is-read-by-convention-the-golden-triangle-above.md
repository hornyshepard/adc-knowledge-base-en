# A web page is read by convention: the golden triangle, above the fold, the logo at the top left

> **The idea in one sentence:** people skim rather than read; the eye enters through the upper left corner and through the dominant element, and users rely on learned conventions, so a design that breaks them costs them effort.

## Explanation

The Poynter Institute's eye-tracking studies (as the authors read them) show that, in the absence of specific design elements, eyes first land in the **upper left** of the page, especially if a headline is there; people look at only the first few words of headlines and look at about five headlines before clicking. Hence the **priority grid**: upper left = priority 1, the middle = 2, the right and the bottom = 3. Heat maps show a "Golden Triangle" (or "F" section) in the upper left. On tablets, people enter a page through a dominant element, often a photograph, and faces attract the eye.

**Above the fold** comes from newspapers: the top half of the folded paper, visible at the kiosk, had to sell the paper. On screen, the most important content for each segment goes in the area visible without scrolling. The complementary rules: copy in short blocks, headings and subheadings, no more than three fonts (sans serif, *websafe*), colors aligned with the brand, white space, images that communicate better than text, fast loading, accessibility.

**Navigation** has to answer four questions: *Where am I?* (color or underlining on the current section), *How did I get here?* (*breadcrumbs*), *Where can I go?* (intuitive menus, designed with the visitor in mind, not the org chart), *How can I get back (home)?* (a clickable logo at the top left). The convention is so ingrained that getting back to the homepage is about six times harder when the logo is centered.

## Application

- In the layout of a campaign page or an email, put the offer and the CTA in the golden triangle and above the fold.
- Do not "reinvent" navigation for the sake of originality: creativity lies in the content, not in hiding the menu.
- Follow the KISS rule: no important content more than three clicks away.

## Example

The evolution of apple.com in the Wayback Machine (1997–2022): from navigation on the left and boxy sections, to tabs at the top, then to large images across the full width and long scrolling. The design follows the shift from desktop to mobile.

## Questions

- When is it worth a brand site breaking conventions (an event experience), and when is it just a cost for the user?
- Is "above the fold" still valid on mobile, where scrolling is the natural gesture?
