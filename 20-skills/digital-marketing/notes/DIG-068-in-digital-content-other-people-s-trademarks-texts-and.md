# In digital content, other people's trademarks, texts and images are used only with a clear right: a license, the public domain or the narrow “fair use” exception

> **The idea in one sentence:** the fact that an image or a text can be found online does not mean it can be used in a campaign; every element you take needs a documented legal basis.

## Explanation

Sachdev, a lawyer by training, separates two families of rights.

**Trademarks** are words, names, symbols or designs that identify the source of a product. They are protected even when unregistered (™), but more strongly when registered (®). In digital, the risk lies in domain names, social media usernames and app names, which cannot contain someone else's trademark without a right to it. A domain registered "in bad faith" can be claimed through a simplified administrative process (UDRP). The domain must be identical or confusingly similar to the trademark, the person who registered it must have no rights or legitimate interests in it, and it must have been registered and used in bad faith. Using a famous trademark in contexts that "tarnish" it is not permitted, even without confusion.

**Copyright** protects a work automatically once it is fixed in a tangible form, without the © symbol. The *fair use* exception is narrow: criticism, comment, news reporting, teaching, scholarship and research, considered case by case according to the purpose of the use, the nature of the work, the amount taken and the effect on the market. An article copied with its images and a few words changed per page, to add content to a website for SEO, will very unlikely be considered *fair use*. The legal alternatives are the public domain, Creative Commons licenses and commercial stock licenses, limited in use and duration. The CC BY license requires only credit, BY-SA requires the same license for adaptations, and BY-NC-ND forbids adaptations and commercial use. Platforms can remove infringing content, and some, like Snapchat, explicitly forbid it in their advertising policies.

## Application

- Keep a rights register for each campaign: the source of every image or text, the license, the permitted use, the duration.
- A "non-commercial" license does not cover an ad; "free" images are not used without checking the license either.
- Register the brand's domain names and usernames early, including misspelled variants.
- In the influencer contract, write down the disclosure of the relationship, the deliverables and the deadlines.

## Example

The NCAA has owned the trademark "Final Four" since 1988. Someone registered the domain finalfourneworleans.com ahead of the 2022 tournament in New Orleans. The UDRP panel found that all three conditions were met and ordered the domain transferred to the NCAA.

## Questions

- Is a meme made from a film still, posted by a brand, still *fair use* if the purpose is commercial?
- Who in the agency checks the licenses before publication?
